Feature Engineering and Scaling

Feature engineering turns raw columns such as categories, dates, free text and unevenly sized numbers into the numeric inputs a model learns from. Scaling, one of its most common steps, puts numeric features on comparable ranges so that no column dominates only because of its units.

1Overview

Why it matters

A model only sees the numbers you hand it. If a mileage column runs to 150,000 and an age column to 12, any method that measures distances or takes gradient steps treats mileage as overwhelmingly more important, whatever the data say. If a category arrives as text, or a timestamp as one long number, the model either fails outright or learns nothing useful from it. How each column is represented often matters as much as which algorithm is chosen: Facebook's ad-click team reported that, of everything they studied, having the right features mattered most.

The second reason is correctness. Scalers, encoders, vocabularies and feature selectors all learn numbers from data. Fit them on rows that are later used for testing and the test score is inflated. Apply them differently in production and the model receives inputs it was never trained on. Both failures are silent. Anyone who trains, reviews or deploys a model on tabular data needs to know which steps learn from data and how a pipeline keeps them honest.

Key terms

Feature
A numeric input the model receives for each row. It may be a raw column or something computed from one or more raw columns.
Categorical variable
A column whose values are labels from a fixed set, such as fuel type or country, with no arithmetic meaning.
One-hot encoding
Replacing a categorical column with one 0/1 column per category, so that exactly one of them is 1 in each row.
Standardisation (z-score)
Subtracting a column's training mean and dividing by its training standard deviation, giving values centred on 0 with a spread of 1.
Min-max scaling
Rescaling a column so that its training minimum maps to 0 and its training maximum maps to 1.
Log transform
Replacing a positive, right-skewed value by its logarithm so that equal ratios become equal steps.
TF-IDF
A weight for a word in a document that rises with how often the word occurs there and falls with how many documents contain it.
Feature selection
Keeping a subset of the available features and dropping the rest, to cut cost and noise.
Data leakage
Using information while building or evaluating a model that will not be available when it makes real predictions, which inflates its measured accuracy.
Pipeline
One object that chains preprocessing steps and a model, fits them together on training rows and replays the same steps at prediction time.

2Explanation

Introduction

You have already met tables whose columns disagree about what a number is. In a spreadsheet of used cars, one column holds kilometres in the tens of thousands, another holds an age between 1 and 12, a third says diesel, and a fourth is a date. A person reads such a row without effort. A model cannot: it multiplies, adds and compares numbers, and it takes every number at face value.

Feature engineering is the work of turning those raw columns into features, the numeric inputs a model actually receives. Four kinds of work recur:

  • Encoding turns categories such as diesel into numbers without inventing an order that is not there.
  • Scaling puts numeric columns on comparable ranges, so that 150,000 km does not drown out 12 years.
  • Transforming and deriving reshapes a column or builds a new one: the logarithm of a skewed price, a car's age from two dates, word weights from a description.
  • Selecting drops columns that add cost or noise without adding signal.

Think of a recipe collected from cooks in different countries: grams, cups, "a pinch", "gas mark 4". Before anyone can cook from it, every quantity has to be converted to one system, and the conversion table has to be the same one tomorrow. That last point is the part beginners miss. Most of these steps learn something from the data: the mean of a column, the list of categories, the vocabulary of a text field. Those learned values must come from the training rows only, and must then be reused unchanged on test rows and on every live request.

A pipeline is the tool that enforces this. It chains the preprocessing steps and the model into one object, fits them together on the training rows, and replays exactly the same steps at prediction time.

This lesson assumes you can already inspect a table as in exploratory data analysis: you know which columns are skewed, which are categorical and which have gaps. It covers how each kind of step works, which models need which steps, and why the split described in train/test splits and cross-validation has to happen before any of them is fitted.

How it works

Every step has two halves: fit and transform

A preprocessing step is a small model of its own. fit looks at the training rows and stores what it needs: a mean and a standard deviation, a list of categories, a vocabulary. transform applies the stored values to any rows. Steps that store nothing, such as taking a logarithm or reading the hour from a timestamp, are stateless. The rest are stateful, and they are where mistakes happen. scikit-learn's pitfalls guide (opens in a new tab) gives the rule for all of them: never call fit on test rows, and call transform on every row.

Encoding categorical variables

  • Ordinal encoding maps each category to an integer. It is right when the order is real (small, medium, large). On unordered categories a linear or distance-based model reads an order that is not there.
  • One-hot encoding makes one 0/1 column per category. Three settings matter in scikit-learn's OneHotEncoder (opens in a new tab): handle_unknown="ignore" encodes a category never seen in training as all zeros instead of raising an error, min_frequency pools rare categories into one column, and drop="first" removes one redundant column for unregularised linear models.
  • Target encoding replaces a category with a smoothed mean of the target for that category:

Outliers decide between them. For the values 20, 25, 30, 35, 40 and 400, min-max scaling squeezes the first five into 0 to 0.053, while robust scaling spreads them from −1 to 0.6 and leaves the outlier at 29.4.

Scaling matters for models that compare magnitudes across columns: distance-based methods such as k-nearest neighbors, k-means and SVMs, PCA, penalised linear models and anything trained by gradient descent, including neural networks. Tree-based models split one column at a time and are, in scikit-learn's words, almost not affected. In its wine example (opens in a new tab), PCA followed by logistic regression scores 35.19 % test accuracy on raw columns and 96.30 % after standardising.

"Normalisation" is used loosely. Google's course (opens in a new tab) uses it for any rescaling to a similar range; scikit-learn's Normalizer rescales each row to unit length.

Transformations that change a column's shape

  • Logarithm. For right-skewed positive values such as income or counts, ln⁡(1+x)\ln(1 + x)ln(1+x) turns 0, 9, 99 and 999 into 0, 2.30, 4.61 and 6.91: equal ratios become equal steps. PowerTransformer chooses a similar curve from the data, and QuantileTransformer maps values to their ranks.
  • Binning. Cutting a number into intervals and one-hot encoding them lets a linear model learn a different level for each interval.
  • Interactions. A product of two columns, or a combined category such as city and weekday, lets a linear model learn an effect that exists only for the combination.

Features from dates and text

A raw timestamp is close to a row ID. The useful information is in what you derive from it: parts (hour, weekday, month, holiday flag), elapsed time (days since sign-up, a car's age) and aggregates over a window that ends before the timestamp (orders in the previous 7 days).

Hour 23 and hour 0 are neighbours on the clock but 23 apart as numbers. A cyclic encoding places each hour on a circle:

Feature selection

Fewer columns mean faster training, cheaper serving and sometimes better accuracy. The tools in the scikit-learn guide (opens in a new tab) fall into three families:

  • Filters score each column on its own: VarianceThreshold drops near-constant columns, and SelectKBest keeps the best scorers on an F-test or mutual information.
  • Wrappers refit a model many times: recursive feature elimination removes the weakest features step by step, and sequential selection adds or removes one at a time by cross-validated score.
  • Embedded methods select while training: an L1 penalty (see regularization) sets coefficients to zero, and tree importances feed SelectFromModel.

Selection that looks at the target is a fitted step like any other. In scikit-learn's demonstration with 10,000 random features and random labels, selecting on all rows before splitting gives an accuracy of 0.76; selecting inside a pipeline gives 0.5, which is chance.

Keeping preprocessing inside a pipeline

A ColumnTransformer (opens in a new tab) routes each column to its own steps, and a Pipeline chains the result to the model. X is a DataFrame with the named columns:

python
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler

prepare = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", StandardScaler(), ["account_age_days", "open_tickets"]),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="infrequent_if_exist", min_frequency=20), ["plan", "country"]),
    ("text", TfidfVectorizer(), "subject"),      # a text column is named without list brackets
])
model = make_pipeline(prepare, SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression())

scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=5)   # each fold refits every step
model.fit(X_train, y_train)                               # one object to save and call predict on

cross_val_score refits the scaler, encoder, vocabulary and selector on the training part of every fold, so no fold's test rows influence them. The fitted pipeline is also the unit to deploy: it accepts raw rows and applies the stored values. Doing the preprocessing a second time in separate serving code invites training-serving skew, a gap between training and production behaviour that Google's Rules of ML (opens in a new tab) attributes partly to handling data differently in the two places. TensorFlow's Transform component (opens in a new tab) applies the same idea by building the transformations into the model graph.

Step 6: read the result

  • The matrix matches the hand calculation, row for row, and the new listing was encoded with the stored means, standard deviations and category list. Nothing was refitted for it.
  • Same data, same algorithm, different answer. Only the representation changed, and the prediction moved from 17,500 to 9,500.
  • The fitted pipeline is the model. It takes a raw row with dates and a fuel name, so the code that serves predictions cannot scale or encode differently from the code that trained.
  • A decision tree would not have needed step 3. It compares one column with a threshold at a time, so units do not matter to it. It would still need steps 1 and 4.

Edge cases and limitations

A category appears at prediction time that training never saw
A new plan name, country or device shows up in production. scikit-learn's OneHotEncoder raises an error by default. With handle_unknown set to ignore it encodes the row as all zeros for that column, and with infrequent_if_exist it places the row in the pooled infrequent category when one exists. Decide which behaviour you want before deployment, and test it by transforming a row with a made-up category.
Centring a sparse matrix destroys its sparsity
One-hot and bag-of-words matrices are mostly zeros and are stored sparsely. Subtracting a mean turns every zero into a non-zero number, so memory use explodes. scikit-learn's StandardScaler refuses sparse input unless with_mean is False, and its guide recommends MaxAbsScaler, which only divides each column by its largest absolute value and keeps the zeros.
One extreme value flattens a min-max scaled column
Min-max scaling is defined by the two most extreme training values. For 20, 25, 30, 35, 40 and 400 the first five land between 0 and 0.053 and the model can barely tell them apart. A later value above the training maximum also maps outside the 0 to 1 range. Check the column's histogram first, then clip, take a logarithm or use a robust scaler.
Logarithms of zero and of negative numbers
The logarithm of zero is undefined, and counts are often zero. Use the logarithm of one plus the value for counts. The Box-Cox power transform accepts only strictly positive data, while Yeo-Johnson also handles zero and negative values. For a column such as account balance that is skewed on both sides, a quantile transform or clipping is usually the better tool.
Scaling can make a model worse when the small-scale columns are noise
Standardising gives every column the same spread, including columns that carry no signal. The scikit-learn feature-scaling example notes that if the low-scale variables are not predictive, performance can drop after scaling because the noisy features then contribute more. Scaling makes the columns comparable; it does not make them useful. Pair it with regularisation or feature selection.
Window aggregates that quietly include the future
A feature such as a customer's average order value is only valid for a row if it is computed from events before that row's timestamp. Computing it over the whole table gives each training row information from its own future, and often from its own label. Compute aggregates as of each row's time, leave the current row out, and evaluate with a time-ordered split instead of a random one.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The model compares magnitudes across columns, as k-nearest neighbors, k-means, SVMs, PCA, penalised linear models and neural networks do. Scale the numeric columns.
  • The table contains columns that are not numbers, such as categories, timestamps or free text. They must be encoded before any model can use them.
  • A linear model underfits because an effect bends, saturates or exists only for a combination of columns. Add a logarithm, bins or an interaction.
  • The signal lives in something no single raw column holds, such as elapsed time, a ratio or a per-customer average over the previous weeks.
  • There are more columns than the data can support, or some are expensive to compute at prediction time. Use feature selection.

Think twice when

  • The model is a tree ensemble and the question is whether to scale numeric columns. It makes almost no difference; spend the effort on encoding and derived features.
  • The inputs are images, audio or long text fed to a deep network or a pre-trained embedding model. The network learns the representation, and manual features rarely help beyond normalising the inputs.
  • The feature cannot be computed at prediction time with the same definition, or it depends on events that happen after the moment of prediction.
  • A categorical column has tens of thousands of values and only a few rows for each. One-hot encoding then creates more columns than the data can estimate.

A simpler alternative. Start with a gradient-boosted tree model on the numeric columns, integer codes or one-hot columns for the categories, and a handful of date parts. Trees need no scaling and find thresholds and interactions without being told. That baseline stops being enough when the model must be linear or distance-based, when the input is free text or very high-cardinality IDs, or when the signal is in aggregates over time that a row-by-row model cannot compute for itself.

Key takeaways

  • A model treats every number literally, so categories, dates and text must be turned into numeric features, and numeric columns with different units must be made comparable.
  • Scaling is needed by models that use distances, dot products, penalties or gradient descent, and is close to irrelevant for tree-based models.
  • One-hot encoding suits unordered categories with few values, and target encoding, hashing or embeddings suit columns with very many values.
  • Most preprocessing steps learn values such as means, category lists, vocabularies and feature scores, and those must be learned from training rows only.
  • A pipeline fits every step on the training rows and replays the identical steps on test rows and live requests, which prevents both data leakage and training-serving skew.
  • Feature selection that looks at the target is a fitted step too, and running it before the split can make pure noise look predictive.

Uber Michelangelo: time-of-day and windowed-average features for UberEATS delivery estimates

UberDocumented in an engineering article

Michelangelo is Uber's internal machine learning platform. The 2017 Uber Engineering post that introduced it uses the UberEATS estimated-time-of-delivery model as its running example and describes how that model's features are computed, stored and transformed. [13]

The problem

UberEATS has to predict how long a meal will take to prepare and deliver, before the order is placed and again at each stage of the delivery. The useful signals are not columns of the order itself. The post points out, for instance, that the order service cannot be queried directly for a restaurant's average preparation time over a period. Features arriving from data pipelines or client services are also often in the wrong format or have missing values, and whatever is done to them in training has to be done identically when a live request arrives.

Where this topic fits

Feature engineering sits between Uber's data lake and event streams on one side and a gradient-boosted tree regression model on the other, in two layers. Aggregates over time windows are precomputed and kept in a feature store. A small transformation language, attached to each model's configuration, then selects, fills, converts and combines features at both training and prediction time.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Take what the request itself carries, such as the time of day and the delivery location. These need no history.
  2. Precompute slow-moving aggregates in batch. A scheduled job reads order history from the data lake, computes each restaurant's average meal preparation time over the last seven days, and loads the values into a low-latency store.
  3. Compute fast-moving aggregates from a stream. The relevant metrics are published to a message queue, and a streaming job maintains each restaurant's average preparation time over the last hour, writes it to the same store and logs it for future training.
  4. Write the per-model transformations once, as expressions in the model configuration: which features to fetch, how to fill missing values, which timestamp becomes an hour-of-day or day-of-week, which values to normalise.
  5. At training time, run those expressions over historical data to build the training matrix for the tree model.
  6. At prediction time, run the same compiled expressions inside the serving container. They take the request, fetch the stored aggregates, and assemble the final feature vector that is passed to the model.

Why this approach fits

Delivery time depends on things a single order row does not contain: how slow this restaurant usually is, and how busy it is right now. A seven-day average and a one-hour average of the same quantity capture the stable part and the current part separately, and both are cheap to look up once they are precomputed. Timestamp parts let the trees split on lunch and dinner peaks, which they cannot do with a raw timestamp.

Keeping the transformations in the model configuration, and not in two separate code bases, is the platform-scale version of a scikit-learn pipeline: one definition, used for fitting and for serving.

Limitation or tradeoff

Window aggregates need infrastructure: a batch path, a streaming path and a store, each of which can lag or fail. A batch feature may be hours old when it is read, and the post notes that the near-real-time path needs a backfill tool to generate training data and avoid a cold start. Historical training rows must be joined with each aggregate as it stood at the time of the order; joining today's value leaks the future. The post says the shared expressions help guarantee identical features, which is weaker than a proof: anything computed upstream of them can still differ between the offline and online paths.

  • UberEATS data scientists use gradient boosted decision tree regression models on Michelangelo to predict end-to-end delivery time, with features from the request (time of day, delivery location), historical features (average meal prep time for the last seven days) and near-real-time features (average meal prep time for the last one hour). Facebook ad click prediction: boosted trees as a learned binning and crossing step

Facebook (now Meta)Documented in a paper

Facebook's click-prediction system for ads, described by its engineers in the 2014 ADKDD paper "Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook". The paper covers the model, its feature transforms and experiments on a week of the company's ad impression data. [14]

The problem

The final-stage model must predict the probability that a user clicks an ad, at a scale the paper gives as over 750 million daily active users and over 1 million active advertisers. Logistic regression is cheap to serve and can be updated online, but it is linear: it cannot respond non-linearly to a continuous feature, or to a combination of features, unless bins and crosses are engineered for it. The team also needed to know how many features were worth keeping, because the feature count drives memory use and latency.

Where this topic fits

Between the raw real-valued feature vector and the linear classifier. Boosted decision trees act as a learned transformation layer that turns raw features into binary ones, and a feature-importance analysis afterwards shows which raw inputs earn their place.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Assemble raw features of two kinds. Contextual features describe the current situation, for example local time of day, day of week or the device. Historical features summarise past behaviour, for example the cumulative number of clicks on an ad or the ad's click-through rate in the last week.
  2. Train boosted decision trees on these features in batch.
  3. Treat each tree as one categorical feature. Pass an impression down the tree, note which leaf it reaches, and one-hot encode that leaf index. A path from root to leaf is a rule over several features, so each leaf acts as a learned bin or cross.
  4. Concatenate the one-hot vectors of all trees into one binary vector and feed it to logistic regression, which learns a weight per leaf.
  5. Score each raw feature's importance by adding up the loss reduction from every split that uses it, across all trees.
  6. Retrain with only the top 10, 20, 50, 100 or 200 features and compare accuracy, to see what dropping the rest would cost.

Why this approach fits

Binning and crossing are the two transformations that let a linear model behave non-linearly, and choosing bin edges and useful crosses by hand does not scale to hundreds of features. A tree picks its thresholds and combinations to reduce the loss, so the trees perform supervised binning and crossing in one step and return a compact binary vector. The linear model on top stays cheap to serve and can be refreshed often. The importance ranking then gives an order in which to cut features when memory and latency are tight.

Limitation or tradeoff

The transformation is itself a trained model, so it goes stale; the paper studies how accuracy decays as the gap between training data and serving time grows. Two stages mean two things to train, version and monitor. Importance scores based on loss reduction depend on the data and the model and split credit arbitrarily between correlated features, so a low score does not prove a feature is useless. Historical features dominate the ranking, yet they do not exist for a new ad or a new user, which is why the paper keeps contextual features for the cold-start case. The actual features are not disclosed.

What the sources document

  • The paper names two simple transforms for the inputs of a linear classifier: binning a continuous feature and treating the bin index as a categorical feature, and building tuple features from the Cartesian product of categorical features. [14]
  • Each boosted tree is treated as a categorical feature whose value is the index of the leaf an instance falls in, with 1-of-K coding; for two trees with 3 and 2 leaves, an instance in leaf 2 of the first and leaf 1 of the second gives the binary vector [0, 1, 0, 1, 0]. [14]
  • Table 1 reports Normalized Entropy relative to the trees-only model as 96.58 % for logistic regression with tree features and 99.43 % for logistic regression alone, and the text says the tree feature transforms decrease Normalized Entropy by more than 3.4 % relative to the model without them.   • The top 10 features are responsible for about half of the total boosting feature importance, while the last 300 features contribute less than 1 %. [14]
  • The top 10 features by importance are all historical and only 2 of the top 20 are contextual, and the paper adds that contextual features are very important for handling the cold-start problem for new users and ads. [14]

Google Play app recommendations: vocabularies, quantile scaling, crosses and embeddings

GoogleDocumented in a paper

The app recommendation ranking model of Google Play, described in the 2016 paper "Wide & Deep Learning for Recommender Systems" by Cheng and colleagues at Google. Section 4 of the paper describes the production pipeline, including how the model's features are generated. [15]

The problem

For each visit to the store, the ranker scores candidate apps by the probability that the user installs them. Its inputs are mostly sparse categorical strings, such as the apps a user has installed, the app being shown, the device class and the language, plus a few continuous values such as age and counts of installs and sessions. Neither kind can be fed to a linear model or a neural network as it stands, and the paper wants the model both to memorise specific combinations and to generalise to combinations it has not seen.

Where this topic fits

In the data generation stage, before training. That stage builds the vocabularies and the quantile boundaries, and the training input layer then uses them to produce the sparse and dense features for the two halves of the model.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Build a vocabulary for each categorical feature: a table from string value to integer ID, keeping only values that occurred more than a minimum number of times.
  2. Normalise each continuous feature by rank. Split its distribution into n_q quantiles and replace a value in the i-th quantile by (i − 1)/(n_q − 1), a number between 0 and 1.
  3. For the wide, linear half, create cross-product features: a binary feature that is 1 only when two conditions hold together, here an app the user has installed and the app being shown.
  4. For the deep half, look up a 32-dimensional embedding vector for each categorical ID and concatenate the vectors with the normalised continuous features.
  5. Train both halves jointly, so the crosses memorise specific pairs while the embeddings generalise to pairs that never occurred together.
  6. Treat the vocabularies and quantile boundaries as learned values. A serving system has to apply the same ones, or an ID or a normalised number would mean something different from what the model was trained on.

Why this approach fits

Counts such as installs and sessions are heavy-tailed. A rank-based mapping spreads users evenly over 0 to 1 and gives an extreme value the same input as any other value in the top quantile, so one outlier cannot stretch the scale the way it does with min-max scaling. A minimum-frequency vocabulary bounds the ID space and keeps one-off strings out.

Crosses and embeddings are complementary encodings of the same categorical inputs. A cross gets its own weight and can memorise that two specific apps go together. An embedding places similar apps near each other, so the model can score pairs it has never seen.

Limitation or tradeoff

The paper itself notes that cross-product features cannot generalise to feature pairs that never appeared in the training data, which is the reason for the deep half. Quantile normalisation throws away magnitude inside a quantile: two users in the top bucket look identical however different their counts are. Values below the vocabulary threshold lose their identity. All of this is learned state, so vocabularies and boundaries go out of date as the catalogue and the user base change and have to be regenerated with each retraining.

What the sources document

  • The system was productionized and evaluated on Google Play, which the paper describes as a mobile app store with over one billion active users and over one million apps. [15]
  • Vocabularies, tables mapping categorical feature strings to integer IDs, are generated in the data generation stage, and the ID space is computed for all string features that occurred more than a minimum number of times. [15]
  • Continuous real-valued features are normalised to [0, 1] by mapping a value x to its cumulative distribution function P(X ≤ x), divided into n_q quantiles; the normalised value is (i − 1)/(n_q − 1) for values in the i-th quantile, and quantile boundaries are computed during data generation. [15]
  • The wide component consists of the cross-product transformation of user installed apps and impression apps; for the deep part a 32-dimensional embedding is learned for each categorical feature and concatenated with the dense features into a vector of approximately 1,200 dimensions. [15]
  • In a three-week online A/B test the Wide & Deep model improved the app acquisition rate on the store's main landing page by 3.9 % relative to the wide-only control and by 1 % over a deep-only model; the paper also states that cross-product transformations do not generalise to query-item feature pairs absent from the training data. [15]

Airbnb search ranking: normalising features when a neural network replaced boosted trees

AirbnbDocumented in a paper

Airbnb's search ranking model, as described by its engineers in the 2018 paper "Applying Deep Learning To Airbnb Search" (Haldar and colleagues). Section 4 of the paper is devoted to feature engineering during the move from gradient boosted decision trees to a neural network. [16]

The problem

Search ranking had been driven by a gradient boosted decision tree model whose gains had plateaued. When the team first trained a neural network on the same features, the loss saturated midway through training and further steps had no effect. The tree model had hidden the fact that the features came in very different ranges and shapes, because trees depend only on the order of values.

Where this topic fits

Between the logged listing, query and user features and the input layer of the network. The paper calls this a different flavour of feature engineering: less about deriving new quantities by hand, more about making each input comply with properties that let the network do the math itself.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Plot the distribution of every feature.
  2. If it looks roughly bell-shaped, standardise it: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation.
  3. If it has a long right tail, as counts and prices do, apply log((1 + value)/(1 + median)), which maps the median to 0 and compresses the tail.
  4. Check that the result is smooth and lies mostly between −1 and 1. Treat spikes as suspects: the paper's examples are prices logged in the wrong currency and monthly prices logged as daily ones.
  5. Where a standard transform is not enough, redesign the feature. Latitude and longitude become offsets from the centre of the displayed map, followed by a logarithm. Occupancy is normalised by the average length of stay.
  6. For location preference, build a high-cardinality categorical feature by hashing the query city together with the map cell of the listing, and let the network learn an embedding for each value.

Why this approach fits

A network multiplies inputs by weights and pushes gradients back through them, so a feature in the thousands beside one between 0 and 1 produces large, unbalanced updates. Standardising handles symmetric features. Count-like features are different: their mean and standard deviation are dominated by a few giants, so a z-score still leaves most values crammed together. The log-ratio to the median fixes both the range and the shape.

Inspecting distributions also works as a cheap data-quality test, because a logging bug rarely produces values that follow the same smooth curve as the real ones.

Limitation or tradeoff

The method relies on a person looking at each feature and choosing a transform. That is manageable for the 195 input features the paper reports and laborious for thousands. Means, standard deviations and medians are learned from past data and drift with the marketplace. The map-offset transform is lossy: the paper points out that many coordinates map to the same offsets, so the model learns distance effects but loses knowledge of specific places, which the hashed city-and-cell feature has to restore. Hashing in turn lets unrelated places collide.

What the sources document

  • In the first attempt at training a neural network the team fed it all the features used to train the GBDT model; the loss saturated in the middle of training, and they traced the issue to features that were not normalised properly. [16]
  • The paper states that for decision trees the exact numeric values of features hardly matter as long as their relative ordering is meaningful, whereas neural networks are sensitive to them, and that out-of-range values can cause large gradients that permanently shut off ReLU activations. [16]
  • Features are restricted to a small range, with the bulk of the distribution in the {-1, 1} interval and the median mapped to 0, using (feature_val − μ)/σ when the distribution resembles a normal one and log((1 + feature_val)/(1 + median)) when it looks closer to a power law. [16]
  • Distribution plots exposed bugs as spikes: currency errors in the prices logged for certain geographies, and the monthly price being logged in place of the daily price for periods greater than 28 days. [16]
  • Raw latitude and longitude were replaced by the offset from the centre of the map displayed to the user, followed by a log, and a categorical feature was built by hashing the query city with the listing's level 12 S2 cell to an integer that is then mapped to an embedding. [16]

Feature hashing for personalised spam filtering at Yahoo! Research, with Vowpal Wabbit

Yahoo! Research and the Vowpal Wabbit projectDocumented in a paper

A 2009 study from Yahoo! Research (Weinberger and colleagues) of feature hashing for personalised email spam filtering, run on a proprietary email dataset with the open-source learner Vowpal Wabbit. Vowpal Wabbit's own wiki documents hashing as the way it indexes features. [17][18]

The problem

A spam filter over email text has a vocabulary of millions of tokens, and the authors want every user to have personal weights on top of a shared classifier. For the dataset in the paper, giving each user a weight for each token would mean 16 trillion possible features. The usual way to save memory is to drop rare tokens, and the paper notes that spammers exploit exactly that by deliberately misspelling words.

Where this topic fits

At the step that turns text into feature columns. A conventional bag of words first learns a vocabulary that maps each token to a column. Hashing replaces that learned table with a fixed function from token string to column index, applied identically in training and at prediction.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Tokenise the email into words.
  2. For personalisation, emit each token twice: once as it is, and once joined to the recipient's user id.
  3. Apply a hash function to each token string and reduce it to an index in a table of fixed size, so every possible string lands in some slot.
  4. Use a second hash of the token to choose a sign, +1 or −1, and add that signed value into the slot. The sign removes the bias that collisions would otherwise introduce.
  5. Train a linear classifier directly on the hashed vector with stochastic gradient descent. The model is one weight per slot, whatever the size of the vocabulary.
  6. At prediction time, hash the incoming email in the same way. A word never seen before still maps to a slot, and no vocabulary has to be stored or shipped.

Why this approach fits

The size of the model is fixed before any data is seen, so memory does not grow as new words, misspellings and users arrive. There is no fitted vocabulary, hence nothing to leak from test data and nothing to keep in sync between training and serving. Rare tokens are not discarded, which matters precisely because spam authors invent them. And because a token joined to a user id is just another string, personalisation becomes a feature-engineering step with no change to the learning algorithm.

Limitation or tradeoff

Different tokens can share a slot and therefore a weight. The paper finds the effect negligible once the table is large enough for its data, but the right size depends on the problem; Vowpal Wabbit defaults to 2^18 slots and its wiki tells users to raise the bit count when more space is needed to avoid collisions. The mapping cannot be inverted, so a weight cannot be traced back to a word without extra bookkeeping, which makes the model harder to debug and explain. Weighting schemes that need corpus statistics, such as idf, bring state back in. The reported results are relative figures on one proprietary dataset, and the paper does not describe a production deployment.

What the sources document

  • The experiments use a proprietary, anonymised email spam-classification dataset of 3.2 million emails from 433,167 users, with 40 million unique words after tokenisation, and the Vowpal Wabbit implementation of stochastic gradient descent. [17]
  • The paper says the naive remedy of eliminating infrequent tokens is targeted by spammers who maliciously misspell words to create infrequent but spam-typical tokens, and that hashing all words into a finite-sized feature vector gives such tokens a chance to contribute. [17]
  • A personalised hash function is obtained by concatenating a unique user id to each word and hashing the new tokens with the same global hash function; without hashing, 400K users and 40M tokens would give 16 trillion possible unique personalised features. [17]
  • The hashed global classifier converges by 2^22 hash keys, which the authors read as collisions no longer affecting the classification error, and personalisation yields a 30 % reduction in spam relative to the baseline once the hash table is indexed by 22 bits. [17]
  • Vowpal Wabbit hashes feature names with a variant of 32-bit murmurhash v3 and keeps the k lowest-order bits, with k = 18 by default, so the table holds 2^18 or 262,144 entries; the -b parameter sets a different size. [18]

4Common mistakes

  1. Fitting a scaler, encoder, vocabulary or feature selector on the whole dataset and splitting afterwards.

    Why it causes problems
    Values learned from the test rows shape the features the model trains on, so the test score is optimistic. For steps that use the target the effect is large: in scikit-learn's demonstration, selecting features from 10,000 random columns before splitting gives an accuracy of 0.76 on labels that are pure noise.
    How to recognise it
    A call to fit or fit_transform appears above the train/test split, or cross-validation is run on a matrix that was already transformed. The score drops when the same steps are moved into a pipeline.
    How to fix it
    Split first. Put every fitted step in a Pipeline and pass the pipeline itself to cross_val_score or a parameter search, so that each fold refits the steps on its own training part.

  2. Transforming the training data one way and the test or production data another way, or not at all.

    Why it causes problems
    The model receives inputs on a scale, or in a column layout, that it never saw. scikit-learn's pitfalls page shows a regression whose mean squared error is 62.80 when the test set is left unscaled and 0.90 when it is transformed like the training set. Building dummy columns separately for each dataset has the same effect when the two sets contain different categories.
    How to recognise it
    Offline scores are good and live results are poor from the first day. The serving code contains its own copy of the preprocessing, or the feature matrix has a different number of columns at prediction time than at training time.
    How to fix it
    Save the fitted pipeline as one object and send raw rows to it everywhere. If serving must be written in another system, log the features it computes and compare them with the training features for the same rows.

  3. Encoding an unordered category as the integers 0, 1, 2 for a linear or distance-based model.

    Why it causes problems
    The model reads the codes as quantities. A linear model fits one slope through an arbitrary order, and a nearest-neighbour model treats categories with adjacent codes as similar. The result depends on how the categories happened to be numbered.
    How to recognise it
    A column of small integers produced by a label or ordinal encoder feeds a model that is not tree-based, and the validation score changes when the categories are renumbered.
    How to fix it
    One-hot encode unordered categories for these models. Keep integer codes for categories with a real order, and for tree models, which can split the codes repeatedly.

  4. Building a target-encoded or aggregated feature from rows that include the row's own label or later events.

    Why it causes problems
    The feature carries the answer. A category that occurs once is encoded with exactly its own target value, and a customer average computed over the whole table includes purchases made after the moment of prediction. Validation looks excellent and production does not.
    How to recognise it
    One engineered feature has an implausibly high importance, accuracy collapses on data from a later period, or the code computes a grouped mean of the target on the full table.
    How to fix it
    Use cross-fitted target encoding, such as TargetEncoder.fit_transform on the training rows. Compute window aggregates as of each row's timestamp with the current row left out, and validate on a later time period.

  5. Min-max scaling or standardising a heavily skewed column without looking at its distribution.

    Why it causes problems
    A few extreme values set the scale and most rows are compressed into a sliver of the range. In this lesson's example five of six values end up between 0 and 0.053. For a neural network that can stall training, as Airbnb's team found when they fed unnormalised features to their first network.
    How to recognise it
    After scaling, the column's histogram is a spike near one end and the median is far from the middle of the range.
    How to fix it
    Apply a logarithm or a power transform first, clip extreme values, or use a robust or quantile-based scaler. Then plot the column again.

  6. Feeding hour, weekday or month to a linear model as a plain number, or feeding it a raw timestamp.

    Why it causes problems
    A linear model can only make the prediction rise or fall steadily with the number, so it cannot represent a morning peak and an evening peak, and it sees 23:00 and 00:00 as opposite extremes. A raw timestamp mostly encodes row order and moves outside the training range as soon as the model is used.
    How to recognise it
    Residuals plotted against hour of day show a repeating wave, or the model gets worse on later data although nothing else changed.
    How to fix it
    Derive parts from the timestamp and encode cyclic ones with one-hot columns, sine and cosine, or periodic splines, comparing the options with cross-validation. Tree models can use the integer parts directly.

prepare = ColumnTransformer([
    ("age", make_pipeline(FunctionTransformer(np.log1p), StandardScaler()), ["account_age_days"]),
    ("plan", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), ["plan"]),
    ("hour", FunctionTransformer(hour_on_circle), ["opened_at"]),
    ("text", TfidfVectorizer(), "subject"),
], sparse_threshold=0)
model = make_pipeline(prepare, LogisticRegression()).fit(X, y)

Z = model[:-1].transform(new)
scaler = prepare.named_transformers_["age"][-1]
print("columns:", Z.shape[1], " words:", len(prepare.named_transformers_["text"].vocabulary_))
print("log-age mean and std:", scaler.mean_.round(3), scaler.scale_.round(3))
print(Z[:, :6].round(3))
print("non-zero text columns:", (Z[:, 6:] != 0).sum(axis=1))
print("P(urgent):", model.predict_proba(new)[:, 1].round(2))
print("fold accuracy:", cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=StratifiedKFold(n_splits=3)).round(2))

Exercise 2

Reproduce and repair a cross-validation score inflated by feature selection

AnalysisIntermediateAbout 30 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

You are reviewing a colleague's notebook. It reports 83 % cross-validated accuracy on a dataset with 100 rows and 2,000 columns. You suspect the preprocessing. Reproduce the situation with data that contains no signal at all, so that any accuracy clearly above 50 % must be an artefact. Use NumPy and scikit-learn, which are free.

  1. Create X as 100 rows by 2,000 columns of standard normal numbers from np.random.default_rng(0), and y as 50 zeros followed by 50 ones.
  2. The colleague's way: fit SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20) on all 100 rows, keep the 20 selected columns, then cross-validate LogisticRegression() on them.
  3. The correct way: put the same selector and model into a pipeline and cross-validate the pipeline on all 2,000 columns.
  4. Repeat both ways with StandardScaler in place of the selector, using only the first 20 columns of X.
  5. Explain the four mean accuracies, and say which kinds of preprocessing step are most dangerous to fit before splitting.

Use StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0) for every cross-validation.

Requirements

  • Use the same cross-validation splitter object for all four runs so that the folds are identical.
  • In the correct version the selector or scaler must be a step of the pipeline passed to cross_val_score.
  • Report the five fold accuracies and their mean for both selection runs, and the mean for both scaler runs.
  • State what accuracy a perfect evaluation should report on this data, and why.

Expected behaviour

With selection fitted on all rows, the fold accuracies are 0.95, 0.75, 0.85, 0.85 and 0.75, a mean of 0.83, on labels that are unrelated to the features. With selection inside the pipeline they are 0.45, 0.35, 0.40, 0.60 and 0.40, a mean of 0.44, which is consistent with the 0.50 that pure chance gives. With the scaler, both ways give 0.41. Other random seeds give roughly 0.8 to 0.9 for the leaky selection and 0.45 to 0.55 for the correct one.

Hint 1

SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit_transform(X, y) returns the 20 selected columns directly.

Hint 2

make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression()) can be passed to cross_val_score like any estimator.

Hint 3

With 100 rows, an accuracy near 0.5 has a standard error of about 0.05, so 0.44 and 0.41 are not evidence of anything.

Show solution
python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(100, 2000))      # 2,000 columns of pure noise
y = np.repeat([0, 1], 50)             # labels that have nothing to do with X
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)

# Colleague's way: the selector sees all 100 rows, including every fold's test rows.
X_selected = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit_transform(X, y)
outside = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(), X_selected, y, cv=cv)

# Correct way: the selector is a pipeline step and is refitted on each training fold.
inside = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression()), X, y, cv=cv)

print("selection outside CV:", outside.round(2), "mean", outside.mean().round(2))
print("selection inside CV: ", inside.round(2), "mean", inside.mean().round(2))

# The same comparison with a step that never looks at y.
X20 = X[:, :20]
scaled_outside = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(), StandardScaler().fit_transform(X20), y, cv=cv)
scaled_inside = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()), X20, y, cv=cv)
print("scaler outside CV:", scaled_outside.mean().round(2), " scaler inside CV:", scaled_inside.mean().round(2))

Output with scikit-learn 1.9.1 and NumPy 2.5.3:

text
selection outside CV: [0.95 0.75 0.85 0.85 0.75] mean 0.83
selection inside CV:  [0.45 0.35 0.4  0.6  0.4 ] mean 0.44
scaler outside CV: 0.41  scaler inside CV: 0.41

Why 0.83 appears. Among 2,000 noise columns, some line up with y over these 100 rows purely by chance. The selector, fitted on all rows, picks exactly those columns. Each fold's test rows helped choose them, so the chance pattern holds in the test rows too, and the classifier is rewarded for finding it. Nothing real was learned: on new data the accuracy would be 0.5.

Why 0.44 is the honest answer. Inside the pipeline, the selector sees only the 80 training rows of each fold. The columns it picks fit those 80 rows by chance and say nothing about the other 20. The true accuracy on this data is 0.5, and with 100 rows the standard error of an estimate near 0.5 is 0.5×0.5/100=0.05\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5 / 100} = 0.050.5×0.5/100​=0.05, so 0.44 is within ordinary noise.

Why the scaler makes no visible difference here. A scaler never looks at y, and the mean and standard deviation of 100 rows barely move when 20 of them are left out. The leak exists but is tiny. It grows with small samples and extreme values, and the fix costs nothing, so the scaler belongs in the pipeline as well.

The rule. The most dangerous steps to fit before splitting are those that use the target: feature selection, target encoding, and any tuning done by looking at test scores. Steps that use only the features, such as scaling, imputation and PCA, leak less but follow the same rule.
