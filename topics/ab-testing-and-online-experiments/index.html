A/B Testing and Online Experiments

An A/B test, also called an online controlled experiment, randomly assigns live users to the current version of a product (the control) or a changed version (the treatment) over the same period and compares a metric chosen in advance. Because chance alone decides who gets which version, a difference larger than chance would produce can be attributed to the change.

1Overview

Why it matters

A model that scores better on a held-out set has shown that it predicts logged data better. Whether it makes the product better, with more purchases, fewer complaints and the same speed, is a separate question, and the two answers often disagree. Booking.com compared 23 pairs of models and found no correlation between the offline gain and the gain measured in a randomised trial. An online experiment is how a team finds that out before the whole user base does.

Most of the damage comes from experiments that are run badly, not from experiments that are skipped. A test with too few users reports "no significant difference" and is read as "no difference". A test is stopped on the first day the dashboard turns green. A broken split silently drops the users who were affected most. A win on clicks costs retention. Treated users change what control users experience in a marketplace or a social network. Anyone who ships model changes, reads an experiment dashboard or signs off a launch needs to recognise these failures.

  • Describe how interleaving and multi-armed bandits differ from an A/B test and what each gives up.

Key terms

Control and treatment
The two groups in an experiment. Control gets the current version; treatment gets the change being tested. Each version is also called a variant or an arm.
Randomization unit
The thing that is randomly assigned to a variant, most often a user, but sometimes a session, a request, a cluster of users or a time window.
Primary metric (OEC)
The one metric, chosen before the test, that decides whether the change worked. It is also called the overall evaluation criterion.
Guardrail metric
A metric the change is not meant to improve but must not damage, such as latency, error rate or support contacts.
Minimum detectable effect (MDE)
The smallest true difference between the variants that the experiment is designed to detect with the chosen power.
Statistical power
The probability that the test reports a significant result when a true effect of the planned size exists. A common target is 80%.
Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)
A split of users between variants that differs from the configured split by more than chance allows, which signals a bug in assignment, logging or filtering.
Novelty effect
A short-lived change in behaviour caused by something being new, which fades as users get used to it.
Interference
A situation in which the variant given to one unit changes the outcome of another unit, for example through messages between friends or shared supply in a marketplace.
Multi-armed bandit
An experiment design that keeps shifting traffic toward the variants that are performing better while it is still learning which one is best.

2Explanation

Introduction

You already know how to compare two models offline: hold out data, score both, keep the one with the better number, as in classification metrics. You also know how to put a model behind an endpoint, from model deployment and serving. This lesson is about the step between "it scores better" and "it is better for users".

Take a bookshop's "recommended for you" row. A new ranking model raises an offline ranking score from 0.412 to 0.431 (invented numbers). That score was computed on clicks logged while the old model chose what to show. It cannot say how people react to books the old model never showed them, whether the new model's extra 40 ms makes them leave, or whether more clicks become more purchases. An offline metric is computed from stored data. An online metric is computed from what live users do while the model is serving them, and it is the one the business cares about.

Comparing last week under the old model with this week under the new one does not settle it either. A holiday, a marketing email or a price change moves the numbers too. The fix is the one clinical trials use. Flip a fair coin for every user. Heads get the old model, the control. Tails get the new one, the treatment. Both groups live through the same week, the same promotions and the same outages, and with enough users the coin makes the groups alike in everything else, measured or not. What remains different is the model.

That is an A/B test. The coin is the easy part. The rest of this lesson covers the decisions around it: which metric decides, what exactly is randomized, how many users are needed before the answer means anything, which metrics must not get worse, and what to do when the usual assumptions fail because users tire of a novelty or because one user's treatment leaks into another user's experience. It ends with two relatives of the A/B test, interleaving and bandits, at overview level. The hypothesis tests and confidence intervals come from statistics for ML.

How it works

Offline metrics filter, online metrics decide

Offline evaluation is fast, cheap and safe, so it stays in the workflow as a filter: a model that is broken or worse offline never reaches users. It cannot be the decision, for four reasons.

  • The training target is a proxy. A model trained on clicks can learn to attract clicks that do not lead to purchases.
  • Logged data is biased. The log only holds reactions to what the previous model chose to show.
  • Serving has side effects. A slower model changes behaviour by being slow.
  • Returns saturate. Once a model is good, a further offline gain may produce no measurable value.

In Booking.com's review of about 150 models (opens in a new tab), 23 comparisons showed a correlation of −0.1 between offline gain and business gain, and the authors call offline performance "only a health check".

The metrics of an experiment are fixed before it starts, in three roles.

A primary metric must be hard to improve by making the product worse. A Bing experiment with a bug that showed very poor results raised queries per user by over 10% and revenue per user by over 30%, because people had to search again and clicked more ads. The paper reporting it (opens in a new tab) recommends sessions per user instead, since satisfied users come back.

The randomization unit

The randomization unit is the thing the coin is flipped for. The default is the user, for two reasons given in the 2009 survey by Kohavi and colleagues (opens in a new tab): a person should get the same experience on every visit, and the statistics assume units are independent. Assignment uses a hash, not a stored coin flip:

python
import hashlib

def variant(experiment_id, user_id, treatment_share=0.5):
    digest = hashlib.md5(f"{experiment_id}:{user_id}".encode()).hexdigest()
    bucket = int(digest, 16) % 1000          # a stable number from 0 to 999
    return "treatment" if bucket < treatment_share * 1000 else "control"

A user always lands in the same bucket, no lookup table is needed, and hashing the experiment id with the user id keeps assignments in different experiments independent, provided the hash mixes well. The survey found that weak hash functions fail this and that MD5 passed; it is used here for mixing, not security. Two traps remain.

  • Unit of serving. A serving platform may split by request. SageMaker production variants (opens in a new tab) distribute requests at random by variant weight, so one user can get both models. For a user-level test, compute the variant yourself and pass it as TargetVariant.
  • Unit of analysis. If you randomize users but treat page views as independent observations, the standard error comes out too small, because one user's page views resemble each other. Reduce the data to one number per user first.

Reading the result

First check the split. If a 50/50 test delivers 140,000 and 137,400 users, something removed users from one arm, and they are rarely a random subset. This is a sample ratio mismatch (SRM), found with a chi-square test on the two counts. Microsoft reports (opens in a new tab) one in about 6% of its A/B tests and flags it at p < 0.0005. A test with an SRM is debugged, not interpreted.

Then estimate the effect with a confidence interval, which shows how large the effect plausibly is and not only whether it is zero:

δ^=p^B−p^A,SE=p^A(1−p^A)nA+p^B(1−p^B)nB,δ^±1.96 SE\hat\delta = \hat p_B - \hat p_A, \qquad \text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p_A(1-\hat p_A)}{n_A} + \frac{\hat p_B(1-\hat p_B)}{n_B}}, \qquad \hat\delta \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}δ^=p^​B​−p^​A​,SE=nA​p^​A​(1−p^​A​)​+nB​p^​B​(1−p^​B​)​​,δ^±1.96SE

Do not stop at the first significant look. The 5% false-positive rate holds for one planned analysis. Checking repeatedly and stopping when p < 0.05 gives chance many attempts, as Evan Miller's article (opens in a new tab) explains. Simulated A/A tests, in which both arms are identical, show the size of the problem.

Looking is harmless; stopping because of what you saw is not. If early stopping is needed, use a sequential test built for it and accept that, as the GrowthBook documentation (opens in a new tab) notes, it reduces power.

Guardrail metrics

A guardrail metric is one the change is not meant to improve but must not damage. Guardrails turn "ship if the primary metric wins" into a rule with conditions. Spotify's published rule (opens in a new tab) ships only when the treatment is significantly better on at least one success metric, significantly non-inferior on every guardrail, shows no significant deterioration and passes the quality checks.

Non-inferiority means choosing a margin (the largest harm you would tolerate) and requiring the confidence bound on the harm to stay inside it. An interval that merely includes zero does not pass, because with few users it includes large harms as well. Every guardrail must pass, so each one added lowers the chance that a good change is approved unless the sample size is planned for it.

Novelty, primacy and interference

Effects can change over time. A novelty effect is a burst of use because something is new, and it fades. A primacy effect is the opposite: experienced users are slower with a changed interface until they relearn it. To look for either, plot the difference between the variants over time, or analyse new users only, who have no habits to unlearn. Treat trends in the first days with suspicion: the Bing authors found that most suspected cases were noise, because early estimates vary widely and drift back toward the final value.

Interference breaks the independence assumption itself: one unit's treatment changes another unit's outcome. A better messaging feature makes treated members write to friends in control, who become more active too. In a marketplace both groups draw on the same drivers or stock. The remedy is to randomize something larger than a user: clusters of connected members, as in LinkedIn's design (opens in a new tab), or alternating time windows for a whole population, a switchback test (opens in a new tab). The price is fewer units and less power.

Interleaving and bandits

Interleaving compares two rankers on the same user. The two result lists are merged, each item is tagged with the ranker that supplied it, and the ranker whose items attract more clicks or bookings wins. Every user is their own control, so far less traffic is needed; Airbnb reports (opens in a new tab) about a 50-fold speed-up. The output is a preference between rankers, not the size of the effect on a business metric, so interleaving serves as a screen before an A/B test.

A multi-armed bandit changes the traffic split while the experiment runs, sending more users to the variants that are doing better. That lowers the cost of showing a poor variant, called regret, which matters with many variants or short-lived decisions. The price is inference: Stitch Fix notes (opens in a new tab) that confidence intervals and error rates are much harder to control than in an A/B test.

Step 3: compute the primary metric and the guardrail

python
from math import ceil, sqrt
from statistics import NormalDist

N = NormalDist()

def sample_size(p_base, rel_lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """Users needed in EACH arm to detect a relative lift in a conversion rate."""
    p_new = p_base * (1 + rel_lift)
    z = N.inv_cdf(1 - alpha / 2) + N.inv_cdf(power)
    variance = p_base * (1 - p_base) + p_new * (1 - p_new)
    return ceil(z ** 2 * variance / (p_new - p_base) ** 2)

def srm_p_value(n_control, n_treatment):
    """Chi-square test with 1 degree of freedom against a 50/50 split."""
    expected = (n_control + n_treatment) / 2
    chi2 = (n_control - expected) ** 2 / expected + (n_treatment - expected) ** 2 / expected
    return chi2, 2 * (1 - N.cdf(sqrt(chi2)))

def compare(x_c, n_c, x_t, n_t):
    """Treatment minus control: difference, z, two-sided p, standard error."""
    p_c, p_t = x_c / n_c, x_t / n_t
    pooled = (x_c + x_t) / (n_c + n_t)
    z = (p_t - p_c) / sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n_c + 1 / n_t))
    se = sqrt(p_c * (1 - p_c) / n_c + p_t * (1 - p_t) / n_t)
    return p_t - p_c, z, 2 * (1 - N.cdf(abs(z))), se

Edge cases and limitations

A sample ratio mismatch can reverse the conclusion
Microsoft describes an MSN test that raised the number of rotating cards in a carousel from 12 to 16. The scorecard showed engagement falling, but the user counts in the two variants differed from the configured ratio. The cause was that users of the new variant engaged so much that a bot-detection filter removed them from the analysis. Once that was fixed the result flipped to a significant increase. Run the chi-square check on user counts before reading any metric, and treat a failure as a bug to find, not a caveat to mention.
Early results look like trends even when nothing is happening
In the first days the estimate rests on few users and swings widely. The Bing team shows a four-day series that climbs steadily from negative toward zero and turns out to be an A/A test with no difference at all; they compute that the first day's estimate has a 67% chance of falling outside the 95% bound that applies at the end of a 21-day run. Do not extrapolate the first days into a novelty or primacy story. Wait for the planned duration, then compare new and returning users.
Some metrics do not get more precise when the test runs longer
For a count such as sessions per user, both the mean and the spread keep growing as days are added. The Bing team found that the confidence interval of the percentage change for sessions per user did not shrink over a 31-day period, so extra weeks added no power. For such metrics, get precision from more users per day (a larger share of traffic) and choose the duration to cover weekly cycles and possible novelty effects.
Two good models agree on most users
When a new model replaces a similar one, most users get the same output from both, and their data only adds noise. Booking.com's toy example has two classifiers that disagree on at most 15% of cases. The fix is triggered analysis: call both models in the treatment groups and analyse only the users for whom the outputs differ. The estimate then applies to those users, and it has to be scaled by the trigger rate to give the effect on everyone. Run the sample ratio check on the triggered population as well.
Too little traffic for the effect you can realistically expect
Detecting a 5% relative lift on a 4% conversion rate takes about 154,000 users per arm. A site with 1,000 eligible users a day would need 309 days. Running the test for two weeks anyway does not give a weaker answer; it gives an interval so wide that both a useful gain and a real loss fit inside. Options are to test only changes expected to have large effects, to use a more sensitive metric closer to the change, or to use interleaving when the change is a ranker.
Twenty metrics give chance twenty attempts
At a significance level of 0.05, a change with no effect still moves about one metric in twenty significantly. Picking the metric that moved after the fact turns noise into a result. Name one primary metric in advance. If there are several success metrics, split the significance level between them, as Spotify's rule does, and treat everything else on the dashboard as a lead for the next experiment.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You are replacing a model, ranker or feature with a changed version and need to know whether a user-level metric moves because of it.
  • The change can be shown to some users and withheld from others at the same time, and the outcome is logged per user within days or a few weeks.
  • A sample size calculation shows that your traffic reaches the needed users per arm in roughly one to four weeks.
  • An offline gain looks promising but depends on things offline data cannot show, such as latency, user adaptation or a proxy training target.

Think twice when

  • Traffic is far below the required sample size, for example a business product with a few hundred accounts. The interval will be too wide to support any decision.
  • Users affect each other strongly and you can only randomize individuals, as with marketplace pricing or social feeds. Use a cluster or switchback design.
  • The outcome takes months to observe, such as annual renewal, and you have no short-term metric that has been shown to predict it.
  • The change cannot be withheld from a control group, as with a security fix or a legal requirement.

A simpler alternative. A staged rollout to 1%, 10%, 50% and then all users, with monitoring of errors and latency, answers "does it break?" and is enough for changes that should not affect behaviour, such as a refactor or an infrastructure migration. It stops being enough when you need to know whether a metric moved because of the change, since a before-and-after comparison cannot separate the change from everything else that happened that week.

Key takeaways

  • An offline metric gain shows that a model predicts logged data better, and only a randomised online experiment shows whether it changes what users do.
  • Choose the primary metric, the guardrails, the randomization unit, the minimum detectable effect and the run time before the test starts.
  • The required sample grows with the variance of the metric and with one over the square of the effect, so detecting half the effect takes four times the users.
  • Check the sample ratio before reading any metric, and do not stop a fixed-length test because an early look was significant.
  • When users affect each other, randomize clusters or time windows instead of individuals and accept wider intervals.
  • Interleaving quickly shows which of two rankers users prefer and bandits reduce the cost of bad variants, but neither gives the trustworthy effect size of an A/B test.

Example 1 of 5

Booking.com: offline model gains checked against randomised controlled trials

Booking.comDocumented in a paper

Booking.com's KDD 2019 paper reviews about 150 customer-facing machine learning applications that were validated through randomised controlled trials, and describes how the company relates offline model performance to business metrics and how it designs experiments for models. [3][4]

The problem

Teams could raise a classifier's ROC AUC or a ranker's mean reciprocal rank offline, but the company cares about business metrics such as conversion, customer service tickets and cancellations. Two questions had to be answered: does an offline gain predict a business gain, and how do you measure a model's causal effect when many users in the experiment are never affected by it, or when the model also slows the page down?

Where this topic fits

The experiment sits after offline evaluation, which the paper treats as a health check, and before the decision to keep a model. Triggered analysis and the choice of comparison groups are part of the experiment design, fixed before traffic is assigned.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A new model is built and checked offline to confirm that the algorithm does what was intended. The offline number is not used as the launch decision.
  2. Users are randomly assigned to groups. The control group is exposed to no change and does not call the model, which also makes it a safety net: all traffic can be sent there if the setup misbehaves.
  3. Two treatment groups both call the model. In T1 a user is shown the change when the model output meets the triggering condition. In T2 the model is called and nothing is shown.
  4. The analysis keeps only the triggered users of T1 and T2, the people for whom the model would have changed something, so that unaffected users do not dilute the effect.
  5. Three comparisons separate the causes. T1 against T2 measures the new functionality at equal computing cost, control against T2 measures the slowdown alone, and control against T1 measures the overall effect.
  6. To compare a new model with the current one, the triggering condition becomes "the two models disagree", because users who get the same output from both cannot show a difference.
  7. Looking back over 23 such comparisons, the authors plotted the offline gain of each new model against its gain in the business metric and found no correlation.

Why this approach fits

Randomisation is the only step in this pipeline that measures the business metric under the new model's own decisions. An offline metric scores the model on data that the previous system selected, and against a target such as clicks that is only a proxy for conversion.

The three-group design answers a problem specific to models. A model-driven feature bundles two changes, the feature and the extra computation, and an ordinary two-group test cannot tell a useless feature from a useful one that was cancelled out by latency.

Limitation or tradeoff

The authors warn against generalising too far: the comparisons come from one company, the models target the same business metric, and each new model tries to improve on one that had already succeeded. Triggered analysis estimates the effect on triggered users, so the result has to be scaled by the trigger rate to describe all users. Splitting traffic into three groups also leaves fewer users per group, which lengthens the test.

What the sources document

  • The paper analyses about 150 successful customer-facing applications of machine learning at Booking.com that were validated through randomised controlled trials. [3]
  • For 23 comparisons (46 models) of a new model against a baseline, evaluated offline by ROC AUC or mean reciprocal rank, the Pearson correlation between the offline performance gain and the business metric gain observed in a trial was -0.1 with a 90% confidence interval of -0.45 to 0.27. [3]

Example 2 of 5

LinkedIn: member-level and cluster-level tests run side by side to detect interference

LinkedInDocumented in an engineering article

A 2019 post on the LinkedIn Engineering blog describes a method used at LinkedIn to find out whether a feature's A/B test is biased by network effects, by running two randomisation schemes at the same time and comparing their estimates. [5]

The problem

A member-level A/B test assumes that the behaviour of control members is unaffected by treated members. On a social network that fails. The post's example is a member whose friend gets a better messaging experience, spends more time on the site and sends more messages, including to the member who is not in the treatment. Treatment leaks into control, the measured difference no longer equals the effect of launching to everyone, and the test can lead to the wrong decision. LinkedIn wanted a test that says whether a given kind of feature has this problem.

Where this topic fits

This is a decision about the randomization unit, taken before the experiment starts. The member graph is clustered in advance. The comparison of the two estimates happens at analysis time and tells the team whether an ordinary member-level test can be trusted for that type of feature or whether a design that measures network effects is needed.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Partition the graph, with active members as nodes and connections as edges, into 10,000 clusters so that as many connections as possible fall inside a cluster and as few as possible cross between clusters.
  2. Split the clusters at random into two parallel experiments, grouping clusters with similar characteristics into strata first.
  3. In the first experiment, randomize individual members to treatment or control, as in an ordinary A/B test.
  4. In the second experiment, randomize whole clusters, so that when a member is treated a large share of that member's connections is treated as well.
  5. Estimate the treatment effect in each experiment. In the cluster experiment the estimate is built from cluster-level means, because the cluster is the unit that was randomized.
  6. Test whether the two estimates differ. Without interference both designs estimate the same quantity, so a significant difference is evidence of network effects.
  7. Reduce variance by stratifying clusters and by subtracting each member's pre-experiment value of the metric, because the number of clusters limits the precision of the comparison.

Why this approach fits

Interference cannot be seen from inside a member-level test: its estimate looks like any other number with a confidence interval. Comparing against a design in which treated members mostly talk to treated members exposes the gap directly, and the randomisation of clusters keeps both estimates causal. Running the two designs at the same time removes differences in season or traffic between them.

Limitation or tradeoff

Ten thousand clusters are far fewer units than the number of members, so the comparison is noisy; the post says the power of the test can be low and spends much of its length on variance reduction. Clusters are never fully isolated, because members have connections across many communities, so some treatment still leaks and the cluster estimate is itself imperfect. The post also notes a trade-off: more clusters give more power and less isolation. The test answers whether interference exists; measuring its size needs further methods.

What the sources document

  • The post explains that most methods assume there is no interference, which it also calls a network effect, and illustrates the violation with a member who is not targeted but receives more messages because a treated friend has a better messaging experience. [5]
  • The LinkedIn graph of active members and their connections is clustered into 10,000 clusters, which are split into two parallel experiments: an individual-level experiment and a cluster-based experiment in which a whole cluster is either in treatment or in control. [5]
  • The post states that if there is no network effect both experiments should give the same estimated effect, and frames the comparison as a hypothesis test whose rejection means there is interference. [5]
  • In the reported results for one experiment, the post-treatment effect measured by the cluster-based experiment (0.81) was much higher than the effect measured by the individual-level experiment (0.24). [5]
  • The post says more clusters mean more power because clusters are the randomization units, but that the more clusters are created the less isolated they are, and that the power of the test can sometimes be low. [5]

Example 3 of 5

Spotify: one shipping rule for success, guardrail, deterioration and quality metrics

SpotifyDocumented in an engineering article

A 2024 article on the Spotify Engineering blog, written by three of the company's data scientists, describes a decision rule for A/B tests with several metrics and the corrections to false-positive and false-negative rates that the rule requires. The article presents it in connection with Confidence, Spotify's experimentation platform. [6]

The problem

An experiment reports many metrics, and a team needs one rule that turns them into a decision to ship or not, with known risks of shipping a bad change and of rejecting a good one. Guardrail metrics change the statistics. The treatment must pass all of them, so the usual corrections for testing many metrics do not apply in the usual way, while the chance that a good change passes every guardrail falls quickly as guardrails are added.

Where this topic fits

The rule is applied twice. At design time it sets the significance level and the power that each metric must be planned for, and so the sample size. At decision time it is the checklist that the results are read against.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Sort the metrics by role. Success metrics should improve and get a superiority test. Guardrail metrics should not deteriorate by more than a chosen margin and get a non-inferiority test.
  2. Add deterioration tests, which look for significant regressions in any metric, and quality tests such as sample ratio mismatch and pre-exposure bias, which check the experiment itself.
  3. Divide the false-positive rate among the success metrics only, because a win on any one of them can trigger a decision to ship.
  4. Leave the false-positive rate unadjusted for guardrails. All of them must pass, so more guardrails do not create more chances to ship by accident.
  5. Tighten the false-negative rate instead. Each metric is powered for a smaller rate so that the probability that a good change passes everything together stays at the target.
  6. Ship only if at least one success metric is significantly better, every guardrail is significantly non-inferior, nothing shows significant deterioration and no quality test fails.

Why this approach fits

A written rule removes the temptation to weigh metrics after seeing them. Testing guardrails for non-inferiority asks the right question, which is whether there is evidence that the harm is smaller than the margin; a guardrail that is merely "not significantly worse" passes most easily in the smallest experiments. Planning power for the whole rule keeps the test from being designed for the success metric alone and then failing on guardrails that never had enough data.

Limitation or tradeoff

The extra power has to be paid for in users, as the calculation shows, and every added guardrail raises the bill. Each guardrail also needs a margin, and choosing how much harm is acceptable is a product judgement the statistics cannot make. The article says the rule does not suit every case, for example when a decision depends partly on external factors that the experiment cannot model.

What the sources document

  • The article describes success metrics tested with superiority tests, guardrail metrics tested with non-inferiority tests, deterioration metrics tested with inferiority tests, and quality metrics such as tests for sample ratio mismatch and pre-exposure bias. [6]
  • The stated rule is to ship if and only if the treatment is significantly superior on at least one success metric, significantly non-inferior on all guardrail metrics, no metric shows evidence of deterioration, and no quality test invalidates the experiment. [6]
  • The article says false-positive rates should not be adjusted for guardrail metrics, while the false-positive rate for success metrics is divided by the number of success metrics. [6]
  • It says false-negative rates must be corrected for the number of guardrail metrics to obtain the intended power, and gives a correction whose simplified form divides the rate by the number of guardrail metrics plus one. [6]
  • For independent guardrail metrics each powered at 80%, the article reports simultaneous power far below 40% with five guardrails and around 11% with ten. [6]

Airbnb search ranking: interleaving as a fast screen before A/B tests

AirbnbDocumented in a paper

A KDD 2025 paper by Airbnb engineers, available on arXiv, describes the interleaving system used in Airbnb's search ranking experiments, how it was validated against A/B tests on real traffic, and where it gave a misleading answer. [7]

The problem

A/B tests of search rankers take weeks at Airbnb. The paper gives three reasons: guests book rarely, so experiment traffic is low compared with a search engine; the target metric is a booking, which can come days after the search; and the effect of each change is small in a mature system. Offline evaluation takes one or two hours but lacks accuracy, partly because offline ranking metrics and online conversion are often inconsistent. With limited A/B capacity, the team needed a middle stage that is faster than an A/B test and more accurate than offline evaluation.

Where this topic fits

Interleaving sits between offline evaluation and the A/B test. A first layer of randomisation sends most users to ordinary A/B tests and a smaller share to interleaving; a second layer maps those users to one interleaving experiment. Rankers that win there become candidates for an A/B test, which remains the measurement of business impact.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. For a user assigned to interleaving, the search system sends the same request to the control ranker and the treatment ranker in parallel and gets two ranked lists.
  2. A coin flip for that search decides which ranker goes first. The lists are then merged by team drafting: at each turn the next unused listing is taken from each ranker.
  3. If the two listings differ they form a competitive pair, placed in the coin-flip order and tagged with their teams. If both rankers offer the same listing it is added once with no team.
  4. When a listing that was shown as part of a pair is booked, its team scores a win. A booking is credited to every occasion on which the listing was shown to that user during the experiment.
  5. For each user, the wins of the two teams are compared. The result is the share of users who preferred treatment minus the share who preferred control, tested with a test of proportions.
  6. Quality checks confirm that both teams received the same number of impressions and came first in half of the pairs; otherwise the experiment is declared invalid.
  7. Rankers that pass go on to an A/B test, with adjustments where the result suggested them.

Why this approach fits

In an A/B test the difference between two rankers has to be found through the noise of two separate groups of people with different trips, budgets and dates. Interleaving removes that noise by letting one person choose between the two rankers' listings side by side in a single result page. Sparse events such as bookings then carry far more information per user, which suits a product where people buy a few times a year.

Limitation or tradeoff

Interleaving judges listings pair by pair, so it misjudges rankers that optimise the result set as a whole. The paper's case is a ranker that traded a little booking probability for a secondary objective: next to control listings its choices looked less attractive, and interleaving reported a significant negative effect while the A/B test showed neutral conversion. The method also needs two full ranking calls per search, applies only to rankers, and produces a preference, not a measurement of revenue or conversion, so an A/B test is still required.

What the sources document

  • The paper says Airbnb's earlier process had two steps, offline evaluation taking 1 to 2 hours but lacking accuracy and A/B testing taking weeks, and introduces interleaving as a middle stage. [7]
  • In the described team drafting, a coin flip decides the order once, differing items from the two rankers form a competitive pair with team assignments, and identical items are added without a team; for C = {a,b,c,d,e} and T = {b,c,a,f,g} the output is {a(C), b(T), c, d(C), f(T)}. [7]
  • Preference is computed per user from the wins of each team, and the p-value comes from a test of proportions on the numbers of users who prefer treatment and control. [7]
  • Across 29 pairs of interleaving and A/B results, the two were directionally aligned 82% of the time with a correlation coefficient of 0.6, and the authors observed about a 50-fold speed-up over A/B testing. [7]
  • For a ranker that re-ordered listings toward a secondary objective, interleaving showed a significant negative impact while conversion remained neutral in the A/B test, which the authors describe as a limitation when set-level optimisation is involved. [7]

Stitch Fix: bandit policies plugged into the experiment allocation engine

Stitch FixDocumented in an engineering article

A 2020 post on Stitch Fix's technology blog describes how the company added multi-armed bandits to its experimentation platform, alongside A/B tests, by reusing the allocation engine that already assigned experiment cells. [8]

The problem

In an A/B test a fixed share of traffic goes to the worse cells for the whole experiment, which the post describes as a relatively high opportunity cost. That cost is hardest to accept when traffic is low or when there are many cells to try. Stitch Fix wanted experiments that learn which cell is best while moving traffic away from poor ones, without asking every engineering application to change how it requests its configuration.

Where this topic fits

The bandit replaces the fixed traffic shares of an A/B test and nothing else. Applications still ask the allocation engine which cell a unit belongs to. Between the outcome data and the engine sit two new pieces: a reward service that reports how each arm is doing, and a policy that turns those reports into selection probabilities.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A data scientist deploys a reward service for the experiment, a small service that returns the current reward estimate of each arm, or the parameters of its posterior distribution.
  2. Batch jobs recompute the estimates from outcome data on a schedule and update the reward service each time they run.
  3. At allocation time the engine asks the reward service for the estimates and passes them to the bandit policy, a deterministic function that returns a selection probability for each arm.
  4. With an epsilon-greedy policy, a fraction epsilon of the probability is spread evenly over all arms and the rest goes to the arm with the highest estimated reward.
  5. The engine turns the probabilities into a cell by hashing the experiment id with the randomization unit to a number from 0 to 999 and looking up which cell owns that number.
  6. As estimates change, the shares move toward better arms. Arms can also be kept running indefinitely as a form of continuous optimisation.

Why this approach fits

The design separates what changes from what does not. Hash-based allocation, logging and the way applications read their configuration are the same for A/B tests and bandits, so a bandit becomes a different way of setting the shares. Putting the reward estimate behind a service lets a data scientist choose the reward, including a model-based one when the true outcome arrives too late to steer the experiment.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post is direct about the cost: an A/B test allows straightforward control of error rates and simple confidence intervals, and for bandits both are much harder. An arm that receives little traffic ends with an imprecise estimate, so a bandit is a poor fit when the goal is to measure how much better the winner is. Estimates updated by batch jobs lag behind reality. In our sketch a returning unit could also be moved to another arm when the slices shift; the post does not describe how the platform handles that.

What the sources document

  • The post says that in an A/B test a fixed proportion of traffic is sent to suboptimal cells for the entire experiment, and that a bandit can be a better choice when traffic is low or the number of cells is large. [8]
  • Each bandit experiment has a dedicated reward microservice that the allocation engine queries at allocation time; it returns a point estimate per arm for epsilon-greedy or posterior parameters for Thompson sampling, and is updated when the data scientists' batch jobs run. [8]
  • A bandit policy is implemented as a deterministic function that takes the arm reward estimates and outputs the arm selection probabilities, and the design requires no changes to the engineering applications that rely on the allocation engine. [8]
  • The allocation engine maps each experiment cell to an exclusive subset of the integers from 0 to 999 and generates a number in that range from the sha1 hash of the experiment id and the randomization unit, modulo 1000. [8]
  • The post states that for multi-armed bandits it can be much harder to estimate confidence intervals and control error rates than for A/B tests. [8]

4Common mistakes

  1. Stopping a fixed-length test on the first day the dashboard shows p below 0.05.

    Why it causes problems
    A p-value is calibrated for one planned analysis. Stopping at the first significant look out of many multiplies the false-positive rate; in the simulation in this lesson, twenty looks turned 5% into about 25%. Changes with no effect get shipped, and the effects reported for real winners are inflated because the test stopped on a lucky high.
    How to recognise it
    The test ended before its planned date or sample size, the experiment record has no planned duration at all, or the reported lift shrinks when the same change is tested again.
    How to fix it
    Write the sample size and end date into the experiment plan before starting, and analyse once at the end. If stopping early for a clear win or loss is a real need, use a sequential testing method that your platform supports and plan for the larger sample it requires.

  2. Reading "not statistically significant" as "the change has no effect".

    Why it causes problems
    An underpowered test cannot tell a useful gain from nothing, or from a loss. A model that would have added 3% is dropped, or a change that costs 2% is shipped as harmless, because the interval was wide enough to hold both.
    How to recognise it
    No sample size was computed before the test, or the confidence interval of the result contains both zero and the minimum detectable effect, as in the rerun of this lesson's first exercise.
    How to fix it
    Compute users per arm from the baseline, the minimum detectable effect and the power before launching. Report the interval, not only the p-value, and describe a wide interval as inconclusive.

  3. Launching a model because its offline metric improved, or choosing a primary metric the model can inflate.

    Why it causes problems
    Offline gains and business gains were uncorrelated across Booking.com's 23 comparisons, and a metric such as clicks or queries can rise while the product gets worse, as the Bing ranking bug showed. The team reports progress and users have a worse experience.
    How to recognise it
    The launch note quotes AUC, NDCG or click-through rate and no experiment result, or the primary metric would go up if results were made deliberately worse.
    How to fix it
    Keep offline metrics as the filter and make a randomised test the launch decision. Choose a primary metric close to lasting user value, such as purchases or return visits, and add guardrails for what the model could damage.

  4. Randomizing by request or session while users see the product across many requests.

    Why it causes problems
    A user who is served by both models sees inconsistent recommendations, and their purchase cannot be credited to either variant. If requests are then analysed as independent, the standard error is too small and noise is declared significant.
    How to recognise it
    The same user id appears in both variants in the exposure log, or the number of analysis units is the number of requests while the metric is something a person does, such as buying.
    How to fix it
    Hash the user id with the experiment id to choose the variant and route explicitly to that model, for example with a target-variant parameter, and compute one value per user before comparing groups.

  5. Reading the metrics without checking the sample ratio first.

    Why it causes problems
    When users are missing from one arm they are usually the ones the change affected most, so every metric is biased. The MSN carousel test showed a loss that became a win once the filter removing engaged users was fixed.
    How to recognise it
    A chi-square test on the user counts of the variants against the configured split gives a very small p-value. Microsoft uses p below 0.0005 as its threshold.
    How to fix it
    Make the sample ratio check the first line of every analysis and block the scorecard when it fails. Then look for the stage that lost users: assignment, redirects or crashes in one variant, log joins, bot filters, or a filter applied in the analysis.

  6. Using a member-level test for a change whose value comes from interactions between users.

    Why it causes problems
    Treatment leaks to control through messages, shared feeds or shared supply. The measured difference then misstates the effect of a full launch, and it can be wrong in either direction depending on whether control users benefit from the spillover or compete with treated users.
    How to recognise it
    The feature involves sending, sharing, matching or a limited shared resource, or a cluster-level estimate differs clearly from the member-level one, as in LinkedIn's comparison.
    How to fix it
    Randomize clusters of connected users, or alternate treatment and control over time windows for a whole region, and analyse at the level that was randomized. Expect wider intervals and plan a longer test.

python
from math import ceil, sqrt
from statistics import NormalDist

N = NormalDist()

def sample_size(p_base, rel_lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    p_new = p_base * (1 + rel_lift)
    z = N.inv_cdf(1 - alpha / 2) + N.inv_cdf(power)
    variance = p_base * (1 - p_base) + p_new * (1 - p_new)
    return ceil(z ** 2 * variance / (p_new - p_base) ** 2)

def srm(n_c, n_t):
    expected = (n_c + n_t) / 2
    chi2 = (n_c - expected) ** 2 / expected + (n_t - expected) ** 2 / expected
    return chi2, 2 * (1 - N.cdf(sqrt(chi2)))

def compare(x_c, n_c, x_t, n_t):
    p_c, p_t = x_c / n_c, x_t / n_t
    pooled = (x_c + x_t) / (n_c + n_t)
    z = (p_t - p_c) / sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n_c + 1 / n_t))
    se = sqrt(p_c * (1 - p_c) / n_c + p_t * (1 - p_t) / n_t)
    return p_t - p_c, z, 2 * (1 - N.cdf(abs(z))), se

n = sample_size(0.08, 0.04)
print(f"A. users per arm: {n:,}; days at 10,000 per arm per day: {n / 10_000:.1f}")

for label, (x_c, n_c, x_t, n_t) in {
    "B. first run": (11_200, 140_000, 11_542, 137_400),
    "C. rerun": (11_184, 139_800, 11_422, 140_150),

    chi2, p_srm = srm(n_c, n_t)
    diff, z, p, se = compare(x_c, n_c, x_t, n_t)

USERS = 30_000

def run(policy, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    shown = {arm: 0 for arm in TRUE_RATE}
    converted = {arm: 0 for arm in TRUE_RATE}
    for i in range(USERS):
        if policy == "equal":
            arm = "ABC"[i % 3]
        else:   # Thompson sampling: one plausible rate per arm, play the largest
            draws = {a: rng.betavariate(1 + converted[a], 1 + shown[a] - converted[a])
                     for a in TRUE_RATE}
            arm = max(draws, key=draws.get)
        shown[arm] += 1
        converted[arm] += rng.random() < TRUE_RATE[arm]
    return shown, converted

for policy in ("equal", "thompson"):
    shown, converted = run(policy, seed=42)
    total = sum(converted.values())
    regret = USERS * max(TRUE_RATE.values()) - sum(shown[a] * TRUE_RATE[a] for a in TRUE_RATE)
    print(f"{policy}: conversions = {total}, expected regret = {regret:.0f}")
    for arm in TRUE_RATE:
        rate = converted[arm] / shown[arm]
        half_width = 1.96 * sqrt(rate * (1 - rate) / shown[arm])
        print(f"   {arm}: shown {shown[arm]:>6}, rate {rate:.4f} +/- {half_width:.4f}")

thompson: conversions = 1658, expected regret = 36

   C: shown  27234, rate 0.0574 +/- 0.0028

What happened. The equal split spent 20,000 users on arms that were worse, which costs 10,000×0.015+10,000×0.010=25010{,}000 \times 0.015 + 10{,}000 \times 0.010 = 25010,000×0.015+10,000×0.010=250 expected conversions. Thompson sampling moved away from A and B after one to two thousand users each and gave 91% of the traffic to C.

What it cost. Under the equal split every arm's rate is known to within about 0.4 points. Under Thompson sampling, B was seen by 1,006 users and its estimate of 3.4% is off by more than a point from the true 4.5%, with a half-width of 1.1 points. The bandit stopped collecting data on B exactly because B looked bad early, so an arm that starts with an unlucky run tends to keep an estimate that is too low. These plain intervals also ignore that the sample sizes depended on the outcomes, so they are too optimistic for bandit data.

Answers. For a two-week banner campaign the goal is conversions during the campaign, and nobody needs a precise estimate of the losing banners, so use the bandit. To report how much better C is than B, use the equal split: its difference of about 1.0 point has a standard error of about 0.3 points, while the bandit's data on B is too thin to support any statement of size.

In repeated runs with seeds 0 to 19 the share given to C ranged from 62% to 97%, so a single bandit run can still be slow to settle. Real systems also update in batches, not after every user.

Check your answer

  • Is your equal-split expected regret exactly 250, and your Thompson sampling regret far smaller?
  • Does the best arm receive most of the traffic under Thompson sampling in your run?
  • Are the confidence intervals of the two weaker arms clearly wider under Thompson sampling than under the equal split?
  • Can you explain why an arm that starts with an unlucky run tends to keep a low estimate under a bandit?

6Curated resources

Chosen for accuracy and usefulness, not search rank. Time figures are estimates.

  • Controlled experiments on the web: survey and practical guide (opens in a new tab)

    Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery (Springer); copy on Ron Kohavi's Stanford page

    PaperIntermediateFreeAbout 2 hours (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The standard practical guide to online controlled experiments: terminology, the sample size formula with worked examples, hash-based randomisation, ramp-up, day-of-week effects, primacy and newness effects, and an appendix on when randomizing by user is not appropriate.
    When to use it
    Right after this lesson, when you are about to plan a first real experiment and want the reasoning behind each rule of thumb.

  • Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: Five Puzzling Outcomes Explained (opens in a new tab)

    Microsoft (KDD 2012 paper hosted on exp-platform.com)

    PaperIntermediateFreeAbout 1 hour (estimate)

    What it teaches
    Five real Bing experiments whose results looked wrong, with the root cause of each: a primary metric that rewarded worse search results, click tracking that changed the result, apparent trends in the first days, metrics that do not gain power with time, and carryover between experiments.
    When to use it
    When an experiment result surprises you, or before you choose a primary metric for a search or recommendation product.

  • Diagnosing Sample Ratio Mismatch in A/B Testing (opens in a new tab)

    Microsoft Research, Experimentation Platform

    ArticleIntermediateFreeAbout 25 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    What a sample ratio mismatch is, how often it occurs, a real case in which it reversed the conclusion, and a method for finding the stage of the experiment that caused it.
    When to use it
    The first time your own sample ratio check fails and you need to find where the users were lost.

  • How Not To Run an A/B Test (opens in a new tab)

    Evan Miller

    ArticleBeginnerFreeAbout 15 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    Why checking a running test repeatedly and stopping at the first significant result invalidates the reported significance level, with a small table of how strict each look must be to keep the overall rate at 5%.
    When to use it
    Before you build or use a dashboard that shows live p-values, and when you need to explain to a colleague why a test cannot be stopped early.

  • Power (opens in a new tab)

    GrowthBook documentation

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 20 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    How an experimentation tool defines power and minimum detectable effect, the formula it uses, which inputs its power calculator needs, and how sequential testing changes the calculation.
    When to use it
    When you move from the hand calculation in this lesson to a tool's power calculator and want to know what it is computing.

  • Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: A Practical Guide to A/B Testing (opens in a new tab)

    Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu (Cambridge University Press)

    BookIntermediatePaid

    What it teaches
    The book-length treatment by Kohavi, Tang and Xu of everything in this lesson, including metric design, guardrails, interference and experimentation platforms. The site offers the first chapter as a free download.
    When to use it
    When experiments become a regular part of your work and you need a reference to keep on the desk.
