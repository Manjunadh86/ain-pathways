Time Series Forecasting

Time series forecasting predicts future values of a quantity that is recorded at regular time steps, using the patterns in its own history, such as trend and seasonality, and sometimes other inputs that are known in advance. A forecasting method is judged by backtesting: fitting it on the data up to a cutoff and scoring it on what came after.

1Overview

Why it matters

Most planning decisions need a number for a period that has not happened yet: how many staff to roster next Saturday, how much stock to order for the next four weeks, how many servers to have running at 9 a.m. on Monday. Those numbers come from time series forecasts, and a forecast that is off turns directly into empty shelves, idle staff or an outage.

The modelling is rarely where the work goes wrong. The testing is. Rows of a time series are not interchangeable, so the shuffle-and-split habit from other machine learning tasks lets a model train on the future and report an error it will never achieve in production. A rolling average that quietly includes the day being predicted does the same. A complex model gets shipped without ever being compared with "same as last week". Analysts, data scientists and ML engineers who build forecasts, and anyone who approves a plan based on one, need to recognise these failures and know how to run a backtest that would expose them.

Key terms

Time series
One quantity recorded at regular time steps, such as orders per day, where the order of the observations carries information.
Forecast horizon
How many steps ahead a forecast reaches, such as the next 4 quarters or the next 28 days. Written h.
Trend
The long-run rise or fall of a series once the repeating pattern and the noise are set aside.
Seasonality and seasonal period
A pattern that repeats with a fixed, known length. The seasonal period m is the number of steps in one cycle, such as 7 for daily data with a weekly rhythm.
Lag
An earlier value of the series. Lag 1 is the value one step back, and lag 7 of daily data is the same weekday one week back.
Naive and seasonal naive forecast
The two no-model baselines. Naive repeats the last observed value, and seasonal naive repeats the value from the same season one cycle earlier.
Stationary series
A series whose statistical properties do not depend on when it is observed, so it has no trend and no seasonality.
Differencing
Replacing each value by its change from the previous value (or from the same season one cycle earlier) to remove trend or seasonality.
Backtest
A test that replays history by fitting a method on the data before a cutoff, forecasting the period after it, and comparing the forecast with what happened.
Leakage (look-ahead)
Any way in which information from after the forecast origin reaches the model during training or feature building, making test results better than real ones.

2Explanation

Introduction

In linear regression every row was a separate case, and you could shuffle the rows without losing anything. A time series is different. It is one quantity recorded at regular steps, such as rentals per quarter or orders per day, and the order of the rows is the information. Forecasting means predicting the values that come next, and how far ahead you predict is the forecast horizon.

Take a bike-hire shop that has counted its rentals every quarter for six years. The 24 numbers are invented for this lesson and return in the worked example.

Three things are visible in the plot, and they are the standard vocabulary for describing any series:

  • Trend: the long-run rise or fall. The yearly average climbs from 107.25 rentals per quarter in year 1 to 158.5 in year 6.
  • Seasonality: a pattern that repeats with a fixed, known period. Here the period is four quarters. The third quarter sits about 33 rentals above the trend line and the first about 24 below it, year after year.
  • Remainder: what is left when trend and seasonality are taken out. Some of it is noise that no model can predict.

A forecast extends the trend and repeats the seasonal pattern, and the remainder limits how accurate it can be. Two questions then decide whether a forecasting project works.

The first is how to capture the patterns. A classical model such as exponential smoothing or ARIMA is built around level, trend and seasonality. A machine learning forecaster turns past values into features called lags and hands them to an ordinary regression model.

The second is how to test the result honestly. The future must never reach the model during training, so forecasters are evaluated by backtesting: fit on the data up to a cutoff, forecast the period after it, compare with what happened, then move the cutoff forward and repeat.

This lesson takes the two questions in order: components, the simple baselines every model has to beat, exponential smoothing, ARIMA, lag features with ML models, and backtests with time-ordered splits. Errors are measured with the regression metrics you already know, plus one scaled metric made for forecasts.

How it works

Components and the seasonal period

A useful first model is that each observation is a sum of parts:

yt=Tt+St+Rty_t = T_t + S_t + R_tyt​=Tt​+St​+Rt​

where TtT_tTt​ is the trend, StS_tSt​ the seasonal component and RtR_tRt​ the remainder. This additive form fits when the seasonal swing stays the same size as the level changes. When the swing grows in proportion to the level, the multiplicative form yt=Tt×St×Rty_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_tyt​=Tt​×St​×Rt​ fits better, and taking logarithms (opens in a new tab) turns it back into a sum.

The seasonal period mmm is the number of steps in one cycle: 4 for quarterly data, 12 for monthly, 7 for daily data with a weekly rhythm. Seasonality always has a fixed and known period (opens in a new tab). Rises and falls of irregular length, such as business cycles, are called cyclic and are much harder to forecast.

Baselines come first

Two forecasts need no model. The naive forecast repeats the last observed value, y^T+h=yT\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_Ty^​T+h​=yT​. The seasonal naive forecast repeats the value from the same season one cycle earlier, y^T+h=yT+h−m\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_{T+h-m}y^​T+h​=yT+h−m​ for horizons up to one cycle: next Tuesday will look like last Tuesday. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos use them as benchmarks (opens in a new tab), and a method that cannot beat them is not worth its complexity.

Dividing a method's mean absolute error by the mean absolute error of the seasonal naive forecast inside the training data gives the mean absolute scaled error (MASE). A value below 1 means the errors are smaller than the baseline's typical error. MASE has no units, so it can be averaged across series of different sizes. Percentage errors such as MAPE cannot always be used for that, because they are undefined when an actual value is zero (opens in a new tab).

Exponential smoothing

Exponential smoothing keeps a running estimate of each component and nudges it towards every new observation. The simplest version (opens in a new tab) tracks only a level ℓt\ell_tℓt​:

ℓt=α yt+(1−α) ℓt−1\ell_t = \alpha\, y_t + (1 - \alpha)\, \ell_{t-1}ℓt​=αyt​+(1−α)ℓt−1​

and forecasts ℓT\ell_TℓT​ for every future step. The smoothing parameter α\alphaα lies between 0 and 1. Near 1 the level chases the latest value, and at exactly 1 this is the naive forecast. Near 0 it changes slowly and averages over a long history. Written out, the level is a weighted average of all past values with weights that shrink exponentially with age, which gives the method its name.

The Holt-Winters method (opens in a new tab) adds a trend btb_tbt​ and mmm seasonal terms sts_tst​, each with its own smoothing parameter:

ℓt=α (yt−st−m)+(1−α)(ℓt−1+bt−1)bt=β (ℓt−ℓt−1)+(1−β) bt−1st=γ (yt−ℓt−1−bt−1)+(1−γ) st−m\begin{aligned} \ell_t &= \alpha\,(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta\,(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\, b_{t-1} \\ s_t &= \gamma\,(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)\, s_{t-m} \end{aligned}ℓt​bt​st​​=α(yt​−st−m​)+(1−α)(ℓt−1​+bt−1​)=β(ℓt​−ℓt−1​)+(1−β)bt−1​=γ(yt​−ℓt−1​−bt−1​)+(1−γ)st−m​​

The forecast hhh steps ahead, for hhh up to one cycle, is y^T+h=ℓT+h bT+sT+h−m\hat{y}_{T+h} = \ell_T + h\,b_T + s_{T+h-m}y^​T+h​=ℓT​+hbT​+sT+h−m​: the last level, plus hhh times the last trend, plus the latest seasonal term for that season. Libraries choose α\alphaα, β\betaβ, γ\gammaγ and the starting values by minimising the squared one-step errors on the training data. A multiplicative variant divides by the seasonal term where this one subtracts it.

ARIMA

ARIMA models describe how each value depends on the values and errors just before it. The name lists three parts:

  • I (integrated), order ddd: difference the series ddd times, yt′=yt−yt−1y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1}yt′​=yt​−yt−1​, to remove trend. The other two parts need a stationary (opens in a new tab) series, one whose statistical properties do not depend on when you look. A seasonal difference yt−yt−my_t - y_{t-m}yt​−yt−m​ removes a stable seasonal pattern.
  • AR (autoregressive), order ppp: a regression of the differenced value on its own previous ppp values.
  • MA (moving average), order qqq: a regression on the previous qqq forecast errors ε\varepsilonε.
yt′=c+ϕ1yt−1′+⋯+ϕpyt−p′+θ1εt−1+⋯+θqεt−q+εty'_t = c + \phi_1 y'_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y'_{t-p} + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q} + \varepsilon_tyt′​=c+ϕ1​yt−1′​+⋯+ϕp​yt−p′​+θ1​εt−1​+⋯+θq​εt−q​+εt​

ARIMA(0,1,0) (opens in a new tab) with no constant is the random walk, whose forecast is the naive forecast, so the baseline is a member of the family. Seasonal ARIMA adds a second set of orders (P,D,Q)(P, D, Q)(P,D,Q) that act at lag mmm. Software usually picks the orders by comparing candidate models, and a backtest should confirm the choice. In Python, statsmodels implements both classical families:

python
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing

hw = ExponentialSmoothing(y, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=4).fit()
sarima = ARIMA(y, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 1, 4)).fit()
print(hw.forecast(4), sarima.forecast(4))   # the next four quarters from each model

Here y is the series as an array or a pandas Series, and fit() estimates the parameters.

Lag features and machine learning forecasters

The AR part of ARIMA is a linear regression on past values. Machine learning forecasters generalise the idea. Build a table in which each row is one time step, the target is the value at that step, and the features are things known before it. Any regressor can then be trained on the table, from linear regression to gradient-boosted trees.

python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({"y": [75, 119, 144, 91, 82, 130, 148, 106]})
df["lag_1"] = df["y"].shift(1)                           # previous quarter
df["lag_4"] = df["y"].shift(4)                           # same quarter last year
df["mean_prev_4"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(4).mean()   # shift first, then average
print(df.dropna())

A lag is the series shifted back by some number of steps. Rolling statistics summarise several lags, and the shift(1) before rolling keeps the current value out of its own feature. Calendar columns (quarter, weekday, holiday flag) and inputs that are planned in advance (price, promotion) join the same table. scikit-learn's example on lagged features (opens in a new tab) builds 13 such columns for hourly bike-sharing demand and trains a gradient-boosting model on them.

Three points separate this from ordinary tabular learning:

  • Every feature must be known when the forecast is made. To predict four steps ahead in one go you cannot use lag_1, because steps two to four would need values that do not exist yet. Either use only lags at least as old as the horizon (direct forecasting), or predict one step, feed the prediction back in as the newest lag and repeat (recursive forecasting), which lets errors compound.
  • One model can learn from many series. Stacking the tables of thousands of products, with an identifier column, lets the model share patterns across them. Classical models are fitted one series at a time.
  • Tree models cannot extrapolate. A tree predicts averages of target values it has seen, so on a rising series its forecasts flatten at the top of the training range. Predict the change from a lag, or remove the trend first.

Backtesting with time-ordered splits

A random train/test split puts later rows in training and earlier rows in the test set. With lag features that is fatal: the training rows that come just after a test row contain its target value as one of their lags. The evaluation has to copy production, where the model at the forecast origin knows only the past.

Time series cross-validation (opens in a new tab), also called evaluation on a rolling forecasting origin, repeats that situation several times. Train on everything up to an origin, forecast the next hhh steps, record the errors, move the origin forward, and average over all origins. An expanding window keeps the whole history in every training set. A sliding window drops the oldest data so the training size stays fixed, which costs less and forgets conditions that no longer apply.

scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit (opens in a new tab) produces these folds:

python
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit

cv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=3, test_size=4)   # three origins, four steps tested at each
# max_train_size=12 turns the expanding window into a sliding one
# gap=2 leaves out the last two rows before each test fold

Set test_size to the real horizon. Use gap when the newest rows would not have arrived yet in production, or when they would leak through lag features. Tune hyperparameters and fit scalers inside each training fold only. Once a winner is chosen, refit it on all the data to produce the real forecast.

Errors grow with the horizon, so look at them per step ahead as well as on average. Their size across the folds is also the honest basis for the range you quote around a forecast.

  • Naive: repeat the last value, 142, four times. The absolute errors are ∣131−142∣=11|131 - 142| = 11∣131−142∣=11, then 25, 52 and 0, so MAE =88/4=22.0= 88 / 4 = 22.0=88/4=22.0.

Step 2: add two models and backtest over three origins

One held-out year is one sample. The script scores four methods on three test years (4, 5 and 6), always training only on the years before the test year. holt_winters implements the three update equations from the previous section with fixed smoothing parameters. lag_regression fits y^t=c+a yt−1+b yt−4\hat{y}_t = c + a\,y_{t-1} + b\,y_{t-4}y^​t​=c+ayt−1​+byt−4​ by least squares and forecasts recursively.

python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit

y = np.array([75, 119, 144, 91, 82, 130, 148, 106, 104, 137, 162, 107,
              113, 142, 173, 125, 126, 161, 191, 142, 131, 167, 194, 142], dtype=float)
M, H = 4, 4   # season length and forecast horizon, in quarters

def naive(train):
    return np.repeat(train[-1], H)

def seasonal_naive(train):
    return train[-M:].copy()

def holt_winters(train, alpha=0.3, beta=0.1, gamma=0.3):
    level = train[:M].mean()
    trend = (train[M:2 * M].mean() - train[:M].mean()) / M
    season = list(train[:M] - level)
    for t in range(M, len(train)):
        last_level, s = level, season[t % M]
        level = alpha * (train[t] - s) + (1 - alpha) * (last_level + trend)
        season[t % M] = gamma * (train[t] - last_level - trend) + (1 - gamma) * s
        trend = beta * (level - last_level) + (1 - beta) * trend
    n = len(train)
    return np.array([level + (k + 1) * trend + season[(n + k) % M] for k in range(H)])

def lag_regression(train):
    X = np.column_stack([train[M - 1:-1], train[:-M]])   # lag 1 and lag 4
    model = LinearRegression().fit(X, train[M:])
    history = list(train)
    for _ in range(H):                                    # recursive: feed predictions back in
        history.append(model.predict([[history[-1], history[-M]]])[0])
    return np.array(history[-H:])

methods = {"naive": naive, "seasonal naive": seasonal_naive,
           "Holt-Winters": holt_winters, "lag regression": lag_regression}
scores = {name: [] for name in methods}
for train_idx, test_idx in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=3, test_size=H).split(y):
    train, test = y[train_idx], y[test_idx]
    for name, forecast in methods.items():
        scores[name].append(np.abs(test - forecast(train)).mean())
for name, maes in scores.items():
    print(f"{name:15s} folds: {np.round(maes, 2)}  mean MAE: {np.mean(maes):.2f}")

The slowdown in year 6 is a warning to carry forward. If growth really has ended, these numbers are too high. When the first quarter of year 7 arrives, compare it with this forecast and with the seasonal naive one (131, 167, 194, 142) before trusting the remaining three quarters.

Edge cases and limitations

Tree-based models flatten out on a trending series
A decision tree, and so a random forest or a gradient-boosted ensemble, predicts averages of training targets and cannot output a value above the highest it has seen. On a series that keeps growing, its forecasts level off at the top of the training range while the actual values carry on. The sign is a backtest error that always has the same direction and grows with the horizon. Train the model on the difference or ratio to a lag, or remove the trend with a simple model first and let the trees predict what is left. The M5 paper reports that the runner-up adjusted its LightGBM forecasts with multipliers to capture the trend.
Seasonal swings that grow with the level
If the peaks get taller as the series rises, an additive model forecasts peaks that are too low and troughs that are too high late in the series. A widening envelope in the plot is the sign. Use a multiplicative seasonal component or model the logarithm of the series. The choice is not permanent: the US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that many of its labour force series used multiplicative seasonal adjustment before April 2020 and were respecified as additive once a significant outlier was detected.
Zeros and intermittent demand
A product that sells nothing on most days gives a series of zeros with occasional small counts. MAPE divides by the actual value, so it is undefined on the zero days. A model scored on absolute error is rewarded for forecasting zero every day, because the median of such a series is zero. The M5 organisers chose a scaled metric built on squared errors for these reasons. Use a scaled error such as MASE or RMSSE, and consider forecasting at a coarser level, by week or by product group, where the series is smoother.
Less than two seasonal cycles of history
A seasonal pattern can be separated from trend and noise only if each season has been observed more than once. With 14 months of monthly data the model has seen one full cycle and cannot tell a seasonal peak from a one-off spike. Excel's forecasting documentation warns against setting a season length that leaves fewer than two cycles of history. With a short history, use the naive forecast or a plain trend, borrow the seasonal shape from a similar series, and say plainly that the forecast carries no seasonal information of its own.
Holidays that move and other calendar effects
Easter can fall in March or April, and a month can contain four or five weekends. A seasonal model with a fixed period treats these shifts as noise, so it misses the Easter peak one year and predicts a peak that does not come the next. The Census Bureau's seasonal adjustment guidance treats trading-day and moving-holiday effects separately from ordinary seasonality. Add explicit columns: a holiday flag, the number of days until the holiday, the count of each weekday in the period.
A break in the pattern
Forecasting assumes that the process that produced the history is still running. A price change, a new competitor, a pandemic or a change in how the metric is counted breaks that assumption, and every method in this lesson keeps extrapolating the old pattern. Backtests over the old period will not warn you. Watch live forecast errors for a run of misses in one direction, shorten the training window or add a step feature after a confirmed break, and widen the stated uncertainty until enough new data has accumulated.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The quantity is recorded at regular intervals and you have at least two full seasonal cycles of history, so that trend and seasonality can be told apart.
  • A decision needs a number for a specific future period, such as stock to order, staff to roster, capacity to provision or a budget line.
  • The series is driven by stable habits such as weekly routines, yearly weather or steady growth, so the past is informative about the future.
  • You forecast many related series (products, stores, regions) and have inputs known in advance such as prices, promotions and holidays, which favours one ML model with lag features.

Think twice when

  • Rows are independent cases with no meaningful order, such as customers or houses. Use ordinary regression with random cross-validation.
  • The question is what would happen if you changed something, such as a price or a policy. A forecast extrapolates what usually happens, and measuring the effect of a change needs an experiment.
  • The series behaves like a random walk, as the prices of many traded assets do. The naive forecast is then about as good as anything, and a model that beats it in a backtest is usually overfitted.
  • There is almost no history, as with a new product. Base the estimate on comparable past launches and judgement, and move to a statistical forecast once data has accumulated.

A simpler alternative. The seasonal naive forecast: the next period equals the same period one season ago, or the last value if the series has no seasonality. It needs no fitting, cannot leak and is hard to beat on noisy series. It stops being enough when the series has a clear trend, which it ignores, or when known future events such as promotions and holidays change demand in ways the previous season did not contain.

Key takeaways

  • A time series is described by its trend, its seasonality with a fixed period and a remainder. A forecast extends the first two, and the remainder limits how accurate it can be.
  • The naive and seasonal naive forecasts are the baseline, and a model earns its place only by beating them in a backtest.
  • Exponential smoothing updates level, trend and seasonal estimates with each new observation, while ARIMA differences the series and regresses on its own past values and past errors.
  • Machine learning forecasters learn from a table of lag, rolling, calendar and known-in-advance features, and every feature must be available when the forecast is made for the whole horizon.
  • Never shuffle time-ordered data. Evaluate with splits that train on the past and test on the period after it, repeated over several forecast origins.
  • Choose a method by its average error across the folds, because any single test window can favour the wrong one.

M5 competition: gradient-boosted trees with lag features on Walmart's daily sales

M5 forecasting competition (Walmart sales data)Documented in a paper

The M5 Accuracy competition, held on Kaggle in 2020, asked teams to forecast 28 days of unit sales for 42,840 series built from data made available by Walmart. The organisers' paper describes the data, the benchmark methods and the winning approaches, and the competition's GitHub repository holds the code the winners supplied. [15][17][16]

The problem

A retailer has to forecast unit sales for every product in every store. In the M5 data that means 3,049 products in 10 stores, or 30,490 daily series, many of them intermittent: most days record no sales at all. Forecasts were needed 28 days ahead and were scored at 12 levels of aggregation, from a single product in a single store up to total sales. Prices, promotions and special events also move demand, and a model that looks only at each series' own past cannot use them.

Where this topic fits

Lag-feature models built on gradient-boosted trees are the forecaster. The naive methods and exponential smoothing from this lesson were the organisers' benchmarks. Time-ordered validation over the most recent 28-day windows was how the leading teams chose and tuned their models before submitting.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Lay the history out as one row per product, store and day, with units sold as the target.
  2. Add the columns the model may use: past unit sales, calendar information, special events, prices, promotion indicators, and identifiers for the product, department and store.
  3. Pool many series into each model. The winner trained separate LightGBM models per store, per store and category, and per store and department, so each model learns from thousands of related series.
  4. Cover the 28-day horizon in two ways: a recursive variant that feeds predictions back in as the newest lags, and a non-recursive variant that relies only on values already known for the whole horizon.
  5. Validate in time order on the last four 28-day windows of the available data, and look at both the mean and the spread of the errors.
  6. Average the models with equal weights to get the product-store forecasts, then add those up to obtain the forecasts for the higher levels.

Why this approach fits

A single product-store series carries little signal: mostly zeros, with demand that depends on price, promotions and the calendar. A model fitted to one such series has little to learn from. Pooling thousands of related series gives a tree ensemble enough rows to learn the shared weekly pattern and the effect of events, and trees accept numeric, binary and categorical inputs without preprocessing. The paper calls this cross-learning and reports that essentially all of the top 50 methods used it.

The classical methods remain the yardstick. The organisers' strongest benchmark was exponential smoothing applied to each product-store series and summed upwards, and beating it was rare.

Limitation or tradeoff

The gains were uneven. The paper reports that the top 50 methods improved on the exponential smoothing benchmark by 40% on average at the most aggregated level and by about 3% at the product, product-state and product-store levels, where the series are noisiest. The winning solution averaged 220 models, a cost that has to be justified by the decisions the forecasts feed. Trees also need help with trend, which is why the runner-up scaled its forecasts with multipliers. Validation was a risk in itself: the organisers note that many teams failed to submit the best forecasts they had produced, probably because their validation scores misled them.

Uber Omphalos: sliding and expanding window backtests across cities

UberDocumented in an engineering article

Uber's engineering blog describes Omphalos, an in-house backtesting framework for time series forecasts, in an article from January 2018. A companion article from September 2018 introduces how forecasting is done at the company. [18][19]

The problem

Uber forecasts quantities such as completed trips per city to support marketplace optimisation, cost modelling and hardware capacity planning, and the article speaks of millions of time series across more than 600 cities. Many algorithms, written in R, Python and Go, compete to forecast them. The team found that judging an algorithm from one chronological split into training and validation data was unreliable for a fast-growing business: a model trained with data from New Year's Eve and one trained without it performed very differently.

Where this topic fits

Backtesting is the evaluation layer between candidate models and the decision to use one. Omphalos takes a series, an algorithm and a window configuration, replays history as a sequence of training passes, and returns the forecast and actual values of every pass so that error measures can be compared across algorithms, cities and passes.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Pick the procedure by data frequency. The article favours a sliding window for daily and hourly series, and an expanding window for weekly, monthly or quarterly series that have few points.
  2. Configure the windows. A sliding window needs a training window size, a forecasting window size (the horizon) and the number of steps to slide between passes. An expanding window needs a starting size, an ending size, a forecasting window size and the number of steps added per pass.
  3. In each pass, train on the points inside the training window only and forecast the points of the forecasting window that follows it. Nothing later than the forecast origin reaches the model.
  4. Run the passes concurrently, one job per algorithm and series, because the number of combinations is large.
  5. Collect the forecast and actual values of every pass, compute error measures from them, and compare algorithms by their typical error across cities and passes, with limits on the worst error and on bias.
  6. Set the result beside a naive forecast, which the companion article recommends as the reference point.

Why this approach fits

A growing business changes quickly. The most recent year is not a larger copy of the one before, and a single validation window can land on an unusual event. Many passes spread over the calendar test each algorithm on ordinary weeks and unusual ones alike. A sliding window bounds the training cost of each pass and keeps the model on recent behaviour, which suits long daily and hourly histories, while an expanding window keeps every point when points are scarce. Returning the raw forecasts and actuals of each pass lets one harness serve any algorithm and any error measure.

Limitation or tradeoff

A sliding window forgets. With the 189-day training window of the article's test, a model never sees a full year, so it cannot learn a yearly pattern from that series alone. Backtesting is also expensive: the article estimates 155 hours to run one large batch of forecasts serially, before parallelisation. A volume-weighted error such as wMAPE is dominated by the busiest series, so small cities can be forecast poorly without moving the headline figure. And no backtest can score a model on an event that has no precedent in the history.

What the sources document

  • Uber operates in more than 600 cities, and its aggregated data generate millions of time series. Forecasts of them feed decisions from marketplace optimisation and cost modelling to hardware capacity planning. [18]
  • The article says that splitting a series once into training and validation sets while preserving chronology proved unreliable for Uber, and gives the example that models trained with and without data from New Year's Eve show significant differences in forecasting performance. [18]
  • The sliding window method takes three hyperparameters (training window size, forecasting window size and sliding steps) and the expanding window form takes four (starting window size, ending window size, forecasting window size and expanding steps). Sliding is described as favourable for daily and hourly data, and expanding as more common for weekly, monthly or quarterly series with limited history. [18]

Prophet at Facebook: trend, seasonality and holidays as separately adjustable components

Meta (Facebook)Documented in an engineering article

Prophet is an open-source forecasting library that Facebook released in 2017. The company's research blog post explains why it was built and what it is used for internally, and the project documentation describes its interface and its backtesting tools. [20][22][21][23]

The problem

The blog post describes a gap between demand and supply. The company needed many forecasts for capacity planning and goal setting, fully automatic techniques were brittle and hard to adjust, and analysts who could produce good forecasts by hand were scarce. The series in question were hourly, daily or weekly business metrics with several seasonal patterns at once, holidays on irregular dates, missing values, outliers and occasional changes in trend.

Where this topic fits

Here the decomposition from the start of this lesson is the model. Prophet fits trend, seasonality and holiday effects as separate additive components, each with a setting an analyst can reason about. Evaluation uses what the documentation calls simulated historical forecasts, which is a backtest over a series of cutoff dates.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Put the history in a table with two columns: the timestamp, named ds, and the value, named y.
  2. Fit an additive model: a piecewise linear or logistic growth curve for the trend, with changepoints selected from the data, a yearly seasonal component built from Fourier terms, a weekly component built from dummy variables, and effects for a list of holidays supplied by the user.
  3. Plot the fitted components and judge them against what is known about the business. Adjust how flexible the trend and the seasonality are, set a capacity if growth must level off, and add special dates.
  4. Backtest: choose cutoff dates, fit on the data up to each cutoff, forecast the horizon after it, and compare the forecast with the actual values.
  5. Summarise the errors by distance from the cutoff, and tune settings such as the changepoint and seasonality scales against those errors.
  6. Forecast the future dates and read each predicted value together with its lower and upper bounds.

Why this approach fits

Metrics driven by people have strong day-of-week and time-of-year patterns, and those are the components the model fits. Because each component has its own setting, an analyst who knows the product but not time series theory can repair a poor forecast by changing one of them, which is the analyst-in-the-loop approach the post describes. Fitting curves against time, where a lag model regresses on recent values, also means that missing days do not leave holes in a feature table.

Limitation or tradeoff

Prophet extends the fitted trend and repeats the fitted seasonal shapes. It does not use the most recent values of the series as predictors, so on short horizons, where yesterday strongly predicts today, a lag-based model or ARIMA can do better. The trend after the last changepoint is assumed to continue, which is risky just after a real change of direction. Holidays help only if someone supplies them. And as with any flexible model, loosening the trend setting improves the fit to history and can worsen the forecast, which is why the documentation tunes it by cross-validation.

It names capacity planning and goal setting as activities for which a large organisation like Facebook needs forecasts. [20]
  • Prophet is described as optimised for hourly, daily or weekly observations with at least a few months, preferably a year, of history, strong day-of-week and time-of-year seasonalities, important holidays at irregular intervals, missing observations or large outliers, historical trend changes and trends that saturate. [20]
  • The procedure is an additive regression model with four components: a piecewise linear or logistic growth curve trend with automatically selected changepoints, a yearly seasonal component modelled with Fourier series, a weekly seasonal component using dummy variables, and a user-provided list of important holidays. [20]
  • The diagnostics page describes cross-validation as selecting cutoff points in the history, fitting the model with data only up to each cutoff and comparing the forecast values with the actual values.   • The quick start shows the Python workflow: a dataframe with the columns ds and y, a Prophet object fitted with fit, future dates from make_future_dataframe, and predict, whose output includes yhat, yhat_lower and yhat_upper. [21]

US labour force statistics: ARIMA forecasts inside X-13ARIMA-SEATS seasonal adjustment

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and U.S. Census BureauDocumented in official docs

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes a methodology page on the seasonal adjustment of national labour force series from the Current Population Survey, which uses the Census Bureau's X-13ARIMA-SEATS program. The Census Bureau documents the software and answers common questions about seasonal adjustment. [24][25][26]

The problem

Monthly employment and unemployment figures swing with the calendar every year, and those swings hide the movement analysts want to see. The seasonal part has to be estimated and removed for every published month, including the newest one. The filters that do this are moving averages, which work best with data on both sides of the month being adjusted, and at the end of the series the future side does not exist yet. Unusual months, such as those at the start of the 2020 pandemic, would also distort the estimated seasonal pattern if they were left in.

Where this topic fits

This is the trend, seasonal and remainder decomposition working together with ARIMA, and the forecast is an intermediate product. A regression-plus-ARIMA model cleans the series and extends it past its last observation. The moving-average filters that separate the components are then applied to the extended series. What is published is the series with its seasonal component removed.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Fit a regARIMA model to each series: a regression part for outliers and other special effects, and an ARIMA part for the normal evolution of the series.
  2. Use the regression estimates to adjust the series for those effects before any seasonal estimation.
  3. Use the ARIMA part to extend the adjusted series with forecasts at the end and backcasts at the start.
  4. Apply the X-11 moving-average filters, or the SEATS method, to the extended series to split it into trend-cycle, seasonal and irregular components, in multiplicative or additive mode.
  5. Remove the seasonal component from the original series to obtain the seasonally adjusted figure.
  6. Rerun the program every month as new data arrive, and re-estimate the seasonal factors at the end of each year with another full year of data, which revises recent history.

Why this approach fits

An ARIMA forecast needs nothing but the series itself, which suits a production process that pushes many series through the same program every month. The forecast does not have to be right in detail. It has to be a better stand-in for the missing future months than leaving them out, so that the filters applied to the latest months resemble the ones applied in the middle of the series. Handling outliers in the same model keeps a one-off shock from being mistaken for seasonality.

Limitation or tradeoff

Because the newest adjusted values rest partly on forecasts, they are provisional. When the real months arrive and replace the forecasts, the seasonal factors change, and the BLS page says each year's data are generally revised five times before they are final. A shock with no precedent also strains the model's assumptions. In multiplicative mode the seasonal component scales with the level of the series, so a sudden jump in level distorts it; the page reports that many series were adjusted multiplicatively before April 2020 and were respecified as additive once a significant outlier was detected.

[24][25]

Someone with a column of dates and a column of values, such as monthly sales, wants a forecast for the coming periods together with a range around it, without writing code. Real spreadsheets also have gaps and sometimes two entries for the same date, and the user may not know the length of the seasonal pattern.

Where this topic fits

Exponential smoothing with a trend and a seasonal component is the forecasting engine. Around it the feature adds the practical steps a library user would do by hand: checking that the timeline has a constant step, filling missing points, combining duplicates, detecting the season length, and reporting a confidence interval.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Supply a timeline with a constant step between its points, and the matching values.
  2. Excel completes missing points, up to 30% of the data, with the average of the neighbouring points unless told to treat them as zeros, and aggregates values that share a time stamp.
  3. The season length is detected automatically or set by the user. A setting of 0 switches seasonality off.
  4. The AAA version of the exponential smoothing algorithm is fitted. In the usual naming of these models the three letters stand for additive error, additive trend and additive seasonality, so its smoothing coefficients play the roles of alpha, beta and gamma in the Holt-Winters equations.
  5. Forecasts are produced for the requested future dates, with a confidence interval described as the range in which 95% of future points are expected to fall.
  6. Moving the forecast start to a date before the end of the history makes Excel use only the earlier data, so the forecast can be compared with the values that were held back.

Why this approach fits

Exponential smoothing needs only the series itself and a few smoothing parameters. It is cheap enough to refit whenever the sheet recalculates, and its components match what most business series contain: a level, a trend and one seasonal pattern. That makes it a sensible default for a tool whose users cannot be asked to choose model orders or build features.

The method is fixed. Seasonality is additive, so a series whose swings grow with its level is fitted less well than it would be by a multiplicative model or after a log transform. 4Common mistakes

  1. Splitting the rows at random, or using shuffled K-fold cross-validation, to evaluate a forecaster.

    Why it causes problems
    Training rows from after the test period carry the test targets inside their lag features, and the model also sees the later level and trend. The reported error is lower than anything it will achieve on real future data, so a weak model gets shipped.
    How to recognise it
    The evaluation code calls train_test_split with its default shuffling, or KFold, on rows that have a date. Check whether the latest training date is later than the earliest test date.
    How to fix it
    Split by time. Use TimeSeriesSplit or an explicit list of cutoff dates, train only on rows before each cutoff and test on the horizon after it.

  2. Building rolling or lag features that include the value being predicted.

    Why it causes problems
    A rolling mean computed without a shift contains the target itself. The model learns to recover it by arithmetic and scores almost perfectly in testing, then fails in production, where that value does not exist yet.
    How to recognise it
    A backtest error close to zero, or a model whose most important feature by far is a rolling statistic. Recompute one feature row by hand from data strictly before the row's date and compare.
    How to fix it
    Shift before rolling, as in y.shift(1).rolling(7).mean(), and build every feature in a function that receives only the data up to the forecast origin.

  3. Using lags shorter than the forecast horizon while testing only one step ahead.

    Why it causes problems
    A model with yesterday's value as a feature looks excellent at predicting tomorrow, but the business needs days 1 to 28. Lag 1 is unknown from day 2 onwards, so the tested accuracy describes a forecast nobody will be able to make.
    How to recognise it
    Compare the smallest lag in the feature list with the horizon plus the delay before data arrive. Ask what the feature row for the last day of the horizon would contain on the day the forecast is produced.
    How to fix it
    Use only lags at least as old as the horizon plus any data delay, or forecast recursively and backtest that whole multi-step procedure with test folds as long as the real horizon.

  4. Reporting a model's accuracy without a naive or seasonal naive baseline beside it.

    Why it causes problems
    An MAE of 8 or a MAPE of 12% means nothing alone. Many series are forecast almost as well by repeating last season, and in the M5 competition fewer than half of the teams beat the naive benchmark. Effort and compute go into a model that adds nothing.
    How to recognise it
    The results table has no baseline row, or accuracy is quoted as a single percentage with nothing to compare it with.
    How to fix it
    Run the seasonal naive forecast through the same backtest and report MASE, or the ratio of model error to baseline error, for every model.

  5. Choosing a model from one hold-out window and then tuning against that window repeatedly.

    Why it causes problems
    One window is one sample and may contain a holiday, a slowdown or plain luck. In the worked example the last year alone picks the seasonal naive forecast, while three folds pick Holt-Winters. Repeated tuning on the same window fits the model to that window.
    How to recognise it
    The ranking of methods changes when the test window is moved by one period, or the live error is clearly worse than the validation error.
    How to fix it
    Backtest over several origins that cover different seasons, compare the mean and the spread of the errors, and keep a final period untouched until the method is fixed.

  6. Training on inputs whose future values will not be known, such as the actual weather or the actual price.

    Why it causes problems
    In the history the true value is in the table, so the model leans on it. At forecast time only a guess or nothing is available, and accuracy collapses or the pipeline fails for lack of the column.
    How to recognise it
    For each feature, ask where its value for next month would come from today. Any answer other than a calendar, a plan or a lag that is old enough is a problem.
    How to fix it
    Keep only inputs that are fixed in advance, or train on the forecasts of that input as they stood at each historical origin so that training matches production.
