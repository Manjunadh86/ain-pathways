Hyperparameter Tuning and Model Selection

A hyperparameter is a setting you choose before training, such as the number of neighbours in k-NN or the depth of a tree, that the training algorithm does not learn from the data. Hyperparameter tuning searches for good values by scoring candidates on rows they were not fitted on, and model selection applies the same discipline to choosing between whole candidate models.

1Overview

Why it matters

Most learning algorithms come with settings that change what they can learn: the strength of a regularisation penalty, the depth of a tree, the learning rate of a neural network. The defaults are a reasonable start and rarely the best choice for your data. Picking values by hand and judging them on the test set is how a team ends up with a model that looks excellent in a notebook and disappoints in production.

The less obvious risk is in the search itself. Every extra configuration you try is another chance to find a score that is high by luck. In a simulation by Varma and Simon, the two classes were identical and the true error was 50%, yet a classifier whose parameter had been chosen by cross-validation reported an average error of 37.8% on that same cross-validation. Anyone who trains, compares or signs off models needs three habits: search cheaply (random or Bayesian search instead of ever larger grids), keep an honest score (an untouched test set or nested cross-validation), and give every candidate the same tuning effort.

Key terms

Parameter
A number the training algorithm fits to the data, such as a regression coefficient or a neural-network weight.
Hyperparameter
A setting fixed before training that controls the model or the training process, such as the penalty strength alpha, k in k-NN or the learning rate. Training does not learn it.
Search space
The set of hyperparameter values a search may try, with a range and a scale (linear or logarithmic) for each, and any conditions such as "gamma only exists for the RBF kernel".
Trial
One evaluation in a search. It trains the model with one candidate configuration and records its validation score.
Validation score
The score on rows not used to fit that trial, usually the mean over the folds of a cross-validation.
Grid search
A search that tries every combination of a fixed list of values for each hyperparameter.
Random search
A search that draws each trial's hyperparameter values at random from distributions over the search space.
Bayesian optimisation
A search that fits a model of score against hyperparameters to the trials so far and runs next the point that looks most promising or most uncertain.
Successive halving
A search that starts many candidates on a small budget, keeps the best fraction, and gives the survivors a larger budget, repeating until one remains.
Nested cross-validation
Cross-validation in which each outer training part is tuned by its own inner cross-validation, so the outer test folds play no part in choosing hyperparameters.

2Explanation

Introduction

You already know that a model is judged on rows it has not seen (see train/test splits and cross-validation) and that a model can be too simple or too flexible (see bias, variance and overfitting). This lesson is about the dials that set that balance.

Take k-nearest neighbours. Training stores the rows, and a prediction is a vote among the kkk closest stored rows. Nothing in training decides what kkk should be, and training error cannot decide it either: with k=1k = 1k=1 every training row is its own nearest neighbour, so training accuracy is perfect however noisy the labels are (unless two identical rows carry different labels). A setting like this is a hyperparameter. A parameter, by contrast, is something the training algorithm fits itself, like the coefficients of a regression. The only honest way to choose a hyperparameter is to try values and score each one on rows that were not used for fitting.

That small step hides two problems. The first is cost. The number of combinations grows quickly and each one needs several model fits, so the way you pick the next candidate matters: grid, random and Bayesian search, and early stopping of weak candidates, are different answers. The second is honesty. Once you keep the best of many scores, that score is no longer a fair estimate of future performance, and a comparison of two models is only fair if both were tuned with the same effort.

The lesson takes the pieces in that order: what can be tuned, the search strategies, the optimism of the winning score and how nested cross-validation removes it, and a checklist for comparing candidates. A worked example in plain Python tunes kkk on 60 rows and then measures the optimism over 400 simulated datasets. Five documented uses follow, from Google's internal tuning service to a study of selection bias on synthetic gene-expression data.

How it works

Parameters and hyperparameters

A parameter is a number the training algorithm fits to the data. A hyperparameter is a setting fixed before training that controls the model or the training process. In scikit-learn the hyperparameters are the constructor arguments of an estimator, and get_params() lists them (see the scikit-learn 1.9.1 tuning guide (opens in a new tab)).

The tuning loop

A search treats the validation score as a function to maximise over a search space Λ\LambdaΛ. One trial trains the algorithm AλA_\lambdaAλ​ with hyperparameters λ\lambdaλ on each training fold and scores it on the matching validation fold:

λ∗=arg⁡max⁡λ∈Λ  1K∑i=1Kscore(Aλ(Dtrain(i)), Dvalid(i))\lambda^{*} = \arg\max_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \; \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \text{score}\big(A_\lambda(D^{(i)}_{\text{train}}),\, D^{(i)}_{\text{valid}}\big)λ∗=argλ∈Λmax​K1​i=1∑K​score(Aλ​(Dtrain(i)​),Dvalid(i)​)

The auto-sklearn paper (opens in a new tab) writes this objective as a loss to minimise. The cost is trials times KKK fits. Every trial stays inside the development data, and a test set that no trial touches gives the final number. The scikit-learn guide calls the two parts the development set and the evaluation set, and GridSearchCV (opens in a new tab) refits the best configuration on the whole development set by default (refit=True).

Give each hyperparameter a range and a scale. Rates and penalties span orders of magnitude, so sample them on a logarithmic scale. If the best trials cluster at the edge of a range, widen it, as the Deep Learning Tuning Playbook (opens in a new tab) advises. It also separates scientific hyperparameters (the effect you want to measure) from nuisance ones, which must be optimised over for each candidate, or the comparison is unfair.

Grid search

Grid search tries every combination of a list of values per hyperparameter. With lists of lengths n1,…,ndn_1, \dots, n_dn1​,…,nd​ it needs n1n2⋯ndn_1 n_2 \cdots n_dn1​n2​⋯nd​ trials, so one more hyperparameter multiplies the cost. It is simple and reproducible, which suits one to three hyperparameters and cheap trials.

Random search

Random search draws each trial's values from a distribution per hyperparameter. Its budget (n_iter) does not depend on the number of hyperparameters, and adding one that does not matter does not reduce efficiency (scikit-learn guide (opens in a new tab)). Trials are independent, so all can run at once (SageMaker documentation (opens in a new tab)).

Random search usually beats a grid because few hyperparameters matter for any one dataset. Bergstra and Bengio call this low effective dimensionality. A grid of nine trials over two hyperparameters tests the important one in only three places, while nine random trials test nine different values of it. Their analysis (opens in a new tab) found that different hyperparameters matter on different datasets, so a grid fits new datasets poorly.

If the best 5% of the space is good enough, at least one of nnn independent random trials lands in it with probability 1−0.95n1 - 0.95^{n}1−0.95n, which first passes 95% at n=59n = 59n=59.

from scipy.stats import loguniform
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV, StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_dev, X_test, y_dev, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=0)

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC())  # the scaler is refitted inside every fold
inner = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=1)

# GridSearchCV(pipe, {"svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "svc__gamma": [0.001, 0.01, 0.1]}, cv=inner) is the grid version
rand = RandomizedSearchCV(
    pipe, {"svc__C": loguniform(1e-2, 1e3), "svc__gamma": loguniform(1e-4, 1e0)},
    n_iter=12, cv=inner, random_state=0)

rand.fit(X_dev, y_dev)                      # 12 trials x 5 folds, plus one refit
print(rand.best_params_, rand.best_score_)  # best_score_ is a mean CV score, so it is optimistic
print(rand.score(X_test, y_test))           # refitted best model, scored once on the test set

Bayesian optimisation

When one trial takes minutes or hours, it pays to spend computation on choosing the next one. Bayesian optimisation fits a cheap surrogate model of score against hyperparameters to the trials so far. It then runs the point with the best value of an acquisition function such as expected improvement, which favours points predicted to be good and points that are still uncertain (Snoek et al. (opens in a new tab)). Each result updates the surrogate. Surrogates differ by tool: a Gaussian process in Google Vizier's default algorithm, random forests in auto-sklearn, and a tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) in Optuna's default sampler. The auto-sklearn paper reports that Gaussian processes perform best in low-dimensional problems with numerical hyperparameters, and tree-based models in high-dimensional, structured spaces. Each choice uses earlier results, so trials are less parallel than in random search. The Tuning Playbook prefers quasi-random search while you are still exploring and Bayesian tools once the space has settled.

Early stopping and successive halving

Many models train in steps (epochs, trees, row subsets), and a weak configuration usually shows itself early. Successive halving starts n0n_0n0​ candidates on a small resource r0r_0r0​, keeps the best 1/f1/f1/f of them and gives the survivors fff times more resource (fff is the factor). In round iii scikit-learn uses ri=fir0r_i = f^{i} r_0ri​=fir0​ resources and ni=n0/fin_i = n_0 / f^{i}ni​=n0​/fi candidates (experimental in HalvingGridSearchCV). With 27 candidates, f=3f = 3f=3 and 100 rows to start, the rounds use 27 × 100, 9 × 300, 3 × 900 and 1 × 2,700 rows: 10,800 row-fits against 72,900 for training all 27 on 2,700 rows (our arithmetic). Hyperband, ASHA and Optuna's successive-halving pruner build on this schedule, and the median stopping rule (Vizier, Optuna) is a related idea. The risk is cutting slow starters: a model with a small learning rate can trail early and win late.

Selection bias and nested cross-validation

The best of many noisy scores is biased upward even when every candidate is equally good. Suppose mmm candidates each have true accuracy 0.5 and are scored on nnn validation rows. Each score is binomial, and the expected best is

E[best]=∑k=0nkn (F(k)m−F(k−1)m)\mathbb{E}[\text{best}] = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{k}{n}\,\big(F(k)^{m} - F(k-1)^{m}\big)E[best]=k=0∑n​nk​(F(k)m−F(k−1)m)

where FFF is the binomial cumulative distribution function and F(−1)=0F(-1) = 0F(−1)=0.

Real candidates are correlated, so the inflation is smaller, but its direction is the same. The winner's cross-validated score (best_score_) is therefore not an estimate of future performance (Cawley and Talbot (opens in a new tab) describe the selection bias this causes). The remedy is to repeat the whole search inside each fold of an outer cross-validation.

python
outer = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=2)
nested = cross_val_score(rand, X, y, cv=outer)  # the whole search runs again inside each outer fold
print(nested.mean(), nested.std())

With KoutK_{\text{out}}Kout​ outer folds, KinK_{\text{in}}Kin​ inner folds and mmm candidates, nested cross-validation needs Kout(Kinm+1)K_{\text{out}}(K_{\text{in}} m + 1)Kout​(Kin​m+1) fits, and the model you ship needs one more search of Kinm+1K_{\text{in}} m + 1Kin​m+1 fits. It estimates the performance of the procedure "search, then refit", not of one set of hyperparameters, so different outer folds may choose different values.

Comparing candidate models fairly

  • Use the same outer splits for every candidate, so that differences are paired by fold.
  • Give each candidate the same tuning procedure and budget. A tuned model beating an untuned one measures the tuning, not the model.
  • Include a baseline with no hyperparameters, such as a majority-class or nearest-centroid model.
  • Report the mean and spread of the outer-fold scores and the paired differences. Scores from overlapping splits are correlated, so a plain paired t-test overstates significance. The Nadeau-Bengio correction multiplies the variance of the differences by 1J+ntestntrain\frac{1}{J} + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}J1​+ntrain​ntest​​ for JJJ scores. In scikit-learn's example (opens in a new tab) it turns t = 2.611 (p = 0.005) into t = 0.750 (p = 0.227) for two SVM kernels.
  • Treat differences inside the noise as ties and keep the simpler or cheaper model. scikit-learn's refit-callable example (opens in a new tab) keeps the model with the fewest PCA components within one standard deviation of the best mean score.
  • Fix the metric before the search, from the decision the model supports (classification metrics).

Worked example: Tune k for k-nearest neighbours, then measure how optimistic the winning score is

We tune kkk for a k-nearest-neighbours classifier on a small synthetic problem, using only Python's standard library (Python 3.13 produced every number below) so that no step is hidden. Each row is (x1,x2,label)(x_1, x_2, \text{label})(x1​,x2​,label). Class 0 is a unit-variance Gaussian centred at (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) and class 1 is one centred at (1.5,0)(1.5, 0)(1.5,0). The classes overlap, so no model can be perfect: the best possible accuracy is Φ(0.75)≈0.773\Phi(0.75) \approx 0.773Φ(0.75)≈0.773, where Φ\PhiΦ is the standard normal distribution function. We use 60 rows to build and choose a model, and 2,000 fresh rows that no choice may touch, standing in for future data.

python
import random
import statistics


def make_data(n_per_class, seed):
    """Two overlapping Gaussian classes in 2-D; class 1 is shifted 1.5 units along x."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    rows = [(rng.gauss(1.5 * label, 1.0), rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0), label)
            for label in (0, 1) for _ in range(n_per_class)]
    rng.shuffle(rows)
    return rows


def make_folds(rows, n_folds):
    """Fold f holds the rows whose index is f modulo n_folds. Returns (train, test) pairs."""
    return [([r for i, r in enumerate(rows) if i % n_folds != f],
             [r for i, r in enumerate(rows) if i % n_folds == f]) for f in range(n_folds)]


def knn_accuracy(train, test, k):
    def predict(p):
        nearest = sorted(train, key=lambda r: (r[0] - p[0]) ** 2 + (r[1] - p[1]) ** 2)[:k]
        return int(2 * sum(r[2] for r in nearest) > k)
    return statistics.mean(predict(r) == r[2] for r in test)


def centroid_accuracy(train, test):
    """Baseline with no hyperparameter: assign each point to the nearer class mean."""
    centre = {c: (statistics.mean(r[0] for r in train if r[2] == c),
                  statistics.mean(r[1] for r in train if r[2] == c)) for c in (0, 1)}

    def predict(p):
        return min((0, 1), key=lambda c: (p[0] - centre[c][0]) ** 2 + (p[1] - centre[c][1]) ** 2)
    return statistics.mean(predict(r) == r[2] for r in test)


GRID = range(1, 30, 2)  # k = 1, 3, ..., 29


def tune_k(rows, n_folds, grid=GRID):
    """Grid search: mean CV accuracy for every k, and the best k (the smallest on ties)."""
    folds = make_folds(rows, n_folds)
    scores = {k: statistics.mean(knn_accuracy(tr, te, k) for tr, te in folds) for k in grid}
    return max(scores, key=scores.get), scores


def nested_cv(rows, outer=5, inner=4):
    """The outer loop scores the whole procedure 'tune k, then fit'; the inner loop does the tuning."""
    results = []
    for tr, te in make_folds(rows, outer):
        k, _ = tune_k(tr, inner)
        results.append((k, knn_accuracy(tr, te, k), centroid_accuracy(tr, te)))
    return results
python
data = make_data(30, seed=7)
best_k, scores = tune_k(data, n_folds=5)
print(" ".join(f"{k}:{scores[k]:.3f}" for k in GRID))
print(f"best k = {best_k}, CV accuracy {scores[best_k]:.3f}")

outer = nested_cv(data)
for i, (k, tuned, base) in enumerate(outer, start=1):
    print(f"outer fold {i}: inner search chose k={k:2d}  tuned {tuned:.3f}  centroid {base:.3f}")
print(f"nested CV: tuned {statistics.mean(o[1] for o in outer):.3f}, "
      f"centroid {statistics.mean(o[2] for o in outer):.3f}")

fresh = make_data(1000, seed=999)  # 2,000 rows that played no part in any choice
print(f"k={best_k} fitted on all 60 rows, scored on fresh rows: {knn_accuracy(data, fresh, best_k):.3f}; "
      f"centroid: {centroid_accuracy(data, fresh):.3f}")

Now repeat everything on 400 datasets (seeds 0 to 399), each with its own 1,000 fresh rows:

python
import math


def one_dataset(seed):
    data, fresh = make_data(30, seed), make_data(500, 10_000 + seed)
    k, scores = tune_k(data, 5)
    nested = statistics.mean(acc for _, acc, _ in nested_cv(data))
    return scores[k], knn_accuracy(data, fresh, k), nested, centroid_accuracy(data, fresh)


runs = [one_dataset(seed) for seed in range(400)]  # about 25 seconds
for i, name in enumerate(["best CV score", "same model, fresh rows", "nested CV estimate", "centroid, fresh rows"]):
    print(f"{name:24s} {statistics.mean(r[i] for r in runs):.3f}")
gap = [r[0] - r[1] for r in runs]
print(f"optimism {statistics.mean(gap):+.3f} (standard error {statistics.stdev(gap) / math.sqrt(len(gap)):.3f}); "
      f"best CV score above fresh accuracy in {sum(g > 0 for g in gap)} of {len(gap)} datasets")
gap = [r[2] - r[1] for r in runs]
print(f"nested minus fresh {statistics.mean(gap):+.3f} (standard error {statistics.stdev(gap) / math.sqrt(len(gap)):.3f})")
print(f"tuned k-NN beats the centroid baseline in {sum(r[1] > r[3] for r in runs)} of {len(runs)} datasets")

Edge cases and limitations

The best value sits at the edge of the search range
If the winning value is the smallest or largest one you tried, the real optimum probably lies outside the range, and the search has only told you which way to move. Widen that side and rerun. The Tuning Playbook gives the same rule: when the best points cluster at a boundary, expand the boundary until the best observed point is no longer close to it. Plot score against each hyperparameter to see it.
Scores inside the noise make the winner a coin toss
In the worked example k = 7 beat k = 9, 11 and 13 by a single correct row out of 60. Differences smaller than the fold-to-fold spread do not identify a winner, and rerunning with another split may reorder them. Prefer the simpler or cheaper configuration inside one standard error, and break exact ties by an order you chose on purpose (the worked code takes the smaller k), because a library may break them differently.
Hyperparameters interact and depend on the amount of data
A lower learning rate usually needs more trees or epochs, so tuning one at a time with the others frozen can stop at a poor corner. A value chosen on a subsample, or from a short run cut off by early stopping, can shift when the model trains on all rows for longer. Search related hyperparameters together, and recheck the winner with a full-size run before trusting it.
Preprocessing fitted outside the search leaks validation rows
If a scaler, imputer or feature selector is fitted on all rows before cross-validation, every validation fold has already influenced the model, and each trial's score is optimistic before selection adds its own bias. Put every data-dependent step in a pipeline and search it as `step__parameter`, as the scikit-learn guide shows, so that each fold refits it on its own training rows.
The splitter must match the data in both loops
Random folds on time-ordered data let the future help tune the past, and random folds on repeated customers or patients put the same entity on both sides of a split. Use the time-aware or grouped splitter in the inner and the outer loop, and stratify when classes are rare (see [train/test splits and cross-validation](/topics/train-test-split-and-cross-validation/) and [handling imbalanced data](/topics/imbalanced-data/)).

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • A model has settings that change its flexibility or its training noticeably, such as a regularisation strength, a tree depth or a learning rate, and the defaults score poorly on validation data.
  • You must choose between several model families or pipelines and want one defensible number per candidate.
  • A reported score will drive a go or no-go decision, so it must not be inflated by the search that produced it. Use a test set that no trial touched, or nested cross-validation when data is scarce.

Think twice when

  • The data is so small, or the candidate list so long, that the differences between candidates are smaller than the fold-to-fold noise. Shrink the search or accept a tie.
  • A single training run takes days and you cannot afford several folds of many trials. Use published defaults, a cheap proxy task or successive halving instead of a full search.
  • The model is wrong in a way tuning cannot fix, such as leaked features or the wrong target. Fix the data first.

A simpler alternative. Start from the library defaults and tune one or two hyperparameters on a coarse logarithmic grid with five-fold cross-validation. Move to random or Bayesian search when there are four or more hyperparameters or each trial takes minutes, and use nested cross-validation only when the data is too small to spare a test set.

Key takeaways

  • Parameters are fitted by the training algorithm, hyperparameters are chosen by you, and they must be chosen with scores on rows the model was not fitted on, because training error always favours the more flexible setting.
  • For the same budget random search tries a new value of every hyperparameter on every trial, so it beats a grid when only a few hyperparameters matter, and Bayesian optimisation or successive halving help when trials are expensive.
  • The best of many validation scores is inflated by selection alone; in the worked example the winning cross-validated accuracy averaged 0.778 while the same models scored 0.744 on fresh rows.
  • Nested cross-validation repeats the whole search inside each outer fold and so scores the procedure honestly, at a cost of about outer folds times inner folds times candidates fits, and the model you ship comes from one final search on all the data.
  • Compare candidates with the same splits and the same tuning effort, include a baseline with no tuning, report the spread as well as the mean, and treat differences inside the noise as ties.

Example 1 of 5

Google Vizier: Bayesian search and early stopping as an internal tuning service

GoogleDocumented in a paper

Google Vizier is the Google-internal black-box optimisation service described in the 2017 KDD paper by Golovin and colleagues. It tunes the hyperparameters of many of Google's machine-learning models and supplies core capabilities to what the paper calls Google's Cloud Machine Learning HyperTune subsystem. [10]

The problem

Teams across Google train models whose learning rates, layer counts and similar settings must be tuned, and one trial is a full training run. They need a shared service that suggests the next configuration, copes with workers that crash or are preempted, stops hopeless runs early, and works from small studies up to studies with millions of trials.

Where this topic fits

Vizier wraps the training job. The training code is the objective function: it receives hyperparameters from the service, trains and evaluates, and reports a metric back. The search strategy, the stored history of trials and the early-stopping decision all live in the service, not in each team's scripts.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A developer writes a study configuration: the parameters with their types and feasible ranges, and whether to maximise or minimise the objective metric.
  2. Worker processes loop. Each asks the service for a suggested trial, trains and evaluates a model with those hyperparameters, and reports the result.
  3. For studies under a thousand trials the suggestion service defaults to batched Gaussian-process bandits, which model the score against the hyperparameters and choose points by expected improvement. Random search and grid search are also supported.
  4. While a trial trains it can report intermediate values such as accuracy per epoch. An automated stopping service, using a median rule or a performance-curve rule, can tell the worker to stop the trial early.
  5. Finished studies can seed new ones, because transfer learning uses the data of earlier studies as a prior.
  6. The best trial's hyperparameters configure the model that is kept.

Why this approach fits

Validation score as a function of hyperparameters is a black box: there is no gradient, and each evaluation costs a training run. A model-based sequential search spends those runs where the surrogate expects gains, and early stopping stops paying for runs that are clearly behind. Keeping the study state in a service means a preempted worker loses one trial, not the whole search, and many teams share one implementation.

Limitation or tradeoff

The paper notes that naive Gaussian-process implementations scale as the cube of the number of training points, so with many completed trials a more scalable algorithm may be preferable, and for studies of tens of thousands of trials it recommends a proprietary local-search algorithm. Early stopping only works when partial results predict final ones. The paper is a 2017 description of an internal system, so details such as the default algorithm and the product names may have changed. Read a current product's documentation for what it does today.

What the sources document

  • The paper describes Vizier as a Google-internal black-box optimisation service that has become the de facto parameter-tuning engine at Google, used to optimise many of Google's machine-learning models and other systems, and providing core capabilities to Google Cloud's HyperTune subsystem. [10]
  • For studies with fewer than a thousand trials Vizier defaults to batched Gaussian-process bandits with a Matérn kernel and the expected-improvement acquisition function. Random search and grid search are supported, and for tens of thousands of trials or more the authors recommend a proprietary local-search algorithm. The paper also remarks that naive Gaussian-process implementations scale as O(n^3) in the number of training points. [10]
  • Two automated stopping rules are described. In the authors' experiments the performance-curve rule gave comparable optimality gaps with about 50% fewer CPU-hours, and the median rule gave a two- to three-fold speedup over random search while still finding the best trial. [10]
  • Vizier is used across Google for research and production models. The authors cite one research project whose tuning studies together held millions of trials, and say Vizier measurably improved production models behind many Google products. [10]
  • Vizier supports transfer learning, in which data from earlier studies guides a new one, for example tuning a system on one dataset and using that study as a prior when tuning the same system on another dataset. [10]

Example 2 of 5

Random versus grid search for neural networks (Bergstra and Bengio, JMLR 2012)

Documented in a paper

Bergstra and Bengio's 2012 paper in the Journal of Machine Learning Research compares random search with the grid and manual search of an earlier large study (Larochelle et al., 2007) that configured neural networks and deep belief networks on image-classification datasets. [9]

The problem

A single-hidden-layer network has seven to nine hyperparameters to set: learning rate, hidden units, weight initialisation, regularisation and optional input preprocessing. The earlier study configured it with a grid of about 100 trials on average. The question is whether sampling configurations at random can match that grid with the same budget or less.

Where this topic fits

The search sits between the model definition and the final test evaluation. Each trial trains one network, validation scores identify the best trial of an experiment, and the test set scores it. The paper's estimate also averages over trials whose validation scores leave doubt about which is best. Random trials are independent, so a 256-trial run can be cut into many smaller experiments of 1, 2, 4 and more trials to show how the result grows with the budget.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Define a distribution for each hyperparameter, for example a learning rate drawn log-uniformly from 0.001 to 10, hidden units drawn log-uniformly from 18 to 1024 and rounded, and sigmoid or tanh with equal probability.
  2. For each dataset, draw 256 independent trials from these distributions and train one network per trial.
  3. Treat the 256 trials as many smaller experiments, for example 32 experiments of 8 trials. In each, use the validation scores to find the best trial and record its test accuracy.
  4. Compare the resulting accuracy with the grid-search result of the earlier study on the same datasets.
  5. Fit a Gaussian process to validation score as a function of the hyperparameters, to see which hyperparameters matter on each dataset.

Why this approach fits

If the score depends mainly on a few hyperparameters, a grid wastes most of its trials repeating the same few values of the ones that matter. Random sampling gives every trial a fresh value of every hyperparameter, and because it has no fixed shape it can absorb extra options such as preprocessing without multiplying the number of trials.

Limitation or tradeoff

The advantage rests on few hyperparameters mattering. The paper shows datasets where even 16 or 32 random trials varied considerably in the quality of the best model, which points to a narrow region of good settings that random draws find only with many trials. Random search also ignores what earlier trials showed, so the authors present it as a baseline that adaptive methods have to beat.

What the sources document

  • Compared with neural networks configured by a pure grid search in a large earlier study, random search over the same domain found models as good or better within a small fraction of the computation time. [9]
  • For deep belief networks, purely random search over the same 32-dimensional space found statistically equal performance to a thoughtful combination of manual and grid search on four of seven datasets, and superior performance on one of seven. [9]
  • A Gaussian-process analysis of validation performance found that for most datasets only a few hyperparameters really matter, but that different hyperparameters matter on different datasets, which makes grid search a poor choice for new datasets. [9]
  • Restricted to trials with no preprocessing, 8 random trials already beat the earlier grid search. In the larger space that includes preprocessing, 32 trials were needed to outperform it consistently, and 64 or more found superior results. [9]
  • Figure 1 of the paper illustrates that with nine trials a grid tests an important hyperparameter in only three distinct places, while nine random trials test nine distinct values of it. [9]

Example 3 of 5

Optuna: define-by-run search spaces and pruning, from object detection to RocksDB

Optuna (Preferred Networks)Documented in a paper

The Optuna paper (KDD 2019) describes the open-source Optuna framework, its pruning of unpromising trials and several real uses, including Preferred Networks' detector for the Open Images Object Detection Track 2018. The Optuna documentation describes the same sampler and pruner interface. [11][7]

The problem

Each trial trains a model for a long time, most sampled configurations are poor, and the set of hyperparameters can depend on earlier choices: the number of layers decides how many unit counts exist. A fixed list of values and a full training run for every trial would waste most of the compute budget.

Where this topic fits

Optuna wraps the training function. A sampler proposes values as the function asks for them, the function reports an intermediate validation value after each training step, and a pruner compares it with other trials at the same step and stops the weak ones.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Write an objective function that asks for each hyperparameter with a `trial.suggest_*` call while it builds the model, so a loop over layers can request a different number of unit counts in each trial. This is the define-by-run style.
  2. A sampler proposes each value. The documentation names TPE as the default sampler; it uses the history of finished trials to choose new values.
  3. During training the function calls `trial.report` with an intermediate validation value and then `trial.should_prune()`.
  4. The pruner lets a trial continue only while its provisional ranking is within the top 1/η at that step. The paper's pruner is a variant of asynchronous successive halving.
  5. When `should_prune()` returns true, the objective raises `TrialPruned` and the trial ends. The freed worker starts a new trial, so many more configurations are explored in the same time.
  6. After the study, the best trial's parameters configure the final model.

Why this approach fits

An expensive objective that reveals progress while it runs is the case pruning is built for: most of the budget goes to the few configurations that stay near the top. Define-by-run lets the search space follow the code, which matters when some hyperparameters exist only for some choices.

Limitation or tradeoff

Pruning assumes that early performance predicts final performance, so a slow starter can be cut. The trial counts in the paper's benchmark come from one model, one dataset and one 44-hour limit and are not a promise for other problems. The documentation also reports that its median pruner is outperformed by the successive-halving and Hyperband pruners on its benchmark.

What the sources document

  • The define-by-run API lets the user construct the search space dynamically while the objective function runs, so the number of hyperparameters can depend on earlier suggestions. [11]
  • The paper's pruner is a variant of asynchronous successive halving, in which a trial enters the next round only if its provisional ranking is within the top 1/η. The documentation says pruning is switched on by calling `report()` and `should_prune()` after each step of iterative training (its example raises `optuna.TrialPruned` when `should_prune()` returns true), and states that the default sampler is TPESampler. [11][7]
  • On a simplified AlexNet with 8 hyperparameters trained on the SVHN dataset, with each study stopped after 44 hours, TPE without pruning completed 35.8 trials per study on average, while TPE with pruning explored 1,278.6 trials on average, of which 1,271.5 were pruned. [11]
  • The paper states that Optuna was a key player in developing Preferred Networks' Faster R-CNN models for the Google AI Open Images Object Detection Track 2018, where the final model won second place. [11]
  • The paper reports tuning 34 RocksDB parameters out of over a hundred. The default setting took 372 seconds on an HDD for the benchmark's operations, and with pruning Optuna found a setting that took 30 seconds, exploring 937 parameter sets in 4 hours against 39 without pruning. [11]

Example 4 of 5

Nested cross-validation exposes selection bias on data with no signal (Varma and Simon, 2006)

Documented in a paper

Varma and Simon's 2006 paper in BMC Bioinformatics measures how biased a cross-validation error estimate is when the same cross-validation also tuned the classifier, and how much nested cross-validation reduces the bias. The scikit-learn documentation repeats the comparison on the Iris data. [12][3]

The problem

Gene-expression studies have a few dozen samples and thousands of features. Classifiers such as shrunken centroids and support vector machines have a parameter chosen by cross-validation, and the lowest cross-validated error is often reported as the classifier's error. If the classes carry no real difference, how wrong is that figure?

Where this topic fits

This is the evaluation protocol around the tuning step. Choosing the classifier's parameter must be repeated inside every loop of the cross-validation that estimates the error, not done once before it.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Generate null data in which no feature separates the classes: at least 1,000 datasets of 40 samples (20 per class) with 6,000 synthetic features, so every classifier's true error is 50%.
  2. Tune each classifier's parameter by cross-validation (10-fold for shrunken centroids, leave-one-out for the SVM) and take the lowest cross-validated error as the error estimate.
  3. Compare that estimate with the true error of 50%.
  4. Repeat with nested cross-validation. Leave one sample out, choose the parameter on the other 39 samples by 10-fold cross-validation, classify the left-out sample, and average over all 40.
  5. Compare the nested estimate with the true error.

Why this approach fits

Each outer iteration plays out what happens in practice: tune on the data you have, then meet a sample that the tuning never saw. Nothing about the left-out sample can reach the choice of parameter, so the estimate does not inherit the selection effect.

Limitation or tradeoff

The simulation is deliberately extreme, with tiny samples, thousands of features and no signal, so the bias is large. The scikit-learn Iris example sits at the other end: six candidates and a clean dataset give a difference under one percentage point. The size of the bias depends on the number of candidates, the noise in the scores and the amount of data. Nested cross-validation also multiplies the number of fits.

What the sources document

  • In the paper's null data the population mean of every gene is zero in both classes, so no feature truly separates them. Each simulation used at least 1,000 datasets of 40 samples (20 per class), each sample a vector of 6,000 synthetic features. [12]
  • On this data, the cross-validation error estimate of the optimised classifier averaged 37.8% for shrunken centroids (10-fold) and 41.7% for the SVM (leave-one-out), against a true error of 50%. [12]
  • The mean nested cross-validation error estimate for shrunken centroids was 54.2% against a true error of 50.0%, a slight overestimate. [12]
  • The authors conclude that proper use of cross-validation requires every step of the algorithm, including classifier parameter tuning, to be repeated in each cross-validation loop. [12]
  • scikit-learn's example tunes an RBF SVC over C in {1, 10, 100} and gamma in {0.01, 0.1} on the Iris data, with 4-fold inner and outer loops over 30 trials, and reports non-nested scores higher than nested ones by an average of 0.007581 (standard deviation 0.007833). [3]

Example 5 of 5

auto-sklearn: choosing the algorithm and its hyperparameters in one search

Documented in a paper

The NIPS 2015 paper by Feurer and colleagues describes auto-sklearn, a system that chooses a scikit-learn preprocessing pipeline, a classifier and all hyperparameters for a new dataset within a fixed time budget. It treats the choice of algorithm as part of the hyperparameter search. [13]

The problem

No single algorithm is best on every dataset, and some, such as non-linear SVMs, depend heavily on hyperparameter tuning, so choosing a model and tuning it are one problem. Without an expert in the loop, the system must produce predictions for a new dataset within a fixed computational budget.

Where this topic fits

Model selection becomes the top of the search space. The classifier is a categorical hyperparameter whose children are that algorithm's own hyperparameters. Bayesian optimisation searches this joint space, each configuration is scored by cross-validation, and every model evaluated feeds a final ensemble.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Define a structured space from scikit-learn components: 4 data preprocessing methods, 14 feature preprocessing methods and 15 classifiers, with 110 hyperparameters, most of them active only when their component is chosen.
  2. Warm start the search. Offline, compute 38 meta-features for 140 OpenML datasets and store the best configuration found for each. For a new dataset, evaluate the stored configurations of the 25 nearest datasets first.
  3. Run SMAC, a Bayesian optimiser with a random-forest surrogate, scoring each configuration by cross-validation one fold at a time so that poor settings can be dropped early.
  4. Keep every model evaluated during the search and combine up to 50 of them into an ensemble by ensemble selection, instead of keeping only the single best.
  5. Measure on held-out test data with a limit of one hour of CPU time per run, using leave-one-dataset-out validation for the meta-learning.

Why this approach fits

When the question is "which model?" as much as "which settings?", comparing families at their defaults is unfair to the families that need tuning. One search over the joint space gives each family its own tuned best inside a single budget, and the same cross-validated score judges all of them.

Limitation or tradeoff

The search is only as good as its budget and its validation protocol. The authors report that numerical weight optimisation and stacking overfit the validation set, the same selection effect this lesson describes, which is why they chose ensemble selection. An ensemble of up to 50 models is harder to explain and slower to run than one model, and the results hold for the paper's benchmark datasets and component list. The counts above describe the 2015 paper's system, not necessarily the current auto-sklearn library.

What the sources document

  • auto-sklearn uses 15 classifiers, 14 feature preprocessing methods and 4 data preprocessing methods, giving a structured space of 110 hyperparameters, most of which are conditional on the component being selected. [13]
  • The paper formalises AutoML as the combined algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation (CASH) problem and solves it with SMAC, a Bayesian optimiser using random forests that can evaluate cross-validation folds one at a time and discard poor settings early. [13]
  • Meta-learning over 140 OpenML datasets with 38 meta-features warm-starts the search with the stored configurations of the 25 nearest datasets in meta-feature space. [13]
  • Models evaluated during the search are combined by ensemble selection with an ensemble of size 50. The authors found numerical weight optimisation and stacking overfit the validation set, while ensemble selection was fast and robust. [13]
  • The system won the first phase of the ChaLearn AutoML challenge, and in experiments on over 100 diverse datasets, with a one-hour CPU limit per run, it substantially outperformed the previous state of the art in AutoML. [13]

4Common mistakes

  1. Reporting the best cross-validation score of the search as the model's expected performance.

    Why it causes problems
    The winner was chosen because its score was high, partly by luck, so the number is biased upward. In the worked example the winning 5-fold score averaged 0.778 against 0.744 on fresh rows, and in Varma and Simon's null data a classifier tuned by cross-validation reported 37.8% error when the true error was 50%.
    How to recognise it
    The search's best score is higher than the score on a test set that played no part in the search, in most repeats. The gap grows with the number of candidates and shrinks with more data.
    How to fix it
    Keep a test set that no trial touches and score the refitted winner on it once, or use nested cross-validation when the data is too scarce to hold one out.

  2. Tuning against the test set, or looking at it between rounds of tuning.

    Why it causes problems
    Each look at the test score that changes a decision turns the test set into a second validation set. After several rounds its score is optimistic in the same way as a validation score, and nothing is left to catch it.
    How to recognise it
    The test score is used to choose between configurations, someone says "one more try" after a disappointing test result, or the test rows are part of the data passed to the search.
    How to fix it
    Pass only the development data to the search, record the test score once for the final candidate, and if you must iterate again, collect or hold out a fresh test set.

  3. Fitting a scaler, imputer or feature selector on all rows before the search.

    Why it causes problems
    The validation rows have already shaped the preprocessing, so every trial's score is optimistic before selection adds its own bias. With many features and few rows, selecting features on all rows can make a useless model look good.
    How to recognise it
    Preprocessing code runs before the train/test split or before the cross-validation call, and scores drop when the same steps are moved inside the pipeline.
    How to fix it
    Put every step that learns from data in a pipeline, tune its settings as step__parameter, and let each fold refit the whole pipeline on its own training rows.

  4. Comparing a tuned model with an untuned one, or giving candidates different budgets.

    Why it causes problems
    The difference then measures the tuning effort, not the model family. A boosted model tuned over 60 trials beats a logistic regression left at its defaults, and the team picks the wrong model.
    How to recognise it
    One candidate has a search and the other has default settings, the numbers of trials or folds differ, or the candidates were scored on different splits.
    How to fix it
    Give every candidate the same search procedure and a stated budget, score all of them on the same outer folds, and include a baseline with no tuning.

  5. Treating a one-row lead as a finding.

    Why it causes problems
    When scores differ by less than their fold-to-fold spread, the winner changes with the split. The choice then carries no information, and a costlier configuration is adopted for nothing.
    How to recognise it
    Several configurations have means within one standard deviation of each other. In the worked example k = 7 led k = 9, 11 and 13 by one prediction out of 60, and the five outer folds chose k = 1, 7, 7, 5 and 5.
    How to fix it
    Report the spread, look at the whole score curve, and choose the simplest or cheapest configuration within one standard error of the best.

  6. Searching a range that is too narrow, or a rate on a linear scale.

    Why it causes problems
    If the best value sits at the edge, the true optimum was never tried. On a linear scale from 0 to 0.1, nine in ten draws of a learning rate fall between 0.01 and 0.1, and only one draw in a hundred falls below 0.001.
    How to recognise it
    The best configuration has a hyperparameter at the smallest or largest value tried, and a plot of score against that hyperparameter is still rising at the edge.
    How to fix it
    Sample rates and penalties on a logarithmic scale, widen any range whose edge wins, and rerun the search.
