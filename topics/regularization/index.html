Regularization (Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net)

Regularization adds a penalty on the size of a model's coefficients to the loss it minimises, so the fit gives up a little training accuracy for coefficients that are smaller and steadier. Ridge penalises squared coefficients, lasso penalises absolute values and can set some coefficients exactly to zero, and elastic net mixes the two.

1Overview

Why it matters

A linear or logistic model with many columns will use every one of them to chase the training data. With one-hot encoded categories, polynomial terms, text features, or simply two columns that measure nearly the same thing, least squares can produce coefficients that are huge, opposite in sign and different every time the data are resampled. Training error looks excellent and held-out error does not. Regularization is the standard fix, and it is cheap: one extra term in the loss and one number to tune.

It is also already switched on in tools you may be using without having chosen it. scikit-learn's LogisticRegression applies a penalty by default, XGBoost penalises leaf weights by default, and forecasting and marketing-mix libraries build it into their models. Anyone who fits, tunes or reviews such a model needs to know three things: what the penalty does to coefficients, why its effect depends on the units of the features, and how to choose its strength without looking at the test set. Most real mistakes with regularization are made in one of those three places.

Key terms

Regularization
Changing the training objective so that it prefers simpler models, most often by adding a penalty on coefficient size to the loss.
Penalty term
The amount added to the loss for the coefficients themselves. It is zero when every coefficient is zero and grows as they move away from zero.
L2 penalty (ridge)
The sum of the squared coefficients. It shrinks every coefficient towards zero but does not make any of them exactly zero.
L1 penalty (lasso)
The sum of the absolute values of the coefficients. It can set coefficients exactly to zero, which removes those features from the model.
Elastic net
A weighted mix of the L1 and L2 penalties, with one setting for the overall strength and one for the mix.
Regularization strength
The number that multiplies the penalty, written as lambda in formulas and called alpha in scikit-learn and lambda in glmnet. Zero means no penalty.
Shrinkage
Pulling coefficient estimates towards zero, which accepts some bias in exchange for less variance.
Sparse model
A model in which most coefficients are exactly zero, so that only a few features are used to make a prediction.
Standardisation
Rescaling a feature by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, so that every feature has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
Regularization path
The sequence of fitted coefficients obtained as the strength is moved from very weak to very strong.

2Explanation

Introduction

In linear regression the fit has one goal: make the squared errors on the training rows as small as possible. Nothing in that goal says the coefficients should be sensible. When two features carry nearly the same information, or when there are many features and few rows, least squares will put a large positive coefficient on one column and a large negative one on another if the combination removes a little more training error. That is the overfitting you have already met, showing up in the coefficients.

Here is a preview of this lesson's worked example. Twelve students, four features: hours studied, pages read (which tracks hours almost exactly), hours of sleep and shoe size. Least squares on standardised features gives hours a coefficient of +22.95 and pages a coefficient of −10.40. Read literally, reading more pages lowers your score. The two numbers are an accident of twelve rows.

Regularization changes the goal. Instead of the smallest error, the model must minimise

training error+λ×size of the coefficients\text{training error} + \lambda \times \text{size of the coefficients}training error+λ×size of the coefficients

Think of it as a price list. Every unit of coefficient costs something, so the model only buys coefficient where it pays for itself in reduced error. A pair of large cancelling coefficients is expensive and buys almost nothing, so it disappears. The number λ\lambdaλ (lambda) is the regularization strength, the price per unit. At λ=0\lambda = 0λ=0 you have ordinary least squares back. As λ\lambdaλ grows, coefficients are pushed towards zero, and in the limit the model predicts the mean of the target for every row.

There are three common ways to measure "size", and they behave differently:

In bias-variance terms, the penalty adds a little bias, because the coefficients are deliberately too small, and removes variance, because they stop swinging from sample to sample. The right strength is the one with the lowest held-out error, and it has to be found from data.

The rest of the lesson covers how each penalty changes a coefficient, why features must be put on a common scale before the penalty is fair, and how to choose λ\lambdaλ with cross-validation. The same loss-plus-penalty objective is what gradient descent minimises when a neural network is trained with an L2 penalty, which plain gradient descent carries out as the weight decay described below.

Ridge: shrink every coefficient in proportion

To see what a penalty does, take one standardised feature (mean 0, standard deviation 1) that is uncorrelated with the others, and call its least-squares coefficient ρ\rhoρ. Minimising the objective with α=0\alpha = 0α=0 gives

wridge=ρ1+λw^{\text{ridge}} = \frac{\rho}{1 + \lambda}wridge=1+λρ​

Every coefficient is divided by the same factor. Large ones stay large relative to small ones, and none becomes exactly zero. In gradient descent terms the penalty adds λw\lambda wλw to the gradient, so each update first multiplies the weight by (1−ηλ)(1 - \eta\lambda)(1−ηλ), where η\etaη is the learning rate, and then takes the usual step. That is why the same idea is called weight decay in neural network training.

Ridge is at its best with correlated features. Squares make it cheaper to split a weight than to concentrate it: two coefficients of 5 cost 25+25=5025 + 25 = 5025+25=50, while one coefficient of 10 costs 100. So ridge spreads weight across near-duplicate columns and removes large cancelling pairs. The scikit-learn guide (opens in a new tab) notes that stronger shrinkage makes the coefficients less sensitive to collinearity.

Lasso: subtract a fixed amount and stop at zero

With α=1\alpha = 1α=1 the same single-feature calculation gives the soft-thresholding rule:

wlasso=sign⁡(ρ) max⁡(∣ρ∣−λ, 0)w^{\text{lasso}} = \operatorname{sign}(\rho)\,\max(\lvert\rho\rvert - \lambda,\ 0)wlasso=sign(ρ)max(∣ρ∣−λ, 0)

Every coefficient moves towards zero by the same amount λ\lambdaλ, and any coefficient whose least-squares value is smaller than λ\lambdaλ in size lands exactly on zero. That feature is out of the model. The cause is the shape of the penalty: the pull of ∣w∣\lvert w \rvert∣w∣ towards zero is the constant λ\lambdaλ however small www already is, while ridge's pull λw\lambda wλw fades as www shrinks. The result is a sparse model, in which choosing features is part of fitting.

Two limits come with that. Absolute values give no discount for sharing (5+55 + 55+5 costs the same as 10), so from a group of strongly correlated features the lasso tends to keep one and drop the rest, and which one it keeps can change with a small change in the data. And with more columns than rows it can keep at most nnn features. Both points are made in the paper that introduced the elastic net.

Because ∣w∣\lvert w \rvert∣w∣ has a corner at zero, plain gradient steps hover around zero without landing on it. scikit-learn and glmnet use coordinate descent instead: update one coefficient at a time with the soft-thresholding rule, and cycle through the features until nothing changes.

Elastic net: threshold, then shrink

With a mix 0<α<10 < \alpha < 10<α<1 the single-feature solution combines both steps:

The L1 part still zeroes weak features. The L2 part restores the discount for sharing, so correlated features tend to enter or leave the model together. The price is a second setting to tune.

Why features must be scaled first

The penalty charges for the size of the number wjw_jwj​, and that number depends on the feature's units. Measure a distance in metres instead of kilometres and its coefficient becomes 1,000 times smaller for the same predictions, so the penalty barely touches it. Measure it in hundreds of kilometres and the coefficient becomes 100 times larger and may be removed. An unscaled penalised model therefore depends on arbitrary unit choices. In the worked example, a lasso keeps a different feature when one column is converted from pages to hundreds of pages.

The fix is to standardise each feature before fitting: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation, z=(x−xˉ)/sz = (x - \bar{x})/sz=(x−xˉ)/s. Each coefficient then means "change in the prediction per one standard deviation of this feature", and a single λ\lambdaλ is equally demanding of all of them. The single-feature formulas above assume this scaling. The scikit-learn preprocessing guide (opens in a new tab) names L1 and L2 regularizers among the methods that assume centred features with comparable variance.

  • Fit the scaler on training data only. Put StandardScaler (opens in a new tab) and the model in one pipeline, so that cross-validation recomputes means and standard deviations inside every fold. Feature engineering covers scalers in general.
  • Know what your library does. scikit-learn's linear models penalise whatever columns you pass. glmnet standardises by default (standardize = TRUE) and reports coefficients on the original scale.
  • Convert back when you need original units. Divide a standardised coefficient by its feature's standard deviation.

With X_train and y_train holding a training split, the whole pattern is four lines:

Choosing the strength

λ\lambdaλ cannot be learned by minimising training error, because training error is always lowest at λ=0\lambda = 0λ=0. It is a hyperparameter, and it is chosen by held-out error:

  1. Set aside a test set and do not look at it.
  2. Lay out candidate strengths evenly on a log scale, for example 13 values from 10−310^{-3}10−3 to 10310^{3}103. Doubling λ\lambdaλ matters about as much at 0.1 as at 10.
  3. For each candidate, estimate held-out error by cross-validation, with scaling inside the pipeline.
  4. Take the candidate with the lowest cross-validated error. A more cautious rule, built into glmnet as lambda.1se, takes the strongest penalty whose error is within one standard error of the lowest. It gives a simpler model at no detectable cost.
  5. Refit on all the training data with that strength, then report test error once.

If the winner sits at either end of the grid, the grid was too narrow: extend it and repeat. scikit-learn bundles steps 2 to 5. RidgeCV (opens in a new tab) uses an efficient leave-one-out calculation, but its default grid is only 0.1, 1 and 10, so pass your own. LassoCV (opens in a new tab) builds a path of 100 strengths automatically and uses 5-fold cross-validation by default, and ElasticNetCV tunes the mix as well. Hyperparameter tuning covers search strategies in general.

Held-out error plotted against strength is U-shaped: too little penalty overfits and too much underfits. The worked example draws that curve for real numbers.

Edge cases and limitations

The same alpha is not the same strength in Ridge and Lasso
scikit-learn's Lasso and ElasticNet divide the squared-error term by twice the number of rows, and Ridge does not. In the worked example, Ridge(alpha=12) on 12 rows applies the same proportional shrinkage as a strength of 1 in the lesson's formula; on 12,000 rows it would take alpha=12000. An alpha copied from one class to the other, or kept after the dataset has grown a hundredfold, silently becomes a different strength. Retune whenever the estimator or the row count changes, and compare models by cross-validated error, never by their alpha values.
Lasso with more columns than rows
When features outnumber rows, least squares has no unique solution, and the lasso can keep at most as many features as there are rows, however many truly matter. The elastic net paper lists this limit, together with the habit of keeping only one of a correlated group, among its reasons for proposing the method. With thousands of columns, a few hundred rows and groups of related features, start with elastic net and tune the mix, or use ridge if you do not need a short feature list.
A feature the lasso dropped is not proven irrelevant
From two correlated features the lasso keeps one almost arbitrarily. In the worked example it dropped pages, yet pages alone predicts the score nearly as well as hours does (12.38 against 12.58 in step 1). The scikit-learn example on interpreting coefficients makes the same point: a dropped feature may still be related to the outcome, and the selection is unstable when features are correlated. Before reporting a selected list, refit on several resamples or cross-validation folds and see which features are chosen every time.
A strength at which every coefficient is zero
For the lasso there is a finite strength above which all coefficients are exactly zero and the model predicts the training mean. With standardised features it equals the largest single-feature coefficient, 12.58 in the worked example, and glmnet starts its path at that value. Ridge has no such point; its coefficients only approach zero. If cross-validation selects a strength at or close to that limit, the features carry no usable linear signal at this sample size and the honest model is the mean.
Sparse, high-dimensional inputs cannot be centred
Subtracting the mean from a sparse matrix, such as word counts or one-hot encoded identifiers, turns every zero into a non-zero value and can exhaust memory. The scikit-learn preprocessing guide recommends MaxAbsScaler, or StandardScaler with with_mean=False, which rescale without centring. Columns that are all 0/1 indicators already share a scale, so very large sparse models are often penalised with no standardisation step at all.
Penalised coefficients are not unbiased estimates
Shrinkage is deliberate bias. A ridge or lasso coefficient understates the least-squares association, and once the lasso has selected features, ordinary standard errors and p-values no longer apply, because the same data chose the features and estimated their coefficients. scikit-learn's penalised models report none. Use penalised models for prediction and for screening features; when you need an effect size with an interval, design the analysis for that purpose.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The model has many columns relative to its rows, for example after one-hot encoding or polynomial expansion, and held-out error is clearly worse than training error.
  • Features are strongly correlated and least-squares coefficients flip sign or change a lot between cross-validation folds. Use ridge, or elastic net if you also want some features removed.
  • You need a short list of features for cost or serving reasons, such as a model that must fit in memory or a set of measurements that is expensive to collect. Use lasso or elastic net.
  • There are more columns than rows. Least squares has no unique answer there, while ridge and elastic net do.
  • You are fitting logistic regression with many features or nearly separable classes, where unpenalised coefficients become very large and numerically unstable.

Think twice when

  • You have few features and many rows, and training and held-out error already agree. A penalty then only adds bias, and cross-validation will show it by selecting a strength near zero.
  • You need unbiased effect estimates with confidence intervals, as in the analysis of an experiment. Shrunken coefficients and p-values computed after selection do not provide them.
  • The relationship is non-linear or depends on interactions you have not encoded. A penalty controls variance and cannot supply a missing shape, so engineer the features or use a tree ensemble.
  • You cannot put the features on a common scale and cannot explain why their raw units are comparable. The penalty will then favour whichever columns happen to hold large numbers.

A simpler alternative. Use fewer features. Dropping one of each pair of near-duplicate columns by hand and fitting plain least squares is easier to explain and often performs as well when there are only a handful of candidates. It stops being enough once there are too many columns to review by eye, or the correlated groups are not obvious, and that is when a penalty tuned by cross-validation does the pruning for you.

Key takeaways

  • Regularization adds a penalty on coefficient size to the loss, accepting a little bias in return for a larger drop in variance, and the strength sets how much is traded.
  • Ridge (L2) shrinks all coefficients in proportion and shares weight among correlated features, but never sets a coefficient exactly to zero.
  • Lasso (L1) subtracts a fixed amount from every coefficient and zeroes the weak ones, which gives a sparse model, but it chooses almost arbitrarily among correlated features.
  • Elastic net mixes the two penalties, so it can drop weak features while keeping correlated ones together, at the cost of a second setting to tune.
  • Standardise features inside the pipeline before penalising them, because the penalty acts on coefficient size and coefficient size depends on units.
  • Choose the strength by cross-validation over a log-spaced grid, widen the grid if the winner is at its edge, and keep the test set for one final check.

Example 1 of 5

Google ad click prediction: L1 regularization keeps a billion-coefficient model small enough to serve

GoogleDocumented in a paper

The KDD 2013 paper "Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches", by McMahan and colleagues at Google, presents case studies from the deployed system that predicted click-through rates for sponsored search ads. Its section on online learning and sparsity describes the learning algorithm and its use of L1 and L2 regularization. [13]

The problem

For every candidate ad the system needs an estimate of the probability of a click, and it needs such estimates many billions of times a day. The model is a logistic regression over an extremely sparse feature space with billions of possible coefficients, updated continuously as new clicks and non-clicks arrive. Trained models are copied to many data centres for serving. Models are stored sparsely, so the number of non-zero coefficients decides how much memory each copy needs.

Where this topic fits

Regularization sits inside the per-coefficient update of the online learning algorithm, FTRL-Proximal. Each coefficient is rebuilt from two stored numbers, and the L1 strength acts as a threshold on one of them: under the threshold the coefficient is exactly zero and is not part of the served model. An L2 term appears in the same formula. Feature extraction comes before this step; calibration and serving come after it.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Each training example arrives as a sparse feature vector with a click or no-click label, and the model predicts a probability with its current coefficients.
  2. For every feature present in the example, the gradient of the log loss is added to an accumulated value z, with a correction term that encodes a learning rate specific to that coefficient.
  3. The coefficient is recomputed from z. If the size of z is at most the L1 strength, the coefficient is set to exactly zero.
  4. Otherwise z is moved towards zero by the L1 strength and divided by a term that contains the learning-rate schedule and the L2 strength. This is the same threshold-then-shrink pattern as the elastic net.
  5. Training keeps z for every feature, so a coefficient that is zero now can become non-zero later if the evidence accumulates.
  6. Only the non-zero coefficients have to be held in memory by the serving copies of the model.

Why this approach fits

At this scale sparsity is a cost requirement, not a matter of interpretation: every non-zero coefficient occupies memory in every serving copy. An L1 penalty is the direct way to ask for exact zeros during training itself, with no separate feature-selection pass over data that is too large to read twice.

The paper also shows why the optimiser matters for L1. The obvious approach, adding the penalty's subgradient to an online gradient step, almost never produces an exact zero, while the FTRL-Proximal formulation applies a hard threshold in closed form.

Limitation or tradeoff

The benefit depends on how the penalty is applied. The authors report that a style of L1 implementation that stops tracking a feature during training once its coefficient is zero cost more accuracy than they could accept; their system tracks more features in training and sparsifies only what is served. The comparison figures are relative, and two of the three baselines were measured on a smaller prototyping dataset.

The inputs are sparse indicators that already share a scale, so this is not a setting that teaches the standardise-first rule. The paper's experiments in that direction, which divided each example's feature vector by its norm, did not improve the authors' metrics.

The Horvath epigenetic clock: elastic net selects 353 DNA sites out of 21,369

Documented in a paper

Steve Horvath's 2013 article in Genome Biology, "DNA methylation age of human tissues and cell types", builds a predictor of age from DNA methylation measurements across many tissues. Its methods section states the penalised regression model, the software and the settings that were used. The article was read from its PubMed Central copy. [14][15]

The problem

DNA methylation arrays measure many thousands of sites, called CpGs, in each sample. After keeping the sites shared by two array platforms the study had 21,369 candidate predictors, against 7,844 non-cancer samples in total across 82 data sets, of which the first 39 data sets were used for training. The aim was one formula that estimates a person's age from almost any tissue. With more candidate predictors than samples, an unpenalised regression has no unique solution, nearby sites are likely to move together, and a formula that needed all 21,369 measurements would be awkward for other laboratories to reuse.

Where this topic fits

The elastic net is the model-fitting step. Before it, the data sets are normalised and chronological age is passed through a calibration transform. After it, the selected sites and their weights are the clock: the article describes the weighted average of the selected sites as an epigenetic clock. Choosing the sites and estimating their weights happen in the same fit.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Assemble methylation data sets from many tissues and assign whole data sets to training or to testing, so that the test uses studies the model has never seen.
  2. Restrict the candidate features to sites measured on both array platforms with few missing values, which leaves 21,369 columns.
  3. Transform chronological age, then regress the transformed age on all candidate sites with glmnet, using a mix of 0.5 between the L1 and L2 penalties.
  4. Choose the penalty strength lambda by cross-validation inside the training data only.
  5. Read off the sites with non-zero coefficients. Those 353 sites and their weights are the published predictor.
  6. Evaluate on the held-out data sets, reporting the correlation with chronological age and the median absolute error in years.

Why this approach fits

Each alternative fails for a specific reason. Least squares has no unique solution when there are more columns than rows, as here. Ridge would keep all 21,369 sites, which makes the predictor expensive to share and apply. A pure lasso would give a short list, but among correlated sites it keeps one of each group almost arbitrarily. Elastic net with an even mix gives a short list while tolerating the correlation, which is the situation the method was designed for.

Selecting lambda by cross-validation inside the training data, and keeping entire data sets aside for testing, is what lets the article describe its test accuracy as unbiased.

Limitation or tradeoff

The selected sites are a predictive set, not a list of the sites that drive ageing. With correlated features a different sample could have selected different members of each group, and the article itself reports that a 110-site subset is nearly as accurate. The article states the mix of 0.5 without describing any search over it. Accuracy also varies by tissue: the article names several tissues in which the predictor is poorly calibrated.

A 2015 erratum corrected the analysis of cancer tissue, where the wrong calibration function had been applied, and says the results for non-cancer tissue were unaffected.

Meta's Robyn: ridge regression for marketing channels that move together

Meta (Robyn)Documented in an open-source project

Robyn is an open-source marketing mix modelling package from Meta. Its "Key Features" documentation explains why the model is a ridge regression, and the model.R source file in the project's repository shows the glmnet call and how the penalty strength is set. [16][17]

The problem

A marketing mix model estimates how much each advertising channel contributed to sales, from a few years of weekly or daily data. Companies raise and lower spend on many channels at the same time, so the channel columns are strongly correlated. The Robyn documentation names the consequences: unstable coefficient estimates, coefficients that are hard to interpret, and overfitting. A budget recommendation built on coefficients that change sign between refits cannot be used.

Where this topic fits

The ridge fit is the core regression. Before it, each media variable is transformed for carry-over (adstock) and for diminishing returns (saturation), and trend and seasonality are separated out. After it, the coefficients are converted into each channel's contribution and passed to a budget allocator. The penalty strength is not fixed in advance: it is one of the hyperparameters that an outer optimisation loop proposes for every candidate model.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Transform each media variable with adstock and saturation functions whose parameters are hyperparameters.
  2. Compute a range for the penalty strength from the data: a maximum derived from the scaled features and the target, and a minimum set to a small fraction of it.
  3. Let the optimiser propose a number between 0 and 1 for the lambda hyperparameter and map it linearly onto that range.
  4. Fit a Gaussian glmnet model with the mix set to 0, which is pure ridge, at that one strength, with limits on the sign of the coefficients.
  5. Score the candidate on prediction error and on how far its channel contributions are from the shares of spend, and report the scores back to the optimiser.
  6. Repeat for many iterations and present the Pareto-optimal candidates to the analyst.

Why this approach fits

The purpose of the model is attribution across channels that are all believed to do something. Ridge suits that: it keeps every channel in the model and shares weight between correlated ones, which steadies the estimates. The documentation gives this reason for preferring it to the lasso, which would set some channels to zero. A single ridge fit is also fast, and that matters when thousands of candidate hyperparameter sets each need a fit.

Limitation or tradeoff

Ridge cannot say that a channel has no effect, so a channel that does nothing still receives part of the credit of the channels it is correlated with. The coefficients are shrunk, so contributions depend on the chosen strength, and here the strength comes from a multi-objective search, not from prediction error alone; the documentation itself calls its second objective controversial. The output is a set of candidate models, and choosing among them remains a judgement. The penalty stabilises the estimates. It does not make them causal, which is why the package also supports calibration against experiments.

What the sources document

  • The documentation attributes multicollinearity in marketing mix models to the usual practice of running several media channels at once, with spend rising on all of them over Christmas as its example, and lists the effects as unstable coefficients, coefficients that are hard to interpret, and overfitting. [16]
  • Robyn uses the L2 form of regularisation, ridge regression. The documentation says it was chosen over the lasso because the lasso removes features whereas ridge only makes their coefficients smaller, and that it is not realistic for many media variables to have no effect. [16]
  • The documentation states that Robyn uses the glmnet package for the regularised regression, and the model source file calls glmnet with a gaussian family, alpha set to 0 with a comment marking it as ridge regression, a single lambda value, and lower and upper limits on the coefficients. [16][17]
  • Lambda for the ridge penalty is listed as one of four types of hyperparameter, alongside adstocking, saturation and the share of training data, and Robyn uses Nevergrad to optimise them against objective functions that include the prediction error NRMSE and the decomposition distance DECOMP.RSSD. Prophet: an L1-style penalty decides where a forecast's trend may bend

Prophet (Meta open source)Documented in official docs

Prophet is an open-source forecasting library. Its documentation pages on trend changepoints and on diagnostics describe how the trend is regularised and how the strength should be tuned. [18][19]

The problem

Real time series change their growth rate from time to time: a product launch, a price change, a new competitor. A forecasting model has to let the trend bend at such moments without bending at every wiggle, because a trend that follows noise will extrapolate noise. Nobody can list the true changepoints by hand for thousands of series.

Where this topic fits

The penalty sits on the trend component. Prophet gives the trend many dates at which its slope is allowed to change and puts a sparse prior on the size of each change, which the documentation describes as equivalent to L1 regularization. Seasonality is a separate component with its own prior scale, which the documentation likens to an L2 penalty, and holiday effects have a similar one. Tuning happens outside the fit, by time-series cross-validation.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Place a grid of potential changepoints over the early part of the history: by default 25 of them, spread over the first 80%.
  2. Give every potential changepoint a parameter for the change in growth rate at that date.
  3. Fit the model with a sparse prior on those parameters, so that most potential changepoints go unused and the trend bends at only a few dates.
  4. Control the strength through changepoint_prior_scale. A larger scale is a weaker penalty and a more flexible trend, so this setting runs in the opposite direction to alpha or lambda.
  5. Tune the scale on a log-spaced grid by cross-validation over cutoff dates, scoring forecasts over a fixed horizon.

Why this approach fits

This is the lasso idea applied to a different kind of coefficient. The question "where does the trend change?" becomes "which of many candidate changes are non-zero?", and an L1 penalty answers that during fitting. It scales to many series because nobody has to nominate dates, and one number controls flexibility.

The seasonal terms get ridge-like treatment for the matching reason: every term of the seasonal curve is expected to contribute a little, so shrinking them together makes more sense than selecting among them.

Limitation or tradeoff

The documentation is direct about the failure modes. Too small a scale underfits the trend and pushes real changes into the noise term; too large a scale lets the trend absorb yearly seasonality. By default no changepoints are placed in the last 20% of the history, so a recent change of direction is not picked up unless that range is extended, and the documentation says that range is hard to tune by cross-validation. As with any L1 selection, the dates that survive are the model's economical description of the data, not confirmed events.

What the sources document

  • Prophet first sets up many candidate changepoints where the growth rate may change and then applies a sparse prior to the sizes of those rate changes; the documentation equates this prior with L1 regularization. [18]
  • The default is 25 candidate changepoints spaced evenly over the first 80% of the series, and the documentation notes that the sparse prior leaves most of them unused. [18]
  • The prior's strength is controlled by changepoint_prior_scale, whose default is 0.05; a larger value gives a more flexible trend and a smaller value a less flexible one. [18]
  • The tuning guidance likens changepoint_prior_scale to a lasso penalty and suggests searching 0.001 to 0.5 on a log scale. It likens seasonality_prior_scale to the L2 penalty of ridge regression and says its default of 10 amounts to almost no regularization. [19]
  • The documentation's tuning example evaluates a 4 by 4 grid of the two prior scales with cross-validation, scoring RMSE averaged over a 30-day horizon. [19]

Example 5 of 5

XGBoost: an L2 penalty on leaf values that is switched on by default

XGBoost projectDocumented in official docs

XGBoost is a gradient-boosted tree library. Its tutorial "Introduction to Boosted Trees" derives the training objective, including the regularization term, and its parameter reference documents the lambda and alpha settings and their defaults. [20][21]

The problem

A boosted tree model adds hundreds of trees, each fitted to the errors of the trees before it. Left unchecked, a tree can create a leaf for a handful of rows and give it a large value that corrects those rows exactly, which is fitting noise. The library needs a way to keep each tree's contribution cautious, and to decide whether a proposed split is worth making, inside one objective.

Where this topic fits

The penalty is part of the objective that every new tree is built to minimise. It enters twice: in the value assigned to each leaf, and in the gain used to accept or reject each split. It works beside the other controls on complexity, such as the learning rate, the tree depth and the per-leaf charge gamma. The numbers being penalised are leaf values, not feature weights.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Write the objective for the tree being added as the training loss plus a complexity term: gamma times the number of leaves, plus half of lambda times the sum of squared leaf values.
  2. For each leaf, add up the first derivatives of the loss over its rows, called G, and the second derivatives, called H.
  3. Set the leaf's value to minus G divided by H plus lambda. With lambda at zero this is the unpenalised best value; a positive lambda shrinks it towards zero, as ridge shrinks a coefficient.
  4. Score each candidate split with the gain formula, in which lambda appears in every denominator and gamma is subtracted, and keep the split only if the gain is positive.
  5. Multiply the tree's leaf values by the learning rate and add the tree to the model.

Why this approach fits

The leaf value formula is ridge regression in miniature. For squared-error loss, H is the number of rows in the leaf, so the leaf value is the sum of the residuals divided by the row count plus lambda. A leaf with thousands of rows is barely affected, while a leaf with two or three rows is pulled strongly towards zero. The penalty therefore concentrates on the leaves most likely to be fitting noise, and the same quantity makes splits that isolate a few rows less attractive.

Limitation or tradeoff

This penalty acts on leaf values, so it does none of the things an L1 or L2 penalty does for the features of a linear model: it selects no features and yields no interpretable coefficients. Trees split on thresholds, so rescaling a feature leaves the tree booster unchanged and the standardise-first rule does not apply to it. The rule does apply to the library's linear booster, whose L1 and L2 terms default to zero.

Lambda also interacts with the learning rate, the depth and the minimum child weight, so tuning it alone can mislead. And because its default is 1, a model trained with default settings is already regularised, which matters when results are compared with those of another library.

What the sources document

  • The tutorial describes the objective as the sum of a training loss and a regularization term, and says the regularization term limits model complexity and so guards against overfitting. [20]
  • XGBoost defines the complexity of a tree as gamma times the number of leaves plus one half of lambda times the sum of the squared leaf scores. [20]
  • The tutorial derives the best leaf weight for a fixed tree structure as minus G divided by H plus lambda, and a split gain in which lambda is added to each denominator and gamma is subtracted. [20]
  • In the parameter reference for version 3.4.2, lambda (alias reg_lambda) is the L2 regularization term on weights with a default of 1 for the tree booster, and alpha (alias reg_alpha) is the L1 term with a default of 0. For both, increasing the value is described as making the model more conservative. [21]
  • For the linear booster, lambda and alpha both default to 0 and are described as normalised to the number of training examples. [21]

4Common mistakes

  1. Fitting a penalised model on features in their raw units.

    Why it causes problems
    Features with small numeric ranges need large coefficients and are penalised heavily, while features with large values are almost free. Which features survive then depends on units. In the worked example an unscaled lasso kept pages and dropped hours, and reversed its choice when pages were expressed in hundreds.
    How to recognise it
    Convert one column to a different unit, for example metres to kilometres, and refit. If the predictions or the set of non-zero coefficients change, the penalty is acting on raw units. Also check whether the kept features are simply the ones with the largest standard deviations.
    How to fix it
    Put StandardScaler, or MaxAbsScaler for sparse data, in a pipeline directly before the penalised estimator and fit the pipeline as a whole. In glmnet, leave standardize at its default of TRUE.

  2. Scaling the whole dataset before splitting it or cross-validating.

    Why it causes problems
    The means and standard deviations then include the validation and test rows, so information from held-out data leaks into training. The cross-validated error is optimistic, and the selected strength is tuned to rows the model was never supposed to see.
    How to recognise it
    Look for fit_transform called on the full feature matrix before train_test_split or cross_val_score. A cross-validated error noticeably lower than the later test error is the downstream sign.
    How to fix it
    Split first. Build make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), estimator) and pass the pipeline to the cross-validation routine, so that the scaler is refitted on the training part of every fold.

  3. Accepting the default strength, or searching a grid of three values.

    Why it causes problems
    A default such as alpha=1.0 in Ridge and Lasso is arbitrary for your data and its scale. The right value can be thousands of times larger or smaller, and a model fitted at the wrong strength is either still overfitted or flattened towards the mean.
    How to recognise it
    The selected alpha equals the smallest or largest value in the grid, or nobody can say how the value in the code was chosen. Plot cross-validated error against the logarithm of alpha; a curve that is still falling at the edge has not reached its minimum.
    How to fix it
    Search a log-spaced grid that spans several orders of magnitude, for example np.logspace(-3, 3, 13), with RidgeCV, LassoCV or a grid search over the pipeline, and extend it until the minimum lies in the interior.

  4. Choosing the strength by its error on the test set.

    Why it causes problems
    The test set has then been used to fit a hyperparameter, so its error is no longer an honest estimate of performance on new data. With a small test set the optimism can be as large as the improvement being claimed.
    How to recognise it
    The code loops over alpha values and evaluates each one on X_test, or the reported test score is the best of several runs.
    How to fix it
    Select the strength by cross-validation inside the training set only, refit once at the chosen value, and evaluate on the test set a single time.

  5. Reading the features a lasso dropped as unimportant, or its kept list as the true drivers.

    Why it causes problems
    Among correlated features the lasso keeps one almost arbitrarily, and a different sample can swap it for another. Decisions such as retiring a data source, or telling a stakeholder that a factor does not matter, then rest on chance.
    How to recognise it
    Refit on bootstrap resamples or on each cross-validation fold and compare the selected sets. Features that appear in only some of the fits, especially ones strongly correlated with a kept feature, are not reliably selected.
    How to fix it
    Report how often each feature is selected across resamples, use elastic net when features come in correlated groups, and describe a dropped feature as redundant given the others, not as irrelevant.

scikit-learn ships a diabetes dataset: 442 patients, ten baseline measurements (age, sex, body mass index, average blood pressure and six blood serum measurements named s1 to s6) and a target that measures disease progression one year later. Load it in raw units with load_diabetes(scaled=False). Everything runs locally with free tools.

To make overfitting visible, expand the ten features to all degree-2 terms (squares and pairwise products). That gives 65 columns for 331 training rows.

  1. Split the data 75/25 with train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0).
  2. Build four pipelines of the form PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, include_bias=False), then StandardScaler(), then an estimator. The estimators are LinearRegression(), RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-2, 4, 25)), LassoCV(cv=5, max_iter=100_000) and ElasticNetCV(l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9], cv=5, max_iter=100_000).
  3. For each, report the training RMSE, the test RMSE, the number of non-zero coefficients, and the selected strength (and mix, for the elastic net).
  4. Check that the selected ridge and lasso strengths lie inside their grids and not at an edge.
  5. Refit the lasso pipeline without StandardScaler. Report its test RMSE and non-zero count, list the columns it keeps, and explain why that list looks so different.

Requirements

  • Fit every preprocessing step inside a pipeline, on the training split only.
  • Use the test split once per model, for the final RMSE, and never to choose a strength.
  • Report the count of non-zero coefficients from the fitted estimator's coef_ attribute.
  • Name the columns kept by the unscaled lasso with get_feature_names_out so that the list can be read.
  • Explain the unscaled result in terms of the size of the numbers in each column, not in terms of accuracy alone.

Expected behaviour

With scikit-learn 1.9.1, least squares has the lowest training RMSE (about 47.0) and the highest test RMSE (about 61.3). The three penalised models land between 56 and 57 on the test set. Ridge selects an alpha near 17.8 and keeps all 65 columns, the lasso selects an alpha near 0.32 and keeps 17, and the elastic net selects a mix of 0.9 and keeps 39. Without scaling, the lasso reaches a similar test RMSE (about 56.3) with an alpha near 225, but every one of the 20 columns it keeps is a square or a product, and none involves s4 or s5.

Hint 1

Build the pipeline with make_pipeline and reach the fitted estimator with model[-1]. Its alpha_ attribute holds the selected strength.

Hint 2

LassoCV stores the grid it searched in alphas_, so its smallest and largest values tell you where the edges are.

Hint 3

Print X.std(axis=0) for the raw data. The standard deviations run from about 0.5 to about 35, before any squaring.

python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.linear_model import ElasticNetCV, LassoCV, LinearRegression, RidgeCV
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler

data = load_diabetes(scaled=False)                    # raw units, not the pre-scaled default
X, y, names = data.data, data.target, data.feature_names
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

def build(estimator, scale=True):
    steps = [PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, include_bias=False)]     # 10 features -> 65 columns
    if scale:
        steps.append(StandardScaler())
    return make_pipeline(*steps, estimator)

def rmse(model, features, target):
    return mean_squared_error(target, model.predict(features)) ** 0.5

def describe(name, model):
    final = model[-1]
    chosen = f"alpha={final.alpha_:.3g}" if hasattr(final, "alpha_") else "no penalty"
    if hasattr(final, "l1_ratio_"):
        chosen += f", l1_ratio={final.l1_ratio_}"
    print(f"{name:14s} train {rmse(model, X_train, y_train):5.1f}  test {rmse(model, X_test, y_test):5.1f}  "
          f"non-zero {np.sum(final.coef_ != 0):2d}/65  {chosen}")

models = {
    "least squares": build(LinearRegression()),
    "ridge": build(RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-2, 4, 25))),
    "lasso": build(LassoCV(cv=5, max_iter=100_000)),
    "elastic net": build(ElasticNetCV(l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9], cv=5, max_iter=100_000)),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    describe(name, model.fit(X_train, y_train))

lasso = models["lasso"][-1]
print(f"lasso grid: {lasso.alphas_.min():.3g} to {lasso.alphas_.max():.3g}")

unscaled = build(LassoCV(cv=5, max_iter=100_000), scale=False).fit(X_train, y_train)
describe("lasso, raw", unscaled)
columns = unscaled[0].get_feature_names_out(names)
print("kept:", ", ".join(c for c, w in zip(columns, unscaled[-1].coef_) if w != 0))

Parts 1 to 3. Least squares fits the training rows best and the test rows worst, a gap of 14 RMSE points. Each penalised model gives up about 4 points of training RMSE and gains about 5 on the test set. The three differ in what they keep: ridge all 65 columns, elastic net 39 and lasso 17.

Part 4. The ridge grid runs from 0.01 to 10,000 and the selected 17.8 is well inside it. The lasso searched from 0.0565 to 56.5 and selected 0.323. Note that 17.8 and 0.323 are not comparable numbers, because Ridge does not divide its loss by the number of rows.

Part 5. The unscaled lasso predicts about as well on this split, so accuracy alone would not warn you. Its selection, however, follows the size of the numbers. Raw standard deviations range from about 0.5 for sex and s5 to about 35 for s1, and products of large columns are larger still. Such columns need tiny coefficients, which are nearly free under the penalty, so they are all the model can afford at an alpha of 225. Columns with small values, including s5 and its products, which the scaled lasso does use, would need large coefficients and are priced out. The kept list therefore reflects units, and it would change if blood pressure were recorded in different units.

Do not read much into differences of half a point between the penalised models. When we repeated the split with random_state 0 to 7, least squares was the worst every time (mean test RMSE 61.2), ridge and the scaled lasso both averaged 55.0, and the unscaled lasso averaged 55.7.

Check your answer

  • Is the least-squares training RMSE the lowest of the four and its test RMSE the highest?
  • Are the test RMSE values of ridge, lasso and elastic net all within about one point of each other?
  • Does the scaled lasso keep roughly a quarter of the 65 columns?
  • Is each selected alpha strictly inside the range of values that was searched?
  • Does the unscaled lasso keep only squares and products, and can you explain why s5 is missing from them?
