AI Safety, Ethics and Governance

AI safety, ethics and governance is the practice of finding out how an AI product can harm people (through biased outputs, misuse, unreliable answers or over-reliance), documenting what it is and is not for, managing those risks with tests and controls, and meeting legal duties, with named people accountable at every step.

1Overview

Why it matters

An AI feature can pass every accuracy test and still harm people. A language model can complete "The woman worked as a ..." with stereotyped jobs, as the model card of a widely used BERT model shows. An image generator tuned to show a range of people can fail in the opposite direction, and Google paused people images in Gemini because of it. A coding assistant can be turned into an extortion tool. All three are documented, and in the last two the response was a product decision, such as turning a feature off or banning accounts and building a classifier, not a better prompt.

Obligations are growing as well. The EU AI Act already bans some practices and sets duties by risk tier. A Texas law that took effect on 1 January 2026 names NIST's Generative AI Profile in one route to a liability defence for companies that find their own violations through an internal review. Model cards, system cards and transparency notes exist because the people who adopt an AI system need to know its limits before they build on it. Engineers who can classify a use case, document it, test it by subgroup and show who signed it off are the ones who can ship in these settings, and the same skills help anyone who builds, integrates or approves AI features, not only lawyers.

  • Design a lightweight governance routine (owners, gates, records, incident path) that fits the risk of a delivery team's feature.

Key terms

Harmful bias
A systematic pattern in a system's outputs or errors that disadvantages some people. It can come from data, from modelling or tuning choices, or from how people use the output.
Misuse
Use of a system in a way its builders did not intend but could anticipate, such as using a coding assistant to automate extortion.
Model card
A short document published with a trained model that states its intended use, how it was evaluated (ideally broken down by group) and where it is known to fail.
System card
A document about a whole released system or model release. Anthropic describes its system cards as documenting capabilities, safety evaluations and deployment decisions.
Transparency Note
Microsoft's name for a document that explains a system's capabilities, its limitations and the choices that affect how it performs, including the people and the environment around the technology.
AI RMF
The AI Risk Management Framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), a voluntary framework organised around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage.
Profile
A NIST companion document that applies the AI RMF to a particular setting. The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) is the one for generative AI.
High-risk AI system
In the EU AI Act, a system that is a safety component of (or is itself) a product covered by the EU safety laws in Annex I and needing third-party conformity assessment, or that is used in an area listed in Annex III, such as employment or education. It carries the Act's heaviest duties for systems that are allowed.
Provider and deployer
The EU AI Act's two main roles. A provider develops an AI system or model and markets it under its own name or trademark. A deployer uses an AI system under its own authority, other than for personal non-professional use.
Impact assessment
A structured review, done before development starts, of who a system will affect and what could go wrong for them, kept as a record that later reviews can check.

2Explanation

Introduction

You know from How Large Language Models Work that a model produces likely continuations of its input, learned from text that people wrote. That has two practical consequences for a product. The model can repeat patterns in that text that you would not endorse, and it will follow instructions from anyone who can reach it, including people you did not plan for. Neither problem shows up in a loss curve or a benchmark score.

This lesson covers the work that catches those problems before users do. It has four parts. First, finding where harm can enter a product: biased outcomes, misuse, unreliable output and over-reliance. Second, writing down what you built, with model cards and system cards, so others know its intended use and limits. Third, using shared structures instead of inventing your own: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (a voluntary US framework) and the EU AI Act (a binding regulation with risk tiers). Fourth, turning those into a routine inside a delivery team: owners, gates, records and an incident path.

An everyday comparison is a restaurant kitchen. It labels allergens, is inspected according to the kind of food it serves, has a named person in charge and keeps a log of what went wrong. None of that cooks the food, but without it nobody can tell whether the kitchen is safe. Governance does the same job for an AI feature. It does not make the model better. It makes risk visible, assigned and checked.

The diagram shows the shape of the process used through the rest of the lesson. Its four groups are the NIST functions Map, Measure, Manage and Govern, which are explained below.

Two notes on scope. "Safety" here means preventing harm from the behaviour of deployed AI products. It does not cover research on aligning future, more capable systems. And nothing here is legal advice: laws, dates and guidance change, so each section points to the official page that was read on 11 October 2026.

How it works

Where product-level harm comes from

Harm usually enters at the level of the product, not only the model. Four patterns recur, and each has a different control.

PatternHow it arisesDocumented caseMain control
Biased outcomesSkewed data, labels or tuning give unequal errors or stereotyped outputThe BERT base uncased model card (opens in a new tab) contrasts masked-word predictions for a man's and a woman's occupation and says the bias also affects fine-tuned versionsEvaluation by subgroup (Fairness and Bias in ML)
Over-correctionA fix for one bias or risk creates another failureGoogle turned off people image generation in Gemini (opens in a new tab) in February 2024 after tuning for variety missed cases that should not show a rangeTests in both directions, plus a switch-off
MisuseA legitimate capability is used for fraud, extortion or attacksAnthropic's threat report (opens in a new tab) describes Claude Code used in a data-extortion operationGuardrails and monitoring (Guardrails, Red Teaming)
Over-reliancePeople accept an output without checking itMicrosoft's Responsible AI Standard (opens in a new tab) asks teams to design systems so decision-makers stay aware of the tendency to over-rely on outputs ("automation bias")Human review designed into the workflow (Human-in-the-Loop)

NIST names three kinds of bias to manage: systemic, computational and statistical, and human-cognitive (see the Measure playbook (opens in a new tab)). The third matters in products because the person reading the output decides what happens to it. NIST also defines risk (opens in a new tab) as a composite of the probability of an event and the magnitude of its consequences, and says the framework does not prescribe how much risk an organisation should tolerate. A simple scoring rule that follows from this (our choice, not NIST's) is:

score=likelihood×severity\text{score} = \text{likelihood} \times \text{severity}score=likelihood×severity

Transparency: say what you built

A model card follows the proposal of Mitchell and colleagues (2019) (opens in a new tab): a short record with model details, intended use, factors, metrics, evaluation and training data, quantitative analyses broken down by group (including intersections of groups), ethical considerations, and caveats and recommendations. The Hugging Face annotated template (opens in a new tab) is a ready-made layout of such sections that assigns them to roles, so a developer, a person who studies technology and society, and a project organiser each fill in what they know.

A model card describes a model. Harm happens in a system: the prompt, retrieval, tools, interface and the human process around them. A product team therefore writes a system-level document as well. Microsoft's Azure OpenAI Transparency Note (opens in a new tab) and Anthropic's system cards (opens in a new tab) are two published forms. A useful minimum is: intended and out-of-scope use, data sources, results by subgroup with the metric named, known failure modes and mitigations, what was not tested, an owner, a version and a date, and the events that trigger an update.

The NIST AI RMF: a shared vocabulary for risk work

The AI RMF 1.0 (opens in a new tab), released on 26 January 2023, is for voluntary use. Its core (opens in a new tab) has four functions. Govern is cross-cutting and runs through the other three. Once governance is in place, Map, Measure and Manage can be applied in any order and repeated. The Playbook (opens in a new tab) breaks each function into subcategories. These are examples, with what a delivery team might produce for each (our interpretation):

FunctionExample subcategoryA team deliverable
GovernGOVERN 2.1 (opens in a new tab): roles, responsibilities and lines of communication for mapping, measuring and managing AI risk are documentedOwner list and escalation path
MapMAP 1.1 (opens in a new tab): intended purpose, laws, norms and deployment settings are understood and documented; MAP 1.5: organisational risk tolerances are determined and documentedIntake form, impact assessment, tolerance statement
MeasureMEASURE 2.11 (opens in a new tab): fairness and bias are evaluated and results documented; MEASURE 2.7 covers security and resilienceSubgroup metrics, red-team report
ManageMANAGE 2.4 (opens in a new tab): mechanisms exist, with assigned responsibility, to supersede, disengage or deactivate a system; MANAGE 4.1 and 4.3 cover post-deployment monitoring and incident trackingSwitch-off, monitoring plan, incident log

NIST published a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) on 26 July 2024, and its overview page says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. The Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (H.B. 149 (opens in a new tab)) names the Profile in Section 552.105(e): a defendant that finds a violation through an internal review, and substantially complies with the Profile or another recognised framework, may not be found liable.

The EU AI Act: obligations follow the use case

The Act classifies by use, not by technology. The same model is minimal-risk inside a spam filter and high-risk when it ranks job applicants, because Annex III (opens in a new tab) lists recruitment, selection and filtering of applications. Tiers can stack: a hiring tool with a chat interface is high-risk and also carries transparency duties. Under Article 6(3) (opens in a new tab) an Annex III system is not high-risk if it poses no significant risk of harm to health, safety or fundamental rights and also meets at least one condition: it only does a narrow procedural task, improves a finished human activity, detects deviations without replacing human assessment, or does preparatory work. That never applies to a system that profiles people, and the provider must document the assessment.

Banned practices in Article 5 (opens in a new tab) include social scoring and inferring emotions in workplaces and education settings. Roles matter too. A provider (opens in a new tab) develops a system or model and markets it under its own name; a deployer uses one under its own authority. For high-risk systems the summary of the Act (opens in a new tab) lists provider duties such as risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight and cybersecurity. Providers of general-purpose models carry separate duties, with more for models trained with over 102510^{25}1025 FLOP of compute.

On the Commission's AI Act page (opens in a new tab), prohibitions and AI literacy duties apply from 2 February 2025, general-purpose model duties from 2 August 2025, and the Act generally from 2 August 2026. The AI Omnibus, which the page says entered into force on 27 July 2026, moved high-risk uses in areas such as employment and education to 2 December 2027 and high-risk systems built into regulated products to 2 August 2028. Check that page before relying on any date here.

Governance inside a delivery team

The flow diagram in the introduction is one workable routine. Four ideas make it work.

  • Named people. Each feature has an owner who answers for it, and a reviewer who did not build it. Without names, "the team" accepts every risk and nobody owns any. Microsoft's Standard has teams name reviewers in the impact assessment and secure their approvals.
  • Gates, not a meeting at the end. That Standard requires the impact assessment to be reviewed and approved before development starts and updated before each new release stage. A review the day before launch can only approve or delay.
  • Depth in proportion to risk. A spam filter needs a one-page checklist. A hiring ranker needs an impact assessment, subgroup testing and independent review. The tier decides which.
  • Rules written before results. Fix the tolerance and the pass thresholds before you see the numbers. The Standard asks teams to document release criteria, such as a minimum level for every group and a maximum difference between groups, before the system is evaluated against them.

Finish with records: the tier and the reasoning, the risk register, the evaluation report, the card, the sign-off and its date, and an incident log. Re-open the review when a trigger fires: a model swap, a new data source, a new user group, an incident, or a change in the law.

Worked example: Triage a CV-ranking assistant before launch (invented scenario)

This scenario is invented so that every number can be reproduced. A team has built an assistant that ranks job applicants for recruiters and answers applicants' questions in a chat window. Before launch the review board wants one record that answers three questions: which tiers apply, which risks reach our tolerance, and does the subgroup gap pass? The script below uses only the Python standard library.

The inputs are decided before the scores are seen:

  • The uses are CV filtering and a chatbot.
  • Each risk gets a likelihood and a severity from 1 to 5. The score is their product, so it runs from 1 to 25. The team's tolerance is 12: any risk at or above it needs a mitigation before launch. NIST does not prescribe a tolerance, and MAP 1.5 asks the organisation to determine and document its own.
  • A test set gives shortlisted and total applicants per group: group A 120 of 400, group B 54 of 300.
  • The team requires the lowest selection rate to be at least 0.8 times the highest, a threshold borrowed from the four-fifths rule that the Fairness and Bias in ML lesson explains.
python
import json

PROHIBITED_USES = frozenset({"emotion_inference_at_work", "social_scoring"})
ANNEX_III_USES = frozenset({"cv_filtering", "candidate_evaluation", "credit_scoring", "exam_scoring"})
TRANSPARENCY_USES = frozenset({"chatbot", "synthetic_media"})


def applicable_tiers(uses):
    tiers = []
    if uses & PROHIBITED_USES:
        tiers.append("unacceptable")
    if uses & ANNEX_III_USES:
        tiers.append("high-risk")
    if uses & TRANSPARENCY_USES:
        tiers.append("transparency")
    return tiers or ["minimal"]


TOLERANCE = 12  # the team's own threshold
RISKS = (
    {"id": "R1", "risk": "Shortlist disadvantages one applicant group", "likelihood": 4, "severity": 5},
    {"id": "R2", "risk": "Recruiters accept the ranking without review", "likelihood": 4, "severity": 4},
    {"id": "R3", "risk": "Instructions hidden in a CV steer the ranking", "likelihood": 3, "severity": 4},
    {"id": "R4", "risk": "Applicants are not told AI is used", "likelihood": 3, "severity": 3},
    {"id": "R5", "risk": "Applicant data leaks through prompts or logs", "likelihood": 2, "severity": 4},
)
SHORTLISTED = {"group_a": (120, 400), "group_b": (54, 300)}  # (shortlisted, applicants)
MIN_RATIO = 0.8


def score(risk):
    return risk["likelihood"] * risk["severity"]


def selection_rates(counts):
    return {group: kept / total for group, (kept, total) in counts.items()}


def decide(uses, risks, counts):
    tiers = applicable_tiers(uses)
    rates = selection_rates(counts)
    ratio = min(rates.values()) / max(rates.values())
    ranked = sorted(((score(r), r["id"]) for r in risks), reverse=True)
    blockers = [f"{rid} scores {s}" for s, rid in ranked if s >= TOLERANCE]
    if ratio < MIN_RATIO:
        blockers.append(f"impact ratio {ratio:.2f} is below {MIN_RATIO}")
    blocked = bool(blockers) or "unacceptable" in tiers
    return {
        "tiers": tiers,
        "selection_rates": {g: round(v, 3) for g, v in rates.items()},
        "impact_ratio": round(ratio, 3),
        "status": "blocked" if blocked else "approved",
        "blockers": blockers,
    }


uses = frozenset({"cv_filtering", "chatbot"})
print(json.dumps(decide(uses, RISKS, SHORTLISTED), indent=2))

What the record does not prove. It is one metric on one invented test set. It does not show that the system is fair, and the high-risk duties (logging, human oversight, documentation) still have to be met. It is the input to a human review, followed by a model or system card, a signed decision record and a monitoring plan.

Edge cases and limitations

The same model lands in different tiers
The AI Act and most internal policies classify by use. A summariser for internal meeting notes may be minimal-risk, while the same model screening job applicants is high-risk under Annex III. Write the use case down at intake and run the classification again whenever a feature, data source or user group is added, because a small product change can move the tier.
Aggregate metrics hide the gap
Overall accuracy can look fine while one group carries the errors. In the first exercise a classifier is 0.866 accurate overall, but 0.925 for one group and 0.787 for the other, and its false positive rate is 0.033 for one group against 0.167 for the other. Report every metric for each factor value, and decide the acceptable gap before you look at the numbers.
Fixing one bias can create another
Google said its Gemini tuning to show a range of people failed to account for cases that clearly should not show a range, and that the model also became more cautious than intended and refused some harmless prompts. Test a mitigation in both directions: prompts where variety is wanted, prompts where a specific attribute is clearly meant, and harmless prompts near sensitive topics. Keep a switch-off so a faulty fix can be paused quickly.
An upstream model card does not cover your system
A card describes the model as released. Your product adds prompts, retrieval, tools, data and users. The BERT card itself warns that its bias also affects fine-tuned versions, so a team that builds on it inherits that limit and still has to measure its own system on its own population. Cite the upstream card in your system card, and do not repeat its claims as if you had tested them.
Rules and dates change under you
The AI Act's high-risk dates moved when the AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026, and NIST's overview page says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. A lesson, a vendor slide or a model's memory of the rules can be out of date. Record the URL and the date you checked, and have someone re-check before each release.
Transparency and security can pull apart
The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice sends model documentation to downstream providers, gives the AI Office access on request, and only encourages signatories to consider disclosing it to the public. Publishing every red-team detail can help attackers. Write different documents for different readers and keep the detailed findings in the internal decision record.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The feature influences a decision about a person (hiring, credit, education, health, benefits), or it produces content that people may take for human-written or factual.
  • The system can act through tools, code execution or messages, so misuse or an error has side effects outside the chat window.
  • You sell or deploy into the EU, or a customer's procurement asks for model cards, transparency notes or a risk assessment.
  • You fine-tune or wrap a third-party model and need to show what you tested yourself, because the upstream card does not cover your system.

Think twice when

  • The work is a throwaway prototype with synthetic data and no real users. A one-page checklist is enough until real users or real data arrive, then classify it again.
  • You expect a card or a framework to replace engineering controls. Guardrails, access limits, monitoring and a switch-off still have to be built.
  • You need a legal opinion on one specific product. This lesson shows how to prepare the facts; counsel decides how the law applies.

A simpler alternative. For a low-risk internal feature, a one-page checklist (purpose, users, data, known failure modes, owner, rollback) and a short model card are often enough. Move to an impact assessment, subgroup testing and independent review as soon as the feature touches an area the EU Act lists as high-risk, handles personal data about many people, or acts on its own.

Key takeaways

  • Most harm from AI features appears at the product level, so test and document the whole system, and measure results for each group instead of in aggregate.
  • A model card or system card states intended use, out-of-scope use, subgroup results and known limits, and it helps only while it has an owner, a version and a date.
  • The NIST AI RMF is a voluntary structure of four functions, with Govern running through Map, Measure and Manage, and it leaves the risk tolerance for your team to set.
  • EU AI Act duties follow the use case, in tiers of banned, high-risk, transparency and minimal plus a tier for general-purpose models, and one product can carry several tiers.
  • Named owners, gates early in the design, depth in proportion to risk, thresholds fixed before results, and a tested switch-off are the parts of governance a delivery team controls.
  • Dates and rules change, so record the official page and the day you read it instead of relying on memory or a summary.

Example 1 of 5

A model card that documents bias, for BERT base (uncased) on the Hugging Face Hub

Hugging Face (card) and Google (model)Documented in official docs

The Hugging Face Hub card for google-bert/bert-base-uncased, read together with the Hub's annotated model card template and the original model card proposal by Mitchell and colleagues (2019). The card is the document a team reads before building on this widely used pre-trained model. [19][20][18]

The problem

BERT is released as a pre-trained model that other teams fine-tune for their own tasks. Anyone who does so inherits what the model learned from its training text, including stereotyped associations. Those teams need to know the intended use and the known bias before they build on it, not after users complain.

Where this topic fits

The model card sits between model release and downstream products. Before it comes training and evaluation by the model's authors. After it comes the downstream team's own evaluation and its system-level documentation, which should cite the upstream card instead of repeating its claims.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The card states the intended use: the raw model can do masked language modelling or next-sentence prediction, but it is mostly meant to be fine-tuned on tasks that use the whole sentence to decide, such as classification or question answering.
  2. A "Limitations and bias" section gives a concrete probe. The same masked-word template is filled for a man and for a woman, and the top predicted occupations are listed for each.
  3. The card states that this bias will also affect all fine-tuned versions of the model, so a downstream team cannot assume that fine-tuning on its own task removes it.
  4. The Hub publishes an annotated template that splits the work between a developer, a person who analyses technology and society, and a project organiser, and asks for evaluation broken down by factors such as population subgroup.
  5. A typical downstream team reads the card, re-runs the probe on the exact checkpoint it plans to use, and adds its own subgroup evaluation on data from its task.
  6. The team then cites the upstream card in its own system card and records what it tested itself.

Why this approach fits

A concrete probe is more useful to a builder than a general warning that the model may be biased, because the builder can re-run it on their own checkpoint and see the same skew. Putting intended use next to known limits answers the first two questions a downstream team has: what is this for, and where does it fail.

Limitation or tradeoff

The probe uses two prompts. It shows that a skew exists, not how large it is or how it affects your task, so it cannot replace your own evaluation by subgroup. A card is also a snapshot of one release: the pre-trained model is only the starting point, and the system built on it needs its own card.

What the sources document

  • The card's "Limitations and bias" section shows fill-mask predictions for "The man worked as a [MASK]" and "The woman worked as a [MASK]" with different top-five occupations for each, and states that the bias will also affect all fine-tuned versions of the model. [19]
  • The card says the raw model is mostly intended to be fine-tuned on downstream tasks that use the whole sentence to make decisions, such as sequence classification, token classification or question answering, and points to a different model for text generation. [19]
  • The card says the team that released BERT did not write a model card, and that the Hugging Face team wrote this one. [19]
  • The Hub's annotated template assigns card sections to three roles (developer, sociotechnic and project organizer) and says evaluation is ideally disaggregated by factors such as task, domain and population subgroup. [20]
  • Mitchell and colleagues propose model cards as short documents that report benchmarked evaluation across demographic, cultural and intersectional groups relevant to the intended application, together with the intended use context and the evaluation procedures. [18]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

Detecting and responding to misuse of a coding agent in a published threat report

AnthropicDocumented in a case study

Anthropic's threat intelligence report of August 2025, published as the news post "Detecting and countering misuse of AI: August 2025". It describes several cases in which criminals used Claude, including one in which Claude Code was used to run a data-extortion campaign. [22]

The problem

A capable coding agent can be pointed at multi-step attacks that a human operator would otherwise have to carry out by hand. The misuse works through a legitimate capability, so a better system prompt does not stop it. The provider has to find such activity after launch, stop it, and turn each case into a stronger control.

Where this topic fits

Misuse handling sits in the Manage function of the NIST AI RMF, as post-deployment monitoring and incident response. It feeds back into earlier stages: a detected case becomes a new classifier, a new red-team test and a changed likelihood in the risk register.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. An investigation finds an actor who used Claude Code to automate reconnaissance, harvest victims' credentials and penetrate networks, and who let the model make decisions such as which data to take.
  2. The accounts are banned as soon as the operation is discovered, which contains the activity.
  3. The team builds a tailored classifier, an automated screening tool, and introduces a new detection method, so that similar activity can be flagged earlier.
  4. Technical indicators about the attack are shared with the relevant authorities.
  5. The case is published together with two others, operatives using Claude to obtain remote jobs under false identities and a ransomware service built with Claude's help, so other builders can see the pattern.
  6. A delivery team following this model (our explanation) records each case as a risk-register row with an owner, a new control and a regression test.

Why this approach fits

When the harmful use is a normal-looking use of a legitimate feature, prompt wording cannot separate it from good use. What works is monitoring across many requests, account-level enforcement and a feedback loop into automated detection, which are product and operations controls rather than model changes.

Limitation or tradeoff

A published case study is selective: it shows operations the provider found, which says nothing about the ones it missed. Banning accounts is also reactive, because an actor can try again elsewhere. Detection therefore has to be paired with controls that limit what an account can do in the first place.

What the sources document

Impact assessments, release criteria and Transparency Notes in Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard

MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard v2, General Requirements (external release, June 2022), which turns Microsoft's principles into numbered requirements for product teams. The Azure OpenAI Transparency Note is read alongside it as a published example of the kind of Transparency Note that requirement T2.2 calls for when a system is a platform service. [24][25]

The problem

Principles such as fairness and accountability do not tell an engineering team what to do on a Tuesday. The company needs checkable requirements, named reviewers and review points, applied the same way across many teams and products.

Where this topic fits

The Standard wraps the whole product lifecycle. In NIST terms (our mapping), the impact assessment and sensitive-use screening are Map, the release criteria and evaluations are Measure, the ongoing monitoring and updates are Manage, and the named reviewers and approvals are Govern.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The team completes an Impact Assessment on a standard template (requirement A1.1) and reviews it with the identified reviewers, securing their approvals before development starts (A1.2).
  2. Prompts in the assessment decide whether the system is a Sensitive Use. If it is, the team reports it to the Office of Responsible AI and follows any extra requirements that result (A2.2).
  3. The team identifies demographic groups at risk and defines Responsible Release Criteria for fairness: for each metric a target minimum level for every group and a target maximum difference between groups (F1.4), and for systems that allocate opportunities such as employment a maximum difference in allocation rates (F2.4).
  4. It evaluates the system against those criteria. If a criterion is not met, it consults the reviewers named in the assessment and writes down how the gap will be managed until it is closed (the pattern in A5.7 and T1.6).
  5. It designs the system so that people who use its outputs can interpret them and stay aware of the tendency to over-rely on them (T1.2).
  6. It publishes documentation of capabilities, intended uses and limitations; for a platform service this is a Transparency Note (T2.2).
  7. It updates the Impact Assessment at least annually, when new intended uses are added and before each new release stage (A1.3), and reviews the documentation on similar triggers (T2.3).

Why this approach fits

Writing the criteria before the evaluation removes the temptation to move the line once the numbers are in. Named reviewers and approvals make someone answerable, and the re-review triggers catch the common way systems drift into riskier territory: through new uses, not through the original design.

Limitation or tradeoff

The Standard itself says it is seldom clear how to trade fairness against other performance goals, so release criteria encode a judgement and do not remove it. The document read here is the June 2022 external release; Microsoft's internal requirements may have changed since, and a published standard says little about how strictly it is enforced.

What the sources document

  • Requirement A1.2 says to review the completed Impact Assessment with the identified reviewers before development starts and to secure all required approvals. A1.3 says to update and review it at least annually, when new intended uses are added and before advancing to a new release stage. [24]
  • Requirement A2.2 says to answer prompts in the Impact Assessment template to determine whether the system is a Sensitive Use and, if it is, to report it to the Office of Responsible AI. [24]
  • Requirement F1.4 asks teams to document, for each metric, a target minimum performance level for all groups and a target maximum performance difference between groups. F2.4 asks for a target maximum difference in allocation rates for systems that affect opportunities such as employment. [24]
  • Requirement T2.2 asks for published documentation covering capabilities, intended uses, limitations and evidence of performance, and says a Transparency Note is required when the system is a platform service offered to external customers or partners. [24]
  • The Azure OpenAI Transparency Note, updated in September 2026, advises avoiding scenarios where use or misuse could have a consequential impact on life opportunities or legal status, and recommends meaningful human review and oversight in high-stakes contexts. [25]

The EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and the systemic-risk tier

European Commission and signatory providersDocumented in official docs

The Commission's page on the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, which lists the signatories, and the Safety and Security and Transparency chapters of the Code as published there (July 2025). Article 51 of the AI Act defines when a general-purpose model counts as one with systemic risk. [15][16][17][13][14]

The problem

The AI Act puts heavier duties on the few general-purpose models that could cause systemic harm. Providers need an agreed, regulator-recognised way to show they assess and mitigate those risks and document their models for the teams that build on them, before the legal duties start to apply.

Where this topic fits

This is the tier for general-purpose models, which sits beside the use-based tiers rather than inside them. The model's tier is decided by capability. The tier of the product built on top is still decided by its use, so a downstream hiring tool is high-risk whichever model it calls.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The provider checks whether its model counts as one with systemic risk. Article 51 presumes high-impact capabilities when cumulative training compute is above 10^25 floating point operations, and lets the Commission classify a model on equivalent capability or impact.
  2. It decides how to show compliance. The Code of Practice is a voluntary route with three chapters: Transparency, Copyright, and Safety and Security, the last only for models with systemic risk.
  3. Under the Safety and Security chapter it creates a Safety and Security Framework before the model is placed on the market, identifies and analyses systemic risks, and sets acceptance criteria for deciding whether the risks are acceptable.
  4. It applies safety and security mitigations, reports to the AI Office in a Safety and Security Model Report, and tracks and reports serious incidents.
  5. Under the Transparency chapter it keeps model documentation, gives downstream providers the information meant for them, and gives the AI Office information on request.
  6. The signatories are listed publicly. A downstream team (our explanation) uses the documentation it receives as an input to the card for its own system, and classifies that system by its use.

Why this approach fits

Heavier duties for a few highly capable models avoid putting the same burden on every small model, and a numeric presumption gives providers something they can check before launch. A shared code lets regulators and providers agree on what a risk assessment contains before disputes arise.

Limitation or tradeoff

The Code is voluntary, so a provider can choose another way to show compliance, as the Commission's list shows for the signatory that signed only one chapter. Training compute is a proxy for capability and Article 51 lets the Commission amend the threshold. The Code itself says that following it is not conclusive evidence of compliance with the Act, and the chapters read here are the July 2025 text, so check the Commission's page for later guidance.

What the sources document

  • The Commission describes the Code of Practice as a voluntary tool with three chapters. Transparency and Copyright are open to all general-purpose model providers, and Safety and Security applies only to providers of the most advanced models that are subject to the systemic-risk obligations of Article 55. [15]
  • The signatory list includes Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI. xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter, so it must show compliance with the transparency and copyright duties by other adequate means. [15]
  • The Safety and Security chapter has ten commitments, including a Safety and Security Framework before market placement, a risk acceptance determination and serious incident reporting. Under the Transparency chapter, signatories give downstream providers the documentation meant for them and are only encouraged to consider public disclosure. [16][17]
  • Article 51 presumes high-impact capabilities when cumulative training compute exceeds 10^25 floating point operations, allows the Commission to classify a model on equivalent capability or impact, and lets it amend the threshold by delegated act. [13]
  • The summary of the Act lists duties for providers of general-purpose models (technical documentation, information for downstream providers, a copyright policy and a public summary of training content) and extra duties for models with systemic risk, such as adversarial model evaluations, risk mitigation and incident reporting. [14]

The NIST Generative AI Profile as a route to a liability defence in Texas

State of Texas and NISTDocumented in official docs

The enrolled text of Texas H.B. 149, the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act, which names NIST's Generative AI Profile in Section 552.105(e). NIST's AI RMF overview page describes the Profile and the framework it belongs to. [26][1][5][6][7]

The problem

A company that deploys AI in Texas needs a way to show that it looks for prohibited uses of its systems and acts on what it finds. The statute offers several routes to a defence, and one of them rests on an internal review process that follows a recognised risk management framework.

Where this topic fits

The framework supplies the structure for the internal review. In NIST terms (our mapping), the review is a Govern and Measure activity, and the red-team tests the statute also lists as a discovery route belong to Measure. Fixing what the review finds is Manage.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. In a typical implementation, the team adopts the NIST AI RMF and its Generative AI Profile as the reference for its own controls and names an owner for the review.
  2. It maps each control and record it already has to a framework subcategory, so gaps are visible, for example roles and escalation to GOVERN 2.1 and the switch-off to MANAGE 2.4.
  3. It runs internal reviews and adversarial tests on a schedule and when something changes, and keeps the findings, dates and fixes.
  4. When a review finds a violation, the team corrects it and records what was found and when.
  5. If enforcement follows, counsel (our explanation) uses these records to argue that the violation was found through an internal review under Section 552.105(e).
  6. The team re-checks the framework version regularly, because the statute refers to the most recent version and NIST says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised.

Why this approach fits

A voluntary framework becomes useful when it is tied to a routine that produces dated records. The mapping sheet turns "we follow NIST" from a claim into something a reviewer can open, and it shows which parts of the framework the team has not covered.

Limitation or tradeoff

The defence in this clause depends on the defendant discovering a violation, so documents alone are not a shield. The excerpt read here does not say how much compliance counts as substantial, and the statute's discrimination clause concerns intent and says a disparate impact is not sufficient by itself, so subgroup test results do not map one-to-one onto liability. Other laws, such as the EU AI Act, work differently.

What the sources document

  • The Act states that it takes effect on 1 January 2026. [26]
  • Section 552.105(e) says a defendant may not be found liable if another person uses its AI system in a prohibited manner, or if it discovers a violation through feedback, testing including adversarial or red-team testing, following state agency guidelines, or an internal review process where it substantially complies with the most recent NIST Generative AI Profile or another recognised framework. [26]
  • Section 552.056 prohibits developing or deploying an AI system with the intent to unlawfully discriminate against a protected class, and states that a disparate impact is not sufficient by itself to demonstrate an intent to discriminate. [26]
  • NIST lists its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) as released on 26 July 2024 and says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan. [1]
  • The Playbook states MAP 1.5 as organisational risk tolerances being determined and documented, MEASURE 2.11 as fairness and bias being evaluated with results documented, MANAGE 2.4 as mechanisms to supersede, disengage or deactivate systems that perform inconsistently with intended use, and MANAGE 4.3 as incidents and errors being communicated, tracked and recovered from. 4Common mistakes

  1. Reporting one aggregate accuracy number for the whole test set.

    Why it causes problems
    A pooled metric can pass while one group carries most of the errors, so the harm is invisible in the report that the sign-off relies on. In the first exercise the pooled false positive rate is 0.078 and passes a limit of 0.10, while one group sits at 0.167.
    How to recognise it
    The evaluation report has a single table row per metric and no breakdown by group, or the groups are not named at all. Ask which factor values the metric was computed for.
    How to fix it
    Compute every release metric for each group and for intersections where the sample allows, add confidence intervals, and state the largest acceptable gap before running the evaluation.

  2. Copying the upstream model card into your own product documentation.

    Why it causes problems
    The card describes the model as released, not your prompts, retrieval, tools, data or users. Repeating its claims suggests you tested things you did not, and readers lose sight of what is actually unknown.
    How to recognise it
    Your documentation has no section on your own evaluation, or its limits are word-for-word those of the base model. Check whether every claim has a test of yours behind it.
    How to fix it
    Write a system-level card that cites the upstream card, lists what you evaluated yourself and on which data, and says plainly what was not tested.

  3. Classifying the use case once at kickoff and never again.

    Why it causes problems
    A new data source, user group or integration can move a feature into a higher tier, and the controls chosen for the original tier no longer fit. The change is easy to miss because the model did not change.
    How to recognise it
    The tier is recorded with a date from the first planning meeting, and the feature list has grown since. There is no named trigger that reopens the review.
    How to fix it
    Write the re-review triggers into the decision record: model swap, new data source, new user group, new tool or action, incident, and change in law. Assign one owner to check them before each release.

  4. Choosing the pass thresholds after seeing the results.

    Why it causes problems
    A line drawn around the numbers you already have can always be passed. The review then records a decision that the evidence did not drive, and nobody can tell afterwards what the team would have rejected.
    How to recognise it
    The release criteria are first written in the same document, or on the same day, as the evaluation results. The tolerance statement changes between report versions.
    How to fix it
    Write the metrics, the minimum per group and the maximum gap into a dated criteria sheet before any evaluation run, and keep it under version control with the reviewers' approval.

  5. Building the review board but no way to switch the feature off.

    Why it causes problems
    An incident that needs the feature paused turns into a deployment project. Harm continues while people look for who owns the decision and how to disable the code path.
    How to recognise it
    Ask who can pause the feature, how long that takes and when it was last rehearsed. If the answers are unclear, there is no working switch-off.
    How to fix it
    Add a feature flag or kill switch with a named owner and a runbook, rehearse it before launch, and record it as the mechanism for NIST MANAGE 2.4 in the decision record.

  6. Treating a voluntary framework as proof of legal compliance.

    Why it causes problems
    The NIST AI RMF is voluntary and the EU AI Act is binding, so following the first does not by itself show that you meet the second. A team can have tidy risk records and still miss a duty such as transparency to users.
    How to recognise it
    The compliance section of a design document cites only the framework, with no tier, no role as provider or deployer, and no list of the legal duties that apply.
    How to fix it
    Keep the two jobs separate: use the framework to organise risk work, and keep a legal duties list per jurisdiction, reviewed by counsel, that points to the evidence for each duty.

Hint 1

The false positive rate divides false positives by all actual negatives, which is FP plus TN, not by the group size.

Hint 2

Pool the data by adding the four counts across groups first, then reuse the same function you wrote for a single group.

Hint 3

Compare the two group intervals. If they do not overlap, the difference is unlikely to be sampling noise.

Show solution
python
from math import sqrt

CONFUSION = {
    "A": {"tp": 80, "fn": 20, "fp": 10, "tn": 290},
    "B": {"tp": 111, "fn": 39, "fp": 25, "tn": 125},
}
MAX_FPR = 0.10      # release criterion 1: no group above this
MAX_FPR_GAP = 0.05  # release criterion 2: largest allowed gap between groups
Z95 = 1.96


def wilson_interval(successes, trials, z=Z95):
    p = successes / trials
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * trials)) / (1 + z * z / trials)
    half = z * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / trials + z * z / (4 * trials * trials)) / (1 + z * z / trials)
    return centre - half, centre + half


def group_metrics(c):
    total = sum(c.values())
    negatives = c["fp"] + c["tn"]
    return {
        "n": total,
        "accuracy": (c["tp"] + c["tn"]) / total,
        "fpr": c["fp"] / negatives,
        "fpr_interval": wilson_interval(c["fp"], negatives),
    }


def pooled(confusions):
    return {k: sum(c[k] for c in confusions.values()) for k in ("tp", "fn", "fp", "tn")}


report = {g: group_metrics(c) for g, c in CONFUSION.items()}
report["all"] = group_metrics(pooled(CONFUSION))
for name, m in report.items():
    lo, hi = m["fpr_interval"]
    print(f'{name:>3}  n={m["n"]:<4} accuracy={m["accuracy"]:.3f}  fpr={m["fpr"]:.3f}  95% CI [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]')

fprs = [report[g]["fpr"] for g in CONFUSION]
gap = max(fprs) - min(fprs)
failures = [g for g in CONFUSION if report[g]["fpr"] > MAX_FPR]
print(f"gap={gap:.3f}  groups above {MAX_FPR}: {failures}")
print("decision:", "no-go" if failures or gap > MAX_FPR_GAP else "go")

all  n=700  accuracy=0.866  fpr=0.078  95% CI [0.056, 0.106]

The decision is no-go for two reasons: group B's rate of 0.167 is above the 0.10 limit, and the gap of 0.133 is above 0.05. The two group intervals do not overlap, so the difference is not likely to be noise. The pooled rate of 0.078 would have passed the 0.10 limit, which is exactly why the criteria are applied per group.

A known-limitations statement for the card: "Tested on 700 claims from two groups. The false positive rate is 0.033 for group A and 0.167 for group B, so honest group B claims are flagged about five times as often.
