Fairness and Bias in ML

Fairness in machine learning is the work of measuring whether a model's decisions and errors fall unevenly on different groups of people, and of reducing the gaps that cause harm. Bias is the umbrella word for the data, label and design choices that produce those gaps.

1Overview

Why it matters

A classifier that is right 90% of the time on average can still be wrong far more often for one group of people than for another, because an average hides who carries the errors. In the Gender Shades audit, three commercial gender classifiers had error rates between 20.8% and 34.7% on darker-skinned women and at most 0.8% on lighter-skinned men. A health-risk algorithm used by health systems gave Black patients the same risk score as White patients who were considerably less sick, because it predicted future cost rather than illness.

Neither failure was a bug in the training code. They came from who was sampled, what was chosen as the label and which number was reported. Anyone who ships a model that decides who gets a loan, an interview, a place in a care programme or a bail recommendation needs to recognise these patterns, and increasingly has to document them: New York City requires a published bias audit for automated hiring tools, with enforcement starting on 5 July 2023. Engineers, analysts, product owners and the people who sign off a launch all need the vocabulary here to ask a precise question: fair on which metric, for which groups, with what evidence?

Key terms

Protected (sensitive) attribute
A characteristic such as race, sex, age or disability that law or product policy says decisions should not unfairly depend on. In an audit it defines the groups that are compared.
Proxy
A feature or label that stands in for something not measured directly, such as health cost for health need or arrest for crime. A proxy can also carry information about a protected attribute.
Base rate (prevalence)
The share of a group whose true outcome is positive, for example the share of defendants who are re-arrested within two years.
Selection rate
The share of a group that the model labels positive or selects. Equal selection rates across groups is demographic parity.
Equal opportunity and equalized odds
Equal opportunity asks for equal true positive rates across groups. Equalized odds asks for equal true positive rates and equal false positive rates.
Calibration by group
A score means the same thing in every group, so among people given score s the same share are truly positive whichever group they belong to. For a yes/no decision the matching check is predictive parity, which asks for equal precision across groups.
Disaggregated evaluation
Computing metrics separately for each group, and for intersections such as darker-skinned women, instead of reporting one overall number.
Impact ratio
A group's selection rate divided by the selection rate of the group with the highest rate. US federal employment guidelines generally regard a ratio below four-fifths as evidence of adverse impact.
Model card
A short structured document that states a model's intended use, the data it was evaluated on, metrics broken down by group and its known limits.

2Explanation

Introduction

You already know how to judge a classifier. Build a confusion matrix, then read accuracy, precision and recall from it (see classification metrics). Each of those numbers is an average over everyone in the test set, and an average gives a small group almost no voice: if one group is 5% of the data, a model that is wrong on all of it loses only five points of accuracy. Fairness work starts by splitting the same confusion matrix by group and comparing the results.

In this lesson bias does not mean the bias of the bias-variance trade-off. It means a systematic difference in how a system treats or serves groups of people, traceable to the data, the labels or the way the model is used. Imagine a lender that trains on its past approval decisions. If its loan officers were stricter with applicants from one neighbourhood, the label "was approved" records that strictness, and a model that fits the label perfectly reproduces it. Nothing in the loss function flags the problem, because the loss measures agreement with the label, not whether the label was right.

The diagram is the idea the lesson is built around: bias can enter at every hand-off, and the decisions a system makes change the world that produces its next batch of data. Three questions follow. Where does the unevenness come from? How do we measure it, and why can't every sensible measure be satisfied at once? What can be done before, during and after training, and how do we audit and document the result so that others can check it?

Two limits are worth stating early. This lesson covers measurable differences between groups in the predictions of a model. It is not legal advice, and what counts as unlawful discrimination depends on the country and the kind of decision. And a metric is not a definition of justice: the Fairlearn guide (opens in a new tab) frames fairness as harm to people (allocation, quality-of-service, stereotyping and erasure harms) and treats ML systems as sociotechnical rather than purely technical. The lesson builds on exploratory data analysis, because many problems show up in a group-by table before any model is trained.

How it works

Where the unevenness comes from

Suresh and Guttag's framework for sources of harm (opens in a new tab) names seven places where harm can enter an ML system. Five matter most when you first audit a model:

SourceWhat goes wrongExample from the paper
Historical biasData that is perfectly measured and sampled still reflects an unequal worldWord embeddings trained on text from one decade carry that decade's stereotypes
Representation biasThe development sample under-represents part of the population the model will serveAbout 45% of ImageNet photos were taken in the United States
Measurement biasA label or feature is a proxy that is measured differently, or less accurately, for some groupsArrest used as a proxy for crime when some communities are policed more heavily
Aggregation biasOne model is fitted to groups whose input-to-label relationship differsA general language model missed the meaning of emojis, hashtags and lyrics in one community's tweets
Evaluation biasThe benchmark or the headline metric hides a group's errorsFace benchmarks with few darker-skinned women

The other two are learning bias (modelling choices such as the loss or model compression that widen gaps) and deployment bias (a tool built for one use is used for another). A proxy deserves a second look because it is how bias survives "removing the sensitive column". If postcode, school or arrest history correlate with race or sex, a model can rebuild much of the attribute from them. ProPublica notes that race was not an item on the COMPAS questionnaire, yet error rates still differed sharply by race.

Measuring: one confusion matrix per group

Take the confusion matrix from classification metrics and build one for each group ggg. Four rates do most of the work:

  • Selection rate, Pr⁡(Y^=1∣G=g)\Pr(\hat{Y}=1 \mid G=g)Pr(Y^=1∣G=g): the share of the group the model labels positive.
  • True positive rate (TPR), Pr⁡(Y^=1∣Y=1,G=g)\Pr(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, G=g)Pr(Y^=1∣Y=1,G=g): the share of truly positive members it catches.
  • False positive rate (FPR), Pr⁡(Y^=1∣Y=0,G=g)\Pr(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=0, G=g)Pr(Y^=1∣Y=0,G=g): the share of truly negative members it flags.
  • Positive predictive value (PPV), Pr⁡(Y=1∣Y^=1,G=g)\Pr(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, G=g)Pr(Y=1∣Y^=1,G=g): the share of flagged members who are truly positive.

Each popular criterion asks one of these, or a score-based version of it, to be equal across groups:

Gaps are reported as a difference (largest minus smallest) or a ratio (smallest over largest). The Fairlearn page on common fairness metrics (opens in a new tab) defines demographic parity this way (equalized odds uses the larger of the TPR and FPR gaps) and warns that the four-fifths threshold has no validity outside its US employment-law context. The code below computes the rates and gaps from scratch with the standard library; Fairlearn's MetricFrame (opens in a new tab) does the same grouping at scale.

python
from collections import defaultdict


def confusion(pairs):
    """pairs: iterable of (y_true, y_pred), each 0 or 1."""
    counts = {"tp": 0, "fp": 0, "fn": 0, "tn": 0}
    for y_true, y_pred in pairs:
        counts[("t" if y_true == y_pred else "f") + ("p" if y_pred else "n")] += 1
    return counts


def rates(c):
    return {
        "selection_rate": (c["tp"] + c["fp"]) / sum(c.values()),
        "tpr": c["tp"] / (c["tp"] + c["fn"]),
        "fpr": c["fp"] / (c["fp"] + c["tn"]),
        "ppv": c["tp"] / (c["tp"] + c["fp"]),
    }


def audit(records):
    """records: iterable of (group, y_true, y_pred). Returns per-group rates and gaps."""
    by_group = defaultdict(list)
    for group, y_true, y_pred in records:
        by_group[group].append((y_true, y_pred))
    per_group = {g: rates(confusion(pairs)) for g, pairs in by_group.items()}

    def spread(name):
        values = [r[name] for r in per_group.values()]
        return max(values) - min(values)

    selection = [r["selection_rate"] for r in per_group.values()]
    gaps = {
        "demographic_parity_ratio": min(selection) / max(selection),
        "equal_opportunity_gap": spread("tpr"),
        "fpr_gap": spread("fpr"),
    }
    return per_group, gaps

On a made-up set of eight people per group (group a: three true positives caught, one missed, one false alarm, three true negatives; group b: one caught, two missed, two false alarms, three true negatives) it prints selection rates of 0.5 and 0.375, TPRs of 0.75 and 0.333, FPRs of 0.25 and 0.4, a demographic parity ratio of 0.75, an equal-opportunity gap of 0.417 and an FPR gap of 0.15. Eight people per group is far too few to conclude anything; it is only there so you can check the arithmetic by hand.

Why the criteria conflict

Suppose a model is calibrated by group: among people who get the same score, the same share are truly positive whichever group they are in. Applied to a yes/no decision (flagged or not), that means equal PPV across groups, which Chouldechova calls predictive parity. Bayes' rule ties PPV to the other rates and to the group's prevalence p=Pr⁡(Y=1∣G=g)p = \Pr(Y=1 \mid G=g)p=Pr(Y=1∣G=g):

PPV=TPR pTPR p+FPR (1−p)⟺FPR=p1−p⋅1−PPVPPV⋅(1−FNR)\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{TPR}\,p}{\text{TPR}\,p + \text{FPR}\,(1-p)} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1-\text{FNR})PPV=TPRp+FPR(1−p)TPRp​⟺FPR=1−pp​⋅PPV1−PPV​⋅(1−FNR)

The second form is equation 2.4 in Chouldechova (2017) (opens in a new tab). Give two groups the same PPV and the same FNR and the FPR is fully determined by prevalence: it rises with ppp. Unless the prevalences are equal, or the classifier is perfect, the groups cannot also share an FPR.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (opens in a new tab) prove the general version for risk scores: calibration within groups, balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class can all hold only when prediction is perfect or the groups have equal base rates. Demographic parity clashes with the others for the same reason. A perfect classifier selects each group at its own base rate, so forcing equal selection rates when base rates differ means giving up some correct decisions. Choosing a criterion is therefore a decision about which error matters more, and for whom, not a technical default.

Mitigation: before, during and after training

Before training (pre-processing) changes the data. You can collect more examples of an under-represented group, repair a proxy label (often the cheapest fix when the label is the problem), or reweight examples. AIF360's Reweighing (opens in a new tab) class, based on Kamiran and Calders, weights each (group, label) combination differently. The classic weight is w(g,y)=Pr⁡(G=g)Pr⁡(Y=y) / Pr⁡(G=g,Y=y)w(g,y) = \Pr(G=g)\Pr(Y=y)\,/\,\Pr(G=g, Y=y)w(g,y)=Pr(G=g)Pr(Y=y)/Pr(G=g,Y=y), which makes group and label statistically independent in the weighted data. Fairlearn's CorrelationRemover removes linear correlation between the sensitive feature and the other features, so non-linear proxies can survive.

During training (in-processing) changes the objective. Fairlearn's reductions (opens in a new tab) approach turns a constrained problem into a sequence of weighted classification problems: ExponentiatedGradient retrains the wrapped classifier on a sequence of re-weighted datasets, and GridSearch returns models along the accuracy-versus-constraint trade-off. Supported constraints include demographic parity, equalized odds and true-positive-rate parity. ExponentiatedGradient's predictions are randomized (opens in a new tab), so the same input can get different outputs. Adversarial methods instead train a second network to recover the sensitive attribute and penalise the predictor when it succeeds.

After training (post-processing) changes decisions made from fixed scores. Hardt, Price and Srebro (opens in a new tab) show how to derive a predictor from any score and the group so that equal opportunity or equalized odds holds, using group-specific thresholds and, for equalized odds, sometimes randomisation. Fairlearn's ThresholdOptimizer applies this idea to an existing classifier. It needs no retraining, which is why LinkedIn chose re-ranking (see the examples), but it needs the group at decision time, and treating groups differently at the moment of decision can be restricted by law in some settings, so involve counsel. The Fairlearn mitigation guide (opens in a new tab) adds a caution that applies to all three stages: the algorithms can drive a chosen metric close to zero, but that does not mean the result is fair.

Auditing and documentation

An audit is a repeatable procedure. Define the decision, the harms and the groups. Assemble evaluation data with group labels and enough examples per group. Compute the per-group rates, also for intersections, with counts and uncertainty. Trace large gaps to a source from the table above. Mitigate or redesign, then document and re-run after every retrain.

Documentation makes the audit usable by people who did not run it. A model card (opens in a new tab) is a one-to-two-page record of intended use, evaluation data, metrics disaggregated by the groups and conditions that matter, ethical considerations and caveats. A datasheet for a dataset (opens in a new tab) does the same for the data: its motivation, composition, collection process and recommended uses. Regulation can also demand it: New York City's rule requires a public summary of a hiring tool's bias audit.

Worked example: Auditing COMPAS-style error rates from published figures

This example uses public numbers. Chouldechova (opens in a new tab) reports the re-arrest rate (prevalence) in the Broward County COMPAS data and quotes the error rates that ProPublica observed. ProPublica's methodology (opens in a new tab) counts any score above "low" (a decile score of 5 or more) as higher risk. We scale the rates to 10,000 defendants per group so every cell is a whole number. The counts are therefore reconstructions from rounded percentages, not the original records.

Step 3: score each criterion
  • Demographic parity: the ratio of selection rates is 34.3 / 58.8 = 0.58, well below the 0.8 used in the four-fifths comparison.
  • Equal opportunity: TPRs differ by 20 percentage points.
  • Equalized odds: fails too, because the FPRs also differ, by 22 points.
  • Predictive parity: PPVs differ by 3.4 points (62.5% against 59.1%), so a high-risk flag has about the same precision in both groups, which fits Chouldechova's reading that COMPAS is approximately calibrated.
Step 4: try to repair it

Hold PPV at 0.60 and FNR at 0.35 in both groups and apply the identity from the lesson: FPR = p / (1 − p) × (1 − PPV) / PPV × (1 − FNR). For the Black group this gives 0.51 / 0.49 × 0.40 / 0.60 × 0.65 = 0.451. For the White group it gives 0.39 / 0.61 × 0.40 / 0.60 × 0.65 = 0.277. Equal PPV and equal FNR therefore force unequal FPRs, because the prevalences differ. Going the other way, force FPR = FNR = 0.35 in both groups and Bayes' rule gives PPV = 0.659 for the Black group and 0.543 for the White group: equal error rates, but a "high risk" flag now means different things in the two groups. The curve in the plot earlier in the lesson shows the same relationship for every prevalence.

Reproduce it
python
GROUPS = {"Black": (0.51, 0.28, 0.45), "White": (0.39, 0.48, 0.23)}  # prevalence p, FNR, FPR
N = 10_000


def counts(p, fnr, fpr, n=N):
    positives = round(n * p)
    negatives = n - positives
    fn = round(positives * fnr)
    fp = round(negatives * fpr)
    return {"tp": positives - fn, "fn": fn, "fp": fp, "tn": negatives - fp}


def ppv_from_rates(p, fnr, fpr):
    tpr = 1 - fnr
    return tpr * p / (tpr * p + fpr * (1 - p))


def fpr_from_ppv(p, fnr, ppv):
    return p / (1 - p) * (1 - ppv) / ppv * (1 - fnr)


for name, (p, fnr, fpr) in GROUPS.items():
    c = counts(p, fnr, fpr)
    flagged = c["tp"] + c["fp"]
    print(name, c, f"selection={flagged / N:.3f} tpr={c['tp'] / (c['tp'] + c['fn']):.3f} ppv={c['tp'] / flagged:.3f}")

print("same PPV 0.60 and FNR 0.35 -> FPR:", {n: round(fpr_from_ppv(p, 0.35, 0.60), 3) for n, (p, _, _) in GROUPS.items()})
print("same FPR = FNR = 0.35 -> PPV:", {n: round(ppv_from_rates(p, 0.35, 0.35), 3) for n, (p, _, _) in GROUPS.items()})

The arithmetic does not say which fairness criterion is right. It says that the disagreement between ProPublica, which emphasised error rates, and the responses that emphasised calibration did not come from an error by either side. It followed from the base rates, and someone has to decide which error costs more.

Edge cases and limitations

Small groups give noisy rates
The sample audit in New York City's rule includes a category of 41 applicants, where one person more or fewer selected moves the selection rate by 2.4 points, and an intersectional cell of 17 applicants, where one person moves it by 5.9. Report counts next to rates and add confidence intervals or a bootstrap. The US guideline behind the four-fifths rule itself says that larger gaps based on small, statistically insignificant numbers may not show adverse impact. Treat a gap on a handful of people as a reason to collect more data, not as a finding.
The group labels may be missing or unusable
An audit needs each person's group, but many systems never collected it, and collecting it can be restricted by law or consent. New York City's rule makes the gap visible: the auditor must report how many assessed individuals fall in an unknown category and are left out of the calculations. Options are voluntary self-reported data collected for audit use only, a separate audit sample, or inference. Inferred groups add their own error, which is worst for the small groups you most want to measure.
Labels exist only for people the old system accepted
In lending, repayment is observed only for approved applicants, and in hiring, job performance only for hired candidates. Error rates computed on those rows describe a filtered sample, and the filter may itself be uneven across groups. Where possible keep a small randomised holdout whose outcomes are observed regardless of the model, and say in the documentation which population the metrics describe.
Single-axis averages hide intersectional gaps
In Gender Shades, IBM's classifier had error rates of 20.3% for all women and 22.4% for all darker-skinned subjects, but 34.7% for darker-skinned women. Auditing gender and skin type separately would understate the worst-off cell by 12 to 14 points. The cost of checking intersections is smaller cells, so pair the breakdown with counts and the small-group caution above.
A satisfied metric is not the same as a fair system
Demographic parity can be met by selecting qualified people in one group and unqualified people in another, because the metric looks only at selection rates. Equalized odds can be met by making the model worse for everyone. Fairlearn's guide states plainly that driving a metric to zero does not make a result fair. Pair each metric with a review of the harm it is meant to track and of the decision the model feeds.
Mitigation decays after deployment
Thresholds, weights and constraints are fitted to one period's data. If the prevalence of the outcome, the applicant mix or the score distribution shifts, the gaps reopen without any code change. Put per-group selection rate and error rates on the monitoring dashboard (see [model monitoring and drift](/topics/model-monitoring-and-drift/)) and re-audit after every retrain.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The model's output decides who receives something of value or bears a burden, such as credit, an interview, a care programme, bail conditions or content visibility.
  • You can evaluate by group, either because the attribute is collected with consent or because an audit sample can be built, and every group has enough examples for stable rates.
  • A regulation, customer contract or internal policy requires a reviewable or published audit, as New York City does for automated employment decision tools.
  • The system learns from past human decisions or from proxies such as cost, arrests or clicks, which are the labels most likely to carry historical bias.

Think twice when

  • You want a passed metric to stand as proof that the system is fair or lawful. Legal standards differ by country and decision type and need legal advice.
  • Groups are undefined or so small that every rate is noise. Start with qualitative error analysis of individual cases instead.
  • You would use mitigation to justify deploying a model for a task that is inappropriate, or whose decisions people cannot contest.

A simpler alternative. Start with a disaggregated evaluation: accuracy, false positive rate, false negative rate and selection rate per group, from a pandas groupby or Fairlearn's MetricFrame, plus a look at how the labels were produced. Better data or a better label often removes a gap more cheaply than a fairness algorithm. Reach for constraints or threshold adjustment only when a measured gap persists after that.

Key takeaways

  • Overall accuracy averages over people, so a model can look good while one group carries most of the errors; always report metrics per group, including intersections, with the counts behind them.
  • Bias enters at several stages (history, sampling, labels and proxies, aggregation, evaluation, deployment), and removing the protected attribute does not remove its information from correlated features.
  • Demographic parity, equalized odds and calibration by group measure different things, and when base rates differ and prediction is imperfect they cannot all hold, so choosing one is a decision about which error matters more.
  • Mitigation can act on the data, the training objective or the final decisions; post-processing is model-agnostic but needs the group at decision time, and none of the three guarantees a system is fair.
  • An audit is a repeatable disaggregated evaluation whose results are written down in a model card or audit summary and re-run after every retrain.

Example 1 of 5

COMPAS recidivism scores: similar precision, unequal error rates

ProPublica and Northpointe (COMPAS)Documented in a case study

ProPublica's 2016 investigation obtained COMPAS recidivism risk scores for defendants in Broward County, Florida, and compared them with two-year re-arrest outcomes. Chouldechova's paper re-analyses the same public data and explains why the pattern ProPublica found follows from the groups' base rates. [1][2][3][4][6]

The problem

COMPAS is a risk assessment used in the US criminal justice system to score how likely a defendant is to be re-arrested. ProPublica reported that among defendants who did not reoffend, Black defendants were labelled higher risk much more often than White defendants, and among those who did reoffend, White defendants were more often labelled low risk. Northpointe, the maker, disputed the analysis. The open question was whether the score is fair when one reading (the score means the same thing for both groups) and another (the errors fall equally on both groups) point in opposite directions.

Where this topic fits

This is the evaluation step of the pipeline, done by outsiders. A deployed score is turned into a yes/no decision with a cut-off (ProPublica counts any score above "low", that is 5 or more, as higher risk), a confusion matrix is built for each racial group, and false positive rate, false negative rate and positive predictive value are compared. The identity from this lesson then links the three to each group's prevalence.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Take each defendant's COMPAS score and the outcome observed two years later (re-arrested or not) from the public Broward County records.
  2. Turn the score into a decision with a cut-off, so each defendant is higher or lower risk, and build one confusion matrix per racial group.
  3. Read the false positive rate (non-reoffenders labelled higher risk), the false negative rate and the positive predictive value (the share of those labelled higher risk who did reoffend) for each group.
  4. Compare. ProPublica emphasised the error rates, which differ sharply. Chouldechova emphasised that the score is roughly calibrated, with similar reoffending rates at each score for both groups.
  5. Apply the identity linking FPR, FNR, PPV and prevalence. Because the groups' re-arrest rates in the data differ (51% and 39%), both observations can be true at once and cannot both be removed.
  6. Conclude that the choice is not a bug fix: narrowing the error-rate gap would cost the equal meaning of the score, so the decision has to say which error matters more and for whom.

Why this approach fits

Group metrics from a confusion matrix turn a heated argument into arithmetic. The two sides were not disputing the counts; they were choosing different metrics, and the identity shows each choice has a mechanical consequence. That is why "is this score fair?" cannot be answered with one number, and why the answer in a deployment has to name the metric and the harm it tracks.

Limitation or tradeoff

The outcome is re-arrest, not reoffending, and arrest rates depend on policing. An equal-precision result is therefore calibrated to arrests rather than behaviour, and neither fairness criterion repairs that proxy problem. The analysis also compares only two groups with one cut-off; other cut-offs and other groups would give different error rates, and the scores are from 2013 and 2014, so they say nothing about later versions of the tool.

  • ProPublica obtained the scores from the Broward County Sheriff's Office through a public records request, defined recidivism as a new arrest within two years, grouped scores 1 to 4 as low, 5 to 7 as medium and 8 to 10 as high risk, and marked every score other than low as higher risk in its error-rate analysis.   • Northpointe disputed the analysis, a company founder said that omitting factors tied to race, such as poverty and joblessness, would lower the assessment's accuracy, and the article notes that race was not one of the questions asked. [1]
  • Chouldechova shows that when prevalence differs between groups, a score with equal positive predictive value in both groups cannot also have equal false positive and false negative rates. In her data, re-arrest rates were 51% for Black and 39% for White defendants, and COMPAS appears to adhere well to test fairness (calibration). Kleinberg and colleagues describe the critique of COMPAS as resting on violated balance conditions and the rebuttal as showing that it satisfies calibration. [3][4]
  • Suresh and Guttag cite COMPAS-style models as an example of measurement bias: arrest is a proxy for crime that is measured differently in more heavily policed communities, and the resulting model had a higher false positive rate for Black defendants. [6]

Example 2 of 5

A care-management risk score that predicted cost instead of health need

Documented in a paper

A 2019 Science paper by Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli and Mullainathan audited a widely used commercial algorithm that health systems rely on to identify patients with complex health needs for extra help. The publisher's page returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching, so the full article was read from a copy hosted by the US Federal Trade Commission, alongside a summary from a Berkeley research centre. [11][12][13]

The problem

Health systems use prediction algorithms to decide which patients are offered additional help. The audit asked whether patients who received the same risk score had the same health needs regardless of race. In one health system's data (6,079 Black and 43,539 White patients) they did not: at the 97th percentile of the score, where patients are automatically identified for the care programme, Black patients had 4.8 active chronic conditions on average against 3.8 for White patients. The score therefore placed Black patients lower in the queue for help than their health warranted.

Where this topic fits

The concept sits at the label, which is where measurement bias enters. The algorithm's target was future health care cost, chosen as a stand-in for health need. The audit is an evaluation by group: patients are compared at the same score using a measure of illness that was not the training label, which is a calibration check against a second outcome. Checked against the cost label itself, the same score looked calibrated, so the two checks disagree and the disagreement points at the label.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The algorithm scores each patient. In the health system studied, patients above the 97th percentile of risk are automatically identified for the care programme, and those above the 55th percentile are referred to their physician for consideration.
  2. Auditors hold the score fixed and compare patients of different races using measures of illness that were not the training label: the number of active chronic conditions, and biomarkers such as blood pressure, HbA1c, cholesterol and creatinine.
  3. They find that at the same score Black patients are sicker, so the score under-ranks them for extra help. At the 97th percentile the gap is 4.8 against 3.8 active chronic conditions.
  4. They also check the training label. At every level of predicted risk, Black and White patients have roughly the same costs the following year, so the score is well calibrated for cost and a calibration-by-group check on the training target would have passed.
  5. They trace the cause to the label. The algorithm predicts cost, and less money is spent on Black patients with the same level of need, so cost understates their need. The paper discusses unequal access to care as one of the channels.
  6. A counterfactual simulation swaps healthier White patients just above a risk cut-off for sicker Black patients just below it until the two are equally sick at the margin. At the 97th percentile the share of Black patients among those auto-identified would rise from 17.7% to 46.5%.
  7. The manufacturer reproduced the analysis on its own national dataset of 3,695,943 commercially insured patients and, working with the authors, kept the existing model infrastructure but changed the label to an index combining health prediction with cost prediction. On that dataset the excess chronic conditions among Black patients at a given score fell by 84%.

Why this approach fits

A calibration-style check against a second, more direct outcome catches errors that the training metric cannot. A cost model that predicts cost well looks accurate while ranking people unfairly, and the paper reports exactly this pattern: costs matched across races at each score, but illness did not. Because the fault is in the label, training-time constraints would only enforce parity on a flawed target, so the repair has to start with the label.

Limitation or tradeoff

A better label is not always available. Need is hard to observe directly, and every substitute, such as diagnoses or visits, can also be measured unevenly across groups. The audit needs the group attribute and an independent measure of need, which many deployments lack. The 17.7% to 46.5% figure comes from a counterfactual simulation, not from a deployed change, and the 84% reduction is for one tested replacement label on the manufacturer's dataset of commercially insured patients, so neither number transfers to other systems.

What the sources document

  • The study examined a widely used commercial algorithm that health systems rely on to identify patients with complex health needs, which the authors say affects millions of patients. In the health system studied, patients above the 97th percentile of risk are automatically identified for a care programme. [11]
  • At the same risk score, Black patients were sicker than White patients, with signs of uncontrolled illness; at the 97th percentile of risk the authors report 4.8 active chronic conditions for Black patients against 3.8 for White patients. [11]
  • At every level of algorithm-predicted risk, Black and White patients had roughly the same costs the following year, so predictions were well calibrated for cost, yet the authors conclude that cost, an apparently effective proxy for health, still produced large racial biases. [11]
  • The bias arises because the algorithm predicts health care costs rather than illness, and less money is spent on Black patients than on equally sick White patients. The authors estimate that remedying the disparity would raise the share of Black patients receiving additional help from 17.7% to 46.5%. [11][13]
  • Retraining with a label that combined health prediction with cost prediction cut the excess chronic conditions among Black patients at a given risk score by 84% in the manufacturer's national dataset. The Berkeley summary says reformulating the algorithm so it no longer uses costs as a proxy for needs eliminates the racial bias in predicting who needs extra care. [11][13]

Gender Shades: auditing commercial face analysis by skin type and gender

Documented in a paper

Buolamwini and Gebru audited three commercial gender classification services (Microsoft, IBM and Face++) in a 2018 conference paper. They first measured who appears in two common face benchmarks and then built a benchmark balanced by gender and skin type on which to test the services. [9][6]

The problem

The benchmarks commonly used for face analysis were overwhelmingly lighter-skinned (79.6% of IJB-A and 86.2% of Adience subjects), so errors on darker-skinned women barely moved any average. The three vendors audited published no performance metrics on existing gender-estimation benchmarks in their documentation, so customers could not tell whether the services worked equally well for lighter and darker-skinned men and women.

Where this topic fits

This is representation bias and evaluation bias, and the remedy is disaggregated evaluation. The authors built a benchmark that covers every combination of gender and skin type and reported each combination separately.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Label benchmark images by Fitzpatrick skin type (grouped into lighter and darker) and by gender, so the composition of each dataset becomes measurable.
  2. Build the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark from 1,270 parliamentarians in three African and three European countries chosen for gender parity, giving 53.6% lighter and 46.4% darker-skinned subjects.
  3. Send every image to each vendor's service and record the predicted gender.
  4. Compute error rate, true positive rate, false positive rate and positive predictive value for the whole set, for each gender, for each skin group and for the four intersections.
  5. Publish the subgroup table. The aggregate error rates (6.3% to 12.1%) looked acceptable, but the darker female cell was far worse for every vendor.

Why this approach fits

A single accuracy figure is dominated by the largest group in the test set. Rebalancing the benchmark and reporting every intersection makes the smallest, worst-served group as visible as the largest, and it gives vendors a concrete number to improve. The intersection also matters: for every vendor the error rate for darker-skinned women is higher than the rate for women overall or for darker-skinned subjects overall.

Limitation or tradeoff

The audit covers one task, binary gender classification from face images, on one benchmark of parliamentarians, who are not a random sample of any population. Results for other tasks, image conditions or later versions of the services may differ. The paper documents the gap but does not say how to close it, and skin type is only a proxy for the groups that matter in a given deployment.

What the sources document

  • The two common benchmarks IJB-A and Adience are overwhelmingly lighter-skinned, at 79.6% and 86.2% of subjects by the Fitzpatrick classification, and the authors introduce a new dataset balanced by gender and skin type. [9]
  • The Pilot Parliaments Benchmark has 1,270 individuals from Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Iceland, Finland and Sweden, selected for gender parity in national parliaments, and is 53.6% lighter-skinned and 46.4% darker-skinned. [9]
  • Across the three commercial classifiers, darker-skinned females were the most misclassified group, with error rates up to 34.7%, while the maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males was 0.8%. [9]
  • None of the three vendors reported performance metrics on existing gender-estimation benchmarks in its documentation, and the Face++ terms of use disclaim any warranty of accuracy. [9]
  • Suresh and Guttag cite this audit as evaluation bias: images of darker-skinned women made up only 7.4% and 4.4% of the Adience and IJB-A benchmarks, so benchmarking on them did not penalise underperformance on that group. [6]

Example 4 of 5

LinkedIn Recruiter: re-ranking search results toward a target gender mix

LinkedInDocumented in a paper

Geyik, Ambler and Kenthapadi of LinkedIn describe, in a 2019 KDD paper, measures for bias in ranked lists of people and re-ranking algorithms that were deployed in LinkedIn Recruiter, the product in which recruiters search for candidates. [10]

The problem

A recruiter's search returns many candidates ranked by a machine-learned score. If qualified candidates of different genders are ranked unevenly, the top positions that attract the most attention under-represent one group. The team wanted the top of each ranked list to reflect the gender mix of the candidates who qualify for that search, without retraining its ranking models and without hurting the recruiters' success metrics.

Where this topic fits

This is post-processing. After the ranking models produce scores, a re-ranker takes the top candidates, their scores and the gender distribution of the qualified candidates, and reorders them so every prefix of the list approaches that distribution. Training is untouched, so the same step can sit behind different ranking models.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. For each search request, retrieve the matching candidates and score them with the existing ranking models.
  2. Set the desired gender distribution for the request to the gender distribution of the candidates who qualify for it, which the paper relates to equality of opportunity.
  3. Measure bias with skew, the log of a group's share in the top k divided by its desired share, and with MinSkew, the skew of the most disadvantaged group.
  4. Re-rank with DetGreedy. At each position, if some group is below its minimum count, take the best remaining candidate among those groups; otherwise take the best remaining candidate among groups still under their maximum count.
  5. In the deployed two-level architecture, a representative re-ranker runs after the first-level ranking and again after the second-level ranking.
  6. Run an A/B test with half of Recruiter users on the fair ranking, compare fairness and business metrics, and ramp up when business metrics hold.

Why this approach fits

Post-processing leaves the trained model alone, so one fairness step can sit behind several ranking models and products, and it can run as the last step, after any business logic that prunes candidates. A greedy rule that prefers the highest-scoring candidate among the groups that still need representation keeps the list close to the original ranking, which is why a small effect on business metrics was plausible and why it was still tested in an A/B experiment.

Limitation or tradeoff

Re-ranking can only reorder candidates the first stage retrieved, so it cannot fix a skewed candidate pool. It needs a gender value for each candidate at ranking time, and the paper's guarantees for DetGreedy hold for attributes with up to three values. Equal representation in the top results does not guarantee equal outcomes later, such as replies or hires, and the paper itself recommends A/B testing to choose between algorithms because none is best everywhere.

What the sources document

  • The paper defines skew as the log ratio of a group's share in the top k results to its desired share, and presents algorithms that re-rank results toward a desired distribution over protected attributes. Choosing the desired distribution can express equality of opportunity or demographic parity. [10]
  • In the deployment the desired gender distribution for a search is the gender distribution of the candidates who qualify for it, and the DetGreedy algorithm was used online. [10]
  • In a three-week A/B test in 2018 with hundreds of thousands of Recruiter users, the share of queries with gender-representative results rose from 33% to 95%, and the average MinSkew at 100 candidates moved from -0.259 to -0.011. [10]
  • The authors observed no statistically significant change in business metrics such as InMails sent and accepted, and the approach was ramped to 100% of Recruiter users worldwide. [10]
  • The authors chose post-processing because it is agnostic to the specifics of each model, because business logic such as pruning candidates is applied to model output so mitigation works best as the last step, and because a stand-alone component is easier to add than changing training or features. [10]

New York City Local Law 144: published bias audits of hiring tools

NYC Department of Consumer and Worker ProtectionDocumented in official docs

Local Law 144 of 2021 bars employers and employment agencies from using an automated employment decision tool unless it has had a bias audit within a year, audit information is public and notices were given. The Department of Consumer and Worker Protection's adopted rule defines the audit calculations and includes a worked example. The Department stated that enforcement would begin on 5 July 2023. [14][15][16]

The problem

Employers use tools that screen or rank candidates, and neither candidates nor regulators can see whether selection differs by sex or race and ethnicity. The city needed an audit that is concrete enough for an independent auditor to run, comparable across tools, and published.

Where this topic fits

The audit sits after the tool is built and runs on historical or test data. For each protected category it computes the selection rate (or, for tools that output scores, the scoring rate), then divides by the highest category's rate to get an impact ratio. The rule says this is consistent with the EEOC's four-fifths comparison. It is a demographic-parity ratio computed from decisions and group membership alone.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Collect applicant-level results for the tool, from historical data or test data, with each person's sex and race and ethnicity category.
  2. For each category compute the selection rate: the number selected or classified divided by the number of applicants in the category. For score-based tools compute the scoring rate, the share scoring above the sample's median.
  3. Divide each category's rate by the highest category's rate to get the impact ratio, separately for sex, for race and ethnicity, and for every intersection of the two.
  4. Count and report the individuals who fall in an unknown category and so are left out of the calculations.
  5. Optionally exclude a category under 2% of the data, but then the summary must give the auditor's justification and that category's number of applicants and rate.
  6. Have an independent auditor run the audit, publish the summary, and repeat it within a year of continued use.

Why this approach fits

The impact ratio needs only the tool's decisions and each person's group, not access to the model or its training labels, so an outside auditor can compute it for any vendor's tool and the results are comparable. Publishing the numbers gives candidates, employers and regulators a shared reference.

Limitation or tradeoff

The adopted rule text we read defines what to calculate and publish but sets no pass or fail cut-off for the ratio. Because the ratio is a demographic-parity measure, it cannot see whether the selected people were qualified, so a tool can have equal selection rates and unequal error rates. Categories with few applicants give unstable ratios, and the audit covers only the sex and race and ethnicity categories the rule names.

What the sources document

  • Local Law 144 prohibits using an automated employment decision tool unless it has been subject to a bias audit within one year of use, information about the audit is publicly available and certain notices have been given to employees or candidates. [14][15]
  • The rule defines selection rate as the rate at which individuals in a category are selected or assigned a classification, and impact ratio as a category's selection rate divided by that of the most selected category (or, for scores, the scoring rate over the highest scoring rate). [14]
  • The audit must calculate selection rate and impact ratio separately for sex categories, race and ethnicity categories and intersectional categories, and must state how many assessed individuals fell in an unknown category and were left out. [14]
  • The rule's worked example shows 1,390 male applicants with 667 selected (48%) and 1,181 female applicants with 555 selected (47%), giving impact ratios of 1.00 and 0.979. [14]
  • The Department says these calculations are consistent with Section 1607.4 of the EEOC Uniform Guidelines, under which a selection rate below four-fifths of the highest group's rate is generally regarded as evidence of adverse impact, with caveats for small samples. [14][16]

4Common mistakes

  1. Deleting the protected attribute from the features and calling the model fair.

    Why it causes problems
    Correlated features such as postcode, school, arrest history or cost let the model rebuild much of the attribute, so the gaps remain, and you have lost the column you need to measure them. ProPublica notes that race was not an item on the COMPAS questionnaire, yet error rates still differed by race.
    How to recognise it
    Keep the attribute aside for evaluation and compute metrics by group. As a second check, train a small model to predict the attribute from the remaining features; if it does well, proxies are present.
    How to fix it
    Keep the attribute for audit under your privacy and legal rules, check for proxies, and choose mitigation from measured gaps rather than from which columns were removed.

  2. Reporting one overall accuracy or AUC and stopping there.

    Why it causes problems
    An average is dominated by the biggest group. In Gender Shades, IBM's overall error rate of 12.1% sat beside 34.7% for darker-skinned women, and the benchmarks in use at the time were mostly lighter-skinned.
    How to recognise it
    The evaluation report has no per-group table, or groups are defined on a single axis only, or no counts are shown next to the rates.
    How to fix it
    Report selection rate, TPR, FPR and PPV for each group and each intersection large enough to measure, with counts and confidence intervals, in the same table as the headline metric.

  3. Trying to satisfy every fairness metric, or picking whichever is easiest to compute.

    Why it causes problems
    When base rates differ and the model is imperfect, calibration and equal error rates cannot both hold, so chasing all of them wastes effort or hides a silent choice. Picking demographic parity because it needs no labels can conceal real differences in who is wrongly rejected.
    How to recognise it
    Nobody can say which error is worse for the affected people, or the metric was chosen after seeing which one looked best.
    How to fix it
    Decide before measuring which harm matters: a wrongly denied benefit (equal opportunity), a wrongly flagged person (FPR) or the meaning of a score (calibration). Write the choice and its rationale in the model card and review it with people who know the domain.

  4. Applying the four-fifths rule as a universal pass/fail test.

    Why it causes problems
    The rule is a US employment-law rule of thumb, and Fairlearn's guide warns that it has no validity outside that context. It is also unreliable on small groups, where one person can swing the ratio.
    How to recognise it
    A report says "passes because every ratio is at least 0.8" with no decision type, counts or intervals.
    How to fix it
    Use the impact ratio as a screening signal beside counts and intervals, and set thresholds for your own setting with legal and domain input.

  5. Fixing a fairness gap once and never checking it again.

    Why it causes problems
    Thresholds, weights and constraints are fitted to one period's data. When prevalence, the applicant mix or the score distribution shifts, the gaps reopen with no code change and nobody notices.
    How to recognise it
    Per-group metrics appear in the launch review but not on the monitoring dashboard, and nobody owns the re-audit date.
    How to fix it
    Add per-group selection rate and error rates to monitoring with alert thresholds, and schedule a re-audit after every retrain, policy change or shift in who applies.

     (0.42, 0), (0.39, 1), (0.36, 0), (0.33, 0), (0.28, 1), (0.25, 0), (0.20, 0), (0.17, 0), (0.12, 0), (0.05, 0)]


def rates(rows, threshold):
    tp = sum(1 for s, y in rows if s >= threshold and y == 1)
    fp = sum(1 for s, y in rows if s >= threshold and y == 0)
    fn = sum(1 for s, y in rows if s < threshold and y == 1)
    tn = sum(1 for s, y in rows if s < threshold and y == 0)
    return {
        "selection": (tp + fp) / len(rows),
        "tpr": tp / (tp + fn),
        "fpr": fp / (fp + tn),
        "ppv": tp / (tp + fp),
    }


def show(label, r):
    print(f"{label:<14} selection={r['selection']:.3f} tpr={r['tpr']:.3f} fpr={r['fpr']:.3f} ppv={r['ppv']:.3f}")


a, b = rates(A, 0.5), rates(B, 0.5)
show("A at 0.50", a)
show("B at 0.50", b)
print(f"demographic parity ratio (B/A) = {b['selection'] / a['selection']:.3f}")
print(f"TPR gap (A-B) = {a['tpr'] - b['tpr']:.3f}   FPR gap (B-A) = {b['fpr'] - a['fpr']:.3f}")

best = min((s for s, _ in B), key=lambda t: abs(rates(B, t)["tpr"] - a["tpr"]))
b2 = rates(B, best)
print(f"best threshold for B = {best}")
show("B at best", b2)
print(f"demographic parity ratio (B/A) = {b2['selection'] / a['selection']:.3f}")
print(f"TPR gap (A-B) = {a['tpr'] - b2['tpr']:.3f}   FPR gap (B-A) = {b2['fpr'] - a['fpr']:.3f}")
print(f"one more positive in B moves TPR by {1 / sum(y for _, y in B):.3f}")

Y's gap against X is unlikely to be noise (z about 2.85, p about 0.004). Z's is not clearly distinguishable from noise at the 5% level (z about 1.87, p about 0.06) and its 95% interval for the selection rate runs from about 14% to 40%. No category is under 2% of the data (Z is 4%), so none can be excluded, and the 12 applicants with an unknown category are reported as a count. The model card names its intended use, the categories and their intersections as factors, selection rate and impact ratio with counts and intervals as metrics, and the small-sample and unknown-category issues as caveats.

Hint 1

The highest selection rate is the denominator for every ratio, so find it first.

Hint 2

For a proportion p from n people, a rough 95% interval is p plus or minus 1.96 times the square root of p(1-p)/n; the Wilson interval is better for small n.

Hint 3

Think about which error the harm in this setting comes from: a qualified person screened out, or an unqualified person invited.

Show solution

Rates and ratios are rate divided by 0.400: X 200/500 = 0.400 (1.000), Y 90/300 = 0.300 (0.750), Z 10/40 = 0.250 (0.625), W 56/160 = 0.350 (0.875). Y and Z are below the 0.8 line.

Whether the gaps could be noise: comparing Y with X, the pooled rate is 290/800 = 0.3625, the standard error is sqrt(0.3625 × 0.6375 × (1/500 + 1/300)) = 0.0351 and z = 0.10 / 0.0351 = 2.85 (p about 0.004), so this gap is very unlikely to be chance. Comparing Z with X, the pooled rate is 210/540 = 0.389, the standard error is 0.080 and z = 0.15 / 0.080 = 1.87 (p about 0.06). Z has the lowest ratio but only 40 people: one person is 2.5 points of selection rate, and the Wilson interval for 10/40 runs from about 14% to 40%, which contains X's rate. The right response is not to dismiss Z but to say that the data cannot yet tell, and to collect more applicants. This is why the US guideline behind the four-fifths rule carries a caveat about small, statistically insignificant differences.

Exclusion: the rule allows an independent auditor to leave out a category under 2% of the data from the impact-ratio calculations. Z is 40 of 1,000, which is 4%, so no category qualifies. Had one qualified, the summary would have to include the auditor's justification and that category's number of applicants and selection rate. The 12 applicants with no stated category are reported as a count of individuals left out of the calculations.

A model card outline:

text
Intended use      Stage-one resume screening to decide who is invited to interview, used by recruiters.
                  Out of scope: final hiring decisions, roles outside the audited job families.
Factors           Categories X, Y, Z, W and intersections with any second attribute where n is large
                  enough to measure; resume format and job family as conditions that may change accuracy.
Metrics           Selection rate and impact ratio per category with counts and 95% intervals. If a
                  qualification label exists, TPR among qualified applicants per category, because the
                  harm is a qualified person screened out. Overall precision for context.
Evaluation data   Audit sample of 1,000 applicants plus 12 with unknown category; how it was collected.
Caveats           Z is small (40) and cannot support a conclusion; unknown-category applicants are
                  excluded; results hold for this period and must be re-run after every retrain.

The criterion to monitor: if the harm is a qualified candidate being screened out, equal opportunity (equal TPR among qualified applicants) tracks it best, with the impact ratio kept as the regulatory signal. If no qualification label exists, the impact ratio plus a review of the features for proxies is the honest minimum, and the card should say so.

Check your answer

  • Did you divide every rate by the highest rate (0.400) and find that Y is 0.750 and Z is 0.625?
  • Did you report that Y's gap is unlikely to be noise while Z's is inconclusive, and say why counts matter?
  • Did you check that no category falls under 2% before considering an exclusion, and state what must be reported if one did?
  • Does your model card say which error the harm comes from and which metric tracks it?

6Curated resources

Chosen for accuracy and usefulness, not search rank. Time figures are estimates.

  • Fairness in Machine Learning (opens in a new tab)

    Fairlearn project

    DocumentationBeginnerFreeAbout 30 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A plain account of the kinds of harm an AI system can cause (allocation, quality-of-service, stereotyping and erasure), how group fairness assessment is framed, and why fairness is a sociotechnical problem rather than a single number.
    When to use it
    Open it first, before choosing any metric, to decide which harms apply to the system you are building.

  • A Framework for Understanding Sources of Harm throughout the Machine Learning Life Cycle (opens in a new tab)

    arXiv (Suresh, Guttag)

    PaperIntermediateFreeAbout 45 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A seven-part framework that places each source of harm at a stage of the ML life cycle, with a worked example for each and guidance on which mitigations suit which source.
    When to use it
    Open it when an audit has found a gap and you need to work out where in the pipeline it came from.

  • Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments (opens in a new tab)

    arXiv (Alexandra Chouldechova)

    PaperIntermediateFreeAbout 40 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The identity linking false positive rate, false negative rate, precision and prevalence, its consequence for calibrated scores, and an analysis of the Broward County COMPAS data that makes the lesson's worked example concrete.
    When to use it
    Open it after the worked example if you want the derivation and the discussion of disparate impact.

  • Model Cards for Model Reporting (opens in a new tab)

    arXiv (Mitchell et al.)

    PaperBeginnerFreeAbout 40 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The nine sections of a model card, the argument for reporting metrics broken down by group and by intersection, and two complete example cards for a smiling classifier and a toxicity scorer.
    When to use it
    Open it when you are ready to write up an audit and want a template that others will recognise.

  • Mitigations (opens in a new tab)

    Fairlearn project

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 30 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The Fairlearn mitigation algorithms grouped as pre-processing, reductions and post-processing, the constraints each supports, and the caution that driving a metric to zero does not make a result fair.
    When to use it
    Open it when a measured gap persists and you need to pick a mitigation technique and understand its limits.
