Linear Regression

Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of input features plus a constant. Least squares chooses the weights that make the total of the squared gaps between the predictions and the observed values as small as possible.

1Overview

Why it matters

Linear regression is the first model to reach for when the answer is a number: a delivery time, an energy bill, a calibration reading, next week's disk usage. It is also the yardstick for everything more complex. If a boosted-tree model cannot beat a least-squares fit on held-out data, the extra complexity has bought nothing.

Most of what goes wrong is not in the fitting, which a library does in one call, but in the reading. A coefficient is quoted as "the effect" of a feature when it only describes an association with the other features held fixed. A fit is accepted on a high R² although its residuals show a curve it missed. A line fitted on one range is trusted far outside it. Analysts, ML engineers and anyone who signs off a forecast or an A/B test result need to recognise these three failures, because the same coefficients and residual plots reappear in logistic regression, neural networks and experiment analysis.

Key terms

Feature
An input column the model uses to make its prediction, such as distance in kilometres. Also called a predictor or explanatory variable.
Target
The number the model is trained to predict, such as delivery time in minutes. Also called the label or response.
Coefficient (weight)
The number a feature is multiplied by. It is the change in the prediction when that feature rises by one unit and the other features stay fixed.
Intercept (bias)
The constant added to every prediction. It equals the prediction when every feature is zero.
Residual
The observed value minus the predicted value for one row. Positive means the model predicted too low.
Least squares
The rule for choosing coefficients that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible.
Hyperplane
The flat surface a linear model describes when it has several features. With one feature it is a line and with two it is a plane.
R² (coefficient of determination)
One minus the model's squared error divided by the squared error of always predicting the mean. 1 is a perfect fit and 0 is no better than the mean.
Polynomial feature
A new column made by raising a feature to a power or multiplying features together, so that a linear model can follow a curve.
Multicollinearity
Two or more features carrying nearly the same information, which makes their individual coefficients unstable even when predictions are fine.

2Explanation

Introduction

You have already built this model by hand. Plot a handful of points, lay a ruler across them so that it passes as close to all of them as it can, and read a new value off the ruler. Linear regression is that ruler with two improvements: a precise definition of "as close as it can", and a way to do the same thing when the answer depends on several inputs at once.

Take six deliveries made by one courier. For each we know the distance in kilometres and how many minutes it took. Distance is the feature (the input) and minutes is the target (the number to predict). The model is the school equation of a line with new names:

y^=b+wx\hat{y} = b + w xy^​=b+wx

Here y^\hat{y}y^​ is the predicted minutes, xxx is the distance, www is the coefficient or weight (minutes added per extra kilometre) and bbb is the intercept (the prediction at zero distance). For the six deliveries below, least squares gives y^=6+2.5x\hat{y} = 6 + 2.5xy^​=6+2.5x: about six minutes of fixed overhead plus two and a half minutes per kilometre.

No delivery sits exactly on the line. The vertical gap between an observed value and the line's prediction is a residual. Least squares chooses the line whose squared residuals add up to the smallest possible total. The residuals that remain are also the main evidence for judging whether a straight line was a reasonable model in the first place.

With two features, say distance and number of stops, the line becomes a plane. With more it becomes a hyperplane, which is only the name for a flat surface in more than three dimensions. The arithmetic stays the same: multiply each feature by its coefficient and add.

The rest of this lesson covers how the fit is computed, how to read coefficients without over-reading them, how squared terms let the same method fit curves, and which assumptions sit underneath, together with the residual plots that test them. It relies on train/test splits and cross-validation for judging a fit on data it has not seen, and it prepares the ground for gradient descent, which reaches the same coefficients by iteration.

How it works

The model is a weighted sum

With ppp features, the prediction for one row is

y^=b+w1x1+w2x2+⋯+wpxp\hat{y} = b + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_p x_py^​=b+w1​x1​+w2​x2​+⋯+wp​xp​

"Linear" describes how the coefficients enter: each one multiplies something, and the pieces are added. The features themselves can be anything computed from the data, including x2x^2x2 or ln⁡x\ln xlnx. The NIST handbook (opens in a new tab) calls such models linear in the parameters, and that property is what lets the same method fit curves later in this lesson.

Least squares: what "best" means

For row iii the residual is ei=yi−y^ie_i = y_i - \hat{y}_iei​=yi​−y^​i​. Least squares picks the coefficients that minimise the sum of squared errors:

SSE=∑i=1n(yi−y^i)2\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2SSE=i=1∑n​(yi​−y^​i​)2

Squaring treats misses above and below the line alike, penalises one large miss more than several small ones, and leads to a problem with an exact solution. With one feature the solution is two formulas:

w=∑(xi−xˉ)(yi−yˉ)∑(xi−xˉ)2,b=yˉ−w xˉw = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad b = \bar{y} - w\,\bar{x}w=∑(xi​−xˉ)2∑(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​)​,b=yˉ​−wxˉ

The slope is "how much xxx and yyy vary together" divided by "how much xxx varies". The intercept formula forces the line through the point of means (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​).

With several features, stack the rows into a matrix XXX whose first column is all ones (that column carries the intercept) and the targets into a vector yyy. Setting the derivative of SSE with respect to each coefficient to zero gives the normal equations:

X⊤X w=X⊤yX^\top X\, w = X^\top yX⊤Xw=X⊤y

That is p+1p + 1p+1 linear equations in p+1p + 1p+1 unknowns. Libraries avoid inverting X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X directly because it is numerically fragile. scikit-learn's LinearRegression (opens in a new tab) wraps scipy.linalg.lstsq, and its user guide (opens in a new tab) describes a solution based on the singular value decomposition of XXX, with a cost that grows as rows times features squared.

python
import numpy as np

def fit_least_squares(X, y):
    """Return [intercept, w1, ..., wp] that minimise the sum of squared residuals."""
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])   # the column of ones carries the intercept
    w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)   # SVD-based solver
    return w

Two properties of the solution are handy for checking your work. When the model has an intercept, the residuals sum to zero, and they are uncorrelated with every feature in the model.

Fit quality is usually summarised as R2=1−SSE/SSTR^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}R2=1−SSE/SST, where SST=∑(yi−yˉ)2\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2SST=∑(yi​−yˉ​)2 is the squared error of always predicting the mean. A value of 0 means no better than the mean and 1 means a perfect fit; on held-out data it can be negative. Other error measures are covered in regression metrics.

Reading the coefficients

A coefficient has units: target units per one unit of its feature. Four rules keep the interpretation honest.

  • The other features are held fixed. In a multi-feature model, wjw_jwj​ is the change in the prediction when feature jjj rises by one unit and the rest stay where they are. It is not the slope you would get by plotting yyy against that feature alone, and it changes when a correlated feature is added or removed. The worked example shows a coefficient moving from 2.5 to 1.96 for this reason.
  • The intercept is the prediction when every feature is zero. That point is often outside the data (a flat with zero floor area), so the number can be meaningless on its own. Subtracting each feature's mean first, called centring, turns the intercept into the prediction for an average row.
  • Size depends on units. Converting a feature from metres to kilometres multiplies its coefficient by 1,000 and changes no prediction. To compare the influence of features, standardise them first or multiply each coefficient by its feature's standard deviation, as scikit-learn's guide to coefficient pitfalls (opens in a new tab) recommends.
  • Association is not causation. A coefficient describes a pattern in the data that was collected. If something unobserved drives both the feature and the target, the coefficient absorbs it.

When two features carry nearly the same information (multicollinearity), the data cannot decide how to split the credit between them. Predictions stay accurate, but the individual coefficients swing from sample to sample and can flip sign.

Polynomial features: curves from a linear model

If the relationship bends, add columns. A quadratic in one feature is

y^=b+w1x+w2x2\hat{y} = b + w_1 x + w_2 x^2y^​=b+w1​x+w2​x2

which is still a weighted sum, now of the two columns xxx and x2x^2x2, so the same solver applies. The data below are illustrative stopping distances. They follow the textbook formula d=v/3.6+v2/(254×0.7)d = v/3.6 + v^2/(254 \times 0.7)d=v/3.6+v2/(254×0.7) metres with vvv in km/h (the distance covered during one second of reaction time, plus a braking distance at a friction coefficient of 0.7) to within one metre of added noise. They are not measurements.

Assumptions and residual checks

Least squares returns coefficients for any data. Whether those coefficients, and the intervals and p-values printed beside them, mean anything depends on assumptions about the errors, the part of yyy the model does not explain. The NIST handbook lists six typical assumptions (opens in a new tab); in regression terms they come down to:

  1. The form is right. Errors average zero at every value of the features. This fails when a curve or an important variable is missing.
  2. Errors are independent. One row's error says nothing about the next. Time-ordered data often break this.
  3. Errors have constant spread across the range of predictions.
  4. Errors are roughly normal. This matters for intervals and tests on small samples, and much less for the predictions themselves.
  5. Features are measured with little error, and the rows are a fair sample of the cases to be predicted.

The first assumption decides whether predictions are systematically biased. The second, third and fourth mainly decide whether the reported uncertainty can be trusted. None of them can be read from R2R^2R2; the handbook's section on model validation (opens in a new tab) is blunt that a high value is no proof of a good fit and that residual plots are the primary tool.

Plot residuals against the fitted values, against each feature, against candidate features left out of the model, and against row order or time when the data have one. Fix the functional form first: NIST points out (opens in a new tab) that a wrong curve can imitate a widening spread, so a funnel only means non-constant variance once the shape is right.

Edge cases and limitations

Extrapolating outside the range of the training data
A linear model keeps the same slope forever. The straight line fitted to stopping distances between 20 and 100 km/h predicts −6.9 m at 10 km/h and 98 m at 120 km/h, where the formula the data follow gives 3.3 m and 114 m. NIST lists poor extrapolation among the main disadvantages of linear least squares. Record the minimum and maximum of each feature at training time and flag predictions requested outside them.
One outlier can move the whole line
Because errors are squared, a point 10 units from the line counts as much as 100 points that are 1 unit away. A single mistyped value at the edge of the feature range can tilt the fit and inflate every other residual. Refit without the suspicious rows and compare coefficients; if they change materially, verify the data, and consider a robust method such as the Huber regression described in the scikit-learn linear models guide.
Features that are exact combinations of each other
If one column can be computed from others, such as a one-hot column for every category alongside an intercept, or the same length in both metres and feet, infinitely many coefficient sets give identical predictions. The solver returns one of them and the individual numbers mean nothing. The sign is a rank_ attribute on scikit-learn's LinearRegression that is smaller than the number of columns. Drop one category per group, for example with the drop option of OneHotEncoder, or remove the duplicate.
High polynomial degrees on unscaled features
Raising speeds of 20 to 100 to the eighth power creates columns that range up to 10^16 beside a column that ranges up to 100. On the nine stopping-distance points, scikit-learn 1.9.1 reported a rank of 2 for those eight columns and a training R² of 0.93, worse than degree 2, because the solver discards directions it cannot resolve numerically. Standardising first removes that problem and exposes the other one: the degree-8 curve then fits every point and predicts 3,413 m at 120 km/h.
As many coefficients as rows
With p features plus an intercept and only p + 1 rows, the model can pass through every training point whatever the data are. SSE is zero, R² is 1 and neither number says anything about new data. This happens quickly with one-hot encoded categories and polynomial terms. Count the columns after preprocessing; when they approach the number of rows, cut features or use a regularised model.
Targets that cannot take every value
Nothing stops a linear model from predicting a negative count, a probability above 1 or a negative price. If the target is a yes/no outcome, use logistic regression. If it is a positive quantity that spans orders of magnitude, fitting the logarithm of the target often straightens the relationship and evens out the residual spread, at the cost of coefficients that now describe percentage changes.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The target is a number and it changes at a roughly steady rate with each feature over the range you care about.
  • You need to explain the model, with one coefficient per feature, in real units, that a domain expert can sanity-check.
  • You have little data, from tens to a few thousand rows, and a model with few parameters is less likely to fit noise.
  • You need a baseline to beat before trying trees, boosting or neural networks.
  • You want to adjust one quantity for others, such as energy use for operating hours and climate, or an experiment metric for its pre-experiment value.

Think twice when

  • The target is a category or a probability. Use logistic regression or another classifier.
  • The relationship depends on thresholds and interactions you cannot list in advance. Tree ensembles find those without being told.
  • Features outnumber rows or are strongly correlated and you need stable coefficients. Use ridge or lasso regularisation.
  • Rows are ordered in time with errors that carry over from one period to the next and you need trustworthy uncertainty. Use a time-series model.

A simpler alternative. Predict the mean of the target for every row, or the mean within a few obvious groups. That baseline has an R² of exactly 0 on the data it was computed from, and a regression that cannot beat it on held-out data has found no usable linear signal. A group mean stops being enough once the target clearly moves with a numeric feature inside each group, which a scatter plot will show.

Key takeaways

  • Linear regression predicts a number as an intercept plus a weighted sum of features, and least squares picks the weights that minimise the sum of squared residuals.
  • A coefficient is the change in the prediction per unit of its feature with the other features held fixed. It moves when correlated features are added, and it is not a causal effect.
  • Raw coefficient sizes depend on units, so compare features only after standardising them or multiplying each coefficient by its feature's standard deviation.
  • Adding squared or product columns lets the same linear method fit curves. Choose the degree by held-out error, because training error never gets worse when a column is added.
  • A high R² does not show that the shape of the model is right. Plot the residuals and look for curves, funnels, drift and outliers.
  • A fitted line can be trusted only inside the range of the data it was fitted on.

Example 1 of 5

NIST load cell calibration: a straight-line fit rejected by its residuals

NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology)Documented in a case study

Section 4.6.1 of the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods is a worked case study in calibrating a load cell, a force sensor. Known loads were applied and the deflection of the cell was recorded. The cited pages give the data, both fitted models, the residual analysis and the use of the final model for calibration. [10][11][12][13][14]

The problem

A calibration needs an equation that relates a known load to the instrument's deflection, so that a later deflection reading can be converted back into a load. Forty measurements were taken at loads from 150,000 to 3,000,000 units. The question is which regression function describes the relationship well enough to calibrate with.

Where this topic fits

Least squares is the estimation step, and it is used twice. A straight line is fitted first and its residuals are examined. The residual plots decide the next model, a quadratic in load, which is fitted by the same method and checked in the same way before the equation is inverted for calibration.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Plot deflection against load. The points look like a straight line, so the first model is deflection = b0 + b1 × load.
  2. Fit the line by least squares. An overlay of the fitted line on the data shows no visible problem.
  3. Plot the residuals against load. They form an arch, negative at low and high loads and positive in the middle. The curvature was invisible on the raw plot because it is tiny compared with the linear trend.
  4. Add load squared as a second column and refit. This is polynomial regression, still solved as a linear least-squares problem.
  5. Repeat the residual plots for the quadratic. The residual standard deviation is about a tenth of what it was and the arch is gone.
  6. Calibrate with the quadratic by putting an observed deflection on the left-hand side and solving for load, keeping the root that lies inside the range of loads used in the experiment.

Why this approach fits

A well-built sensor responds almost proportionally, so a model that is linear in its coefficients is the natural first choice, and it remains the right tool after the correction: adding a squared column keeps the problem a linear least-squares one with an exact solution and standard intervals.

The case also shows why the residual plot, not the fit statistic, is the acceptance test. Recomputing from the published data, the straight line already has an R² above 0.9999, yet its residuals are about ten times larger than the quadratic's and follow a systematic arch.

Limitation or tradeoff

The quadratic describes this instrument over the loads that were tested; it is not a physical law. Outside 150,000 to 3,000,000 units the squared term keeps bending the curve, and the equation has a second root with no physical meaning. NIST also notes that the data were collected in order of increasing load, which makes instrument drift hard to separate from the effect of load.

Prometheus predict_linear: alerting before a disk fills up

Prometheus projectDocumented in an open-source project

Prometheus is an open-source monitoring system. Its query-language documentation defines the functions predict_linear() and deriv(), and the alert rules published with the project's node_exporter (the node-mixin) use predict_linear() to warn that a filesystem is about to run out of space. [15][16][17]

The problem

An alert that fires when a disk is 95 % full leaves little time to react if the disk is filling quickly, and fires for no reason if the disk has sat at 95 % for months. Operators want to be told when the current trend will exhaust the space within a set number of hours.

Where this topic fits

The regression runs inside the query engine every time the alert rule is evaluated. Its input is the free-space samples in a trailing time window; its output is one extrapolated value per filesystem, which the rule compares with zero. No fitted model is stored between evaluations.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The node exporter publishes the free bytes of each filesystem as a gauge, and Prometheus stores a sample of it at each scrape.
  2. For each filesystem the rule selects the samples in the trailing window, six hours by default. Each sample is a pair of timestamp and free bytes.
  3. predict_linear fits a simple linear regression of the value on time: one feature, giving a slope in bytes per second and an intercept.
  4. The fitted line is evaluated at the requested horizon, 24 hours ahead for the warning rule and 4 hours ahead for the critical rule.
  5. The condition holds if the extrapolated value is below zero and the filesystem is already under the free-space threshold and is not mounted read-only.
  6. The alert fires only after the condition has held for one hour, which filters out short dips.

Why this approach fits

Over a few hours, steady growth of logs or data is close to a straight line, and a one-feature fit with a closed-form solution is cheap enough to recompute for every filesystem at every evaluation. The slope has a direct meaning, bytes per second, and extrapolating it answers the operational question as asked. Fitting over the whole window is also steadier than taking the difference between two samples, because every sample contributes to the slope.

Limitation or tradeoff

This is deliberate extrapolation, the weakest use of a linear model. Disk usage that moves in steps, such as a nightly cleanup job, a log rotation or one large write, is not linear, so the predicted value swings as those events enter and leave the window. The mixin's configuration comments acknowledge this, and the extra threshold condition and the one-hour hold are there to compensate. A window that is too short reacts to spikes; one that is too long is slow to notice a new trend.

What the sources document

  • predict_linear takes a range vector and a number of seconds t, and returns the value expected t seconds ahead according to a simple linear regression over the samples in the range. It requires at least two float samples and is meant for gauges only. [15]
  • deriv returns the per-second derivative of each float series in a range vector, and the page says it is also computed with simple linear regression. [15]
  • The NodeFilesystemSpaceFillingUp rules combine a free-space percentage threshold, a predict_linear() call on node_filesystem_avail_bytes compared with zero and a read-only check, held for 1h, with a summary saying the filesystem is predicted to run out of space within the configured number of hours. BigQuery ML: training a linear regression with one SQL statement

BigQuery ML trains models inside the BigQuery data warehouse from SQL. Google's reference for the CREATE MODEL statement, its page on automatic preprocessing and its penguin-weight tutorial document how the LINEAR_REG model type is prepared, trained and evaluated. [18][19][20]

The problem

The data for a regression often already sits in warehouse tables, and the people who know it best write SQL. Exporting rows to a separate training environment means another pipeline to build and keep running. The tutorial's task is to predict a penguin's body mass in grams from its species, island, sex and three body measurements.

Where this topic fits

Linear regression is the model selected by model_type='linear_reg'. Before it runs, BigQuery ML preprocesses the selected columns automatically. After it, the trained model is a warehouse object that ML.EVALUATE, ML.PREDICT and ML.EXPLAIN_PREDICT take as input.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A SELECT statement defines the training rows, and input_label_cols names the target column, which must hold real numbers.
  2. The features are preprocessed automatically. Numeric columns are standardised, missing numeric values are replaced with the column mean, and text columns are one-hot encoded.
  3. The service picks a training strategy. For a modest number of features and no L1 penalty it uses the normal equation, the closed-form least-squares solution, so training finishes in a single iteration.
  4. When an L1 penalty or a warm start is requested, the total feature cardinality exceeds 10,000, or there are fewer than ten training rows per unit of cardinality, it switches to batch gradient descent.
  5. ML.EVALUATE returns regression metrics including mean absolute error, mean squared error and the R² score.
  6. ML.PREDICT scores new rows, and ML.EXPLAIN_PREDICT adds per-feature attributions for each prediction.

Why this approach fits

Least squares has a closed-form solution, and a typical implementation of the normal equation needs only sums over the rows (the entries of X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X and X⊤yX^\top yX⊤y), which is the kind of aggregation a warehouse engine is built to run at scale. That makes linear regression a natural first model to offer in SQL: no learning rate to tune, and weights that an analyst can inspect. The documented switch to gradient descent covers the cases where the closed form stops being practical.

Limitation or tradeoff

The convenience hides the diagnostic work. The statement returns a model whether or not a linear relationship is reasonable, and nothing in the documented workflow shows a residual plot; you have to compute residuals yourself from the ML.PREDICT output. P-values and standard errors exist only if CALCULATE_P_VALUES was switched on at training time, and the normal equation is unavailable with an L1 penalty. The tutorial's evaluation query scores the model on the same table it was trained on, which the page itself says not to do in production.

CUPED at Microsoft: a regression slope that makes A/B tests more sensitive

Microsoft (Experimentation Platform and Bing)Documented in an engineering article

Microsoft's Experimentation Platform team describes its variance-reduction method, CUPED (Controlled experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data), in an engineering article. The 2013 paper by Deng, Xu, Kohavi and Walker that introduced the method reports its use in Bing's experimentation system. [21][22]

The problem

In an online controlled experiment the measured difference between treatment and control is noisy because users differ enormously from one another: a heavy user generates many queries whichever variant they see. Small real effects are hard to detect unless the experiment runs longer or on more users.

Where this topic fits

Here the regression is not used to predict anything for its own sake. A linear model of each user's metric during the experiment on a covariate measured before the experiment explains the part of the variation that has nothing to do with the treatment. That explained part is removed, and the usual comparison of group averages is made on what is left.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. For each user, record the metric during the experiment (y) and a covariate fixed before assignment (x), typically the same metric measured in the weeks before the experiment.
  2. Fit a linear model of y on x in each group. With one covariate the coefficient is the simple regression slope, the covariance of x and y divided by the variance of x.
  3. Adjust each group's average of y by the slope times the gap between that group's average of x and the overall average of x.
  4. Estimate the treatment effect as the difference between the adjusted averages. Because x was fixed before random assignment, its expected value is the same in both groups and the adjustment does not shift the expected effect.
  5. The variance of the estimate shrinks by the share of variance the linear model explains, its R². The platform reports 1 / (1 − R²) for each metric as an effective traffic multiplier.

Why this approach fits

A metric and its own pre-experiment value are usually related in a roughly straight-line way, and a one-coefficient least-squares fit is cheap enough to run for thousands of metrics. The model does not have to be right in any deep sense. Random assignment balances the covariate between the groups, so even a mediocre fit removes some noise without biasing the comparison. The better the fit, the larger the gain, and R2R^2R2 measures that gain directly.

Limitation or tradeoff

The gain depends entirely on how well the covariate predicts the metric, and the article reports a product surface where most metrics gained almost nothing. Users with no history before the experiment have no covariate value; the paper handles them with an extra indicator for whether a user appeared in the pre-experiment period, which adjusts them only as a group. The method also fails if the covariate can be influenced by the treatment: the paper shows an experiment in which a covariate measured during the experiment produced an estimate pointing in the opposite direction to the known effect.

What the sources document

  • The article describes the procedure in three steps: linear models of the metric on the covariates are estimated separately for treatment and control (or with an assignment-group indicator), adjusted metric values are formed using the estimated coefficient and the overall covariate mean, and the difference in average adjusted values estimates the treatment effect. [21]
  • It states that the reduction in variance is proportional to the share of variance the linear model explains in treatment and control (the total R²) and that the estimator stays consistent. The ratio 1/(1 − R²) is described as the amount of traffic a simple difference estimator would need to match the variance reduction; the platform surfaces it for each variance-reduced metric as the effective traffic multiplier. [21]
  • The paper derives the variance-minimising coefficient as cov(Y, X)/var(X), points out that this is the ordinary least squares solution of regressing centred Y on centred X, and gives the resulting variance as var(Y)(1 − R²). [22]
  • On one Microsoft product surface more than 68 % of metrics had an effective traffic multiplier of at most 1.05x, while on another more than 55 % of metrics had a multiplier above 1.2x. [21]
  • The paper says CUPED was live in Bing's experimentation system, that three experiments showed variance reductions of 45 %, 52 % and 49 % with one week of experiment data and one week of pre-experiment data, and that covariates which could be affected by the treatment should never be used. [22]

ENERGY STAR score: a regression that sets each building's expected energy use

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Portfolio Manager tool gives commercial buildings an ENERGY STAR score from 1 to 100. EPA's technical reference documents describe the regression behind the score, and the reference for offices publishes the fitted coefficients and a worked calculation. [23][24][25]

The problem

Comparing buildings by energy use per square foot alone is unfair. An office that is open 80 hours a week with dense staffing needs more energy than one open 40 hours, however well it is run. EPA needs an expected energy use for a building of a given size, schedule, occupancy and climate, so that the building's actual use can be judged against it.

Where this topic fits

The regression supplies the denominator of the score. It is fitted once per property type on a national survey sample. Portfolio Manager then evaluates the equation for each building, divides the building's actual source energy use intensity (EUI) by the predicted value, and converts that ratio to a percentile with a lookup table.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start from a nationally representative survey of buildings and filter it to the peer group. For offices this is the 2012 Commercial Building Energy Consumption Survey, and 886 buildings remain.
  2. Choose the target, source energy per square foot, and features that describe how a building is used, such as hours, workers, computers and climate. Features that describe how well it is equipped, such as lighting technology, are deliberately left out.
  3. Fit a weighted ordinary least squares regression using the survey's observation weights, and keep the features that are statistically significant.
  4. Centre each feature on its survey mean. The constant, 143.1, is then the predicted EUI of an office with average characteristics, and each coefficient is an adjustment for being above or below average.
  5. For one building, multiply each centred feature by its coefficient and add the constant to get the predicted source EUI.
  6. Divide the actual EUI by the predicted EUI and look the ratio up in a table built from the distribution of ratios in the survey. Lower ratios receive higher scores.

Why this approach fits

The goal is adjustment, not the best possible prediction, and that calls for a model whose every term can be published and argued about. Each coefficient is a stated allowance: about 0.61 kBtu per square foot for each extra weekly operating hour, and 15.9 for each extra worker per 1,000 square feet. A building owner can reproduce the predicted value with a calculator, which a black-box model would not allow. Leaving equipment quality out of the features is what turns the leftover, actual divided by predicted, into a measure of efficiency.

Limitation or tradeoff

By EPA's own figure the office equation explains 22 % of the variance in energy use per square foot (about 65 % when recomputed for total energy rather than energy per square foot), so much of the difference between buildings is not captured by the features. The score treats that remainder as efficiency, although part of it is unmeasured circumstance. The coefficients are associations in one survey year, not engineering laws. EPA also reports that the survey data showed no positive relationship between heating degree days and EUI, so the heating adjustment came from a separate analysis, and that dew point and cooling degree days were too correlated for both to be kept: a direct case of multicollinearity limiting what coefficients can say.

4Common mistakes

  1. Accepting a fit because R² is high without plotting the residuals.

    Why it causes problems
    R² measures how much variance is explained, not whether the shape is right. A straight line through gently curved data can score above 0.99 and still be biased in the same direction at both ends of the range, so every prediction near the edges is systematically off.
    How to recognise it
    Plot residuals against the fitted values and against each feature. An arch, a U, or long runs of residuals with the same sign mean structure is left over. In the stopping-distance example the line scores 0.98 and its residuals run positive, negative, positive.
    How to fix it
    Add the missing term (a square, a logarithm, an interaction or a new feature), refit, and repeat the plots until they show no pattern. Report held-out error next to R².

  2. Reading a coefficient as the causal effect of its feature, or as the slope of a scatter plot.

    Why it causes problems
    In a model with several features a coefficient is conditional on the others, and it absorbs the influence of anything correlated with its feature that was left out. A decision to change that feature can then have a smaller effect than the coefficient suggests, or the opposite effect.
    How to recognise it
    The coefficient moves noticeably, or flips sign, when a related feature is added or removed, or it varies widely across cross-validation folds. In the worked example the distance coefficient moves from 2.5 to 1.96 when stops are added.
    How to fix it
    Report coefficients as associations with the other listed features held fixed, and check their stability across folds. For a causal question, run a randomised experiment or use a study design that accounts for confounders.

  3. Ranking features by the raw size of their coefficients.

    Why it causes problems
    Coefficient size depends on the feature's units. A feature with large values gets a tiny coefficient and looks unimportant, while a 0/1 flag gets a large one and looks dominant. The ranking changes if you merely convert metres to kilometres.
    How to recognise it
    Features with very different ranges share one coefficient table. In the quadratic stopping-distance model the squared-speed coefficient is 0.0059, the smallest number in the equation, yet that term supplies about 59 of the 84 metres predicted at 100 km/h.
    How to fix it
    Standardise the features before fitting, or multiply each coefficient by its feature's standard deviation, and compare those values. Keep the unstandardised model for stating effects in real units.

  4. Choosing the polynomial degree or the feature set by the training score.

    Why it causes problems
    Training error can only fall as columns are added, so this rule always selects the most complex model. That model fits noise, and its predictions between and beyond the training points can be badly wrong.
    How to recognise it
    Training R² keeps rising while cross-validated error bottoms out and then climbs. On the nine stopping-distance points, training RMSE falls from 0.73 to 0.62 between degree 2 and degree 6 while leave-one-out RMSE rises from 1.03 to 6.64.
    How to fix it
    Choose complexity with a validation set or cross-validation, keep a final test set untouched, and prefer the simplest model whose validation error is close to the best.

  5. Using the fitted line far outside the range of the training features.

    Why it causes problems
    A linear model continues at the same slope indefinitely. Where the real relationship bends, levels off or cannot go below zero, extrapolated predictions are wrong without any warning from the model.
    How to recognise it
    Predictions that are impossible, such as a negative distance or a share above 100 percent, or inputs outside the minimum and maximum seen in training. The stopping-distance line gives −6.9 m at 10 km/h.
    How to fix it
    Store each feature's training range with the model and flag or refuse requests outside it. If the wider range matters, collect data there before predicting there.

  6. Quoting p-values and confidence intervals from a model whose residuals are correlated or fan out.

    Why it causes problems
    The standard formulas assume independent errors with a constant spread. With time-ordered or grouped rows the standard errors are typically too small, so weak effects look significant and intervals look narrower than they are.
    How to recognise it
    Residuals plotted in row or time order drift or stay on one side for long stretches; residuals against fitted values widen like a funnel; or several rows come from the same customer, device or day.
    How to fix it
    Model the structure with time features, group terms, a transformed target or weighted least squares, or use standard errors designed for correlated or unequal errors. Treat plain least-squares intervals as optimistic until the residual plots are clean.

speed = np.array([40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 120], dtype=float).reshape(-1, 1)
litres = np.array([6.9, 6.2, 5.5, 5.4, 5.3, 5.9, 6.3, 7.4, 8.4])

    model = make_pipeline(
        StandardScaler(),
        PolynomialFeatures(degree=degree, include_bias=False),
        LinearRegression(),
    )
    held_out = cross_val_predict(model, speed, litres, cv=LeaveOneOut())

          f"LOO RMSE = {loo_rmse:.2f}, 130 km/h -> {model.predict([[130]])[0]:.1f}")

b, (w1, w2) = raw[-1].intercept_, raw[-1].coef_

  2. Explain in plain words why the bedroom coefficient is positive in one model and negative in the other, and say what question each one answers.
  3. Which feature has more influence in the two-feature model? Answer with coefficients multiplied by the feature's standard deviation, not with raw coefficients.
  4. Use the two-feature model to predict the price of an 80 m² flat with two bedrooms and with three. Then say why the model should not be used for a 200 m² flat, and why its intercept is not "the price of a flat with no area".
  5. A third colleague concludes that removing a wall to turn a three-bedroom flat into a two-bedroom one will raise its value by 10 thousand. Give two reasons why that does not follow.

Requirements

  • Report all three fitted equations with coefficients to two decimals and R² to three decimals.
  • Explain the sign change using the idea of holding area fixed, and state the correlation between area and bedrooms.
  • Compare the influence of the two features with standardised quantities, not raw coefficients.
  • State the range of areas in the data when discussing the 200 m² flat.
  • Separate what the data show, an association, from what the renovation claim needs, a causal effect.

Expected behaviour

  • Bedrooms only: price = 131.1 + 31.14 × bedrooms, R² = 0.508.
  • Area only: price = 37.7 + 2.10 × area, R² = 0.977.
  • Both: price = 30.5 + 2.50 × area − 10.37 × bedrooms, R² = 0.997.
  • The correlation between area and bedrooms is 0.81.
  • Coefficient times standard deviation is about 52.8 for area and −10.6 for bedrooms.
  • The two-feature model predicts about 210.2 for 80 m² with two bedrooms and 199.8 with three.
Hint 1

Compare flats 3 and 4, or flats 7 and 8. Within each pair the areas are similar but the bedroom counts differ.

Hint 2

The bedrooms-only model has no way to know that flats with more bedrooms are also larger, so its coefficient carries the effect of size as well.

Hint 3

Use the population standard deviation of each feature, which is what NumPy's std returns by default, and multiply it by that feature's coefficient.

Show solution
python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

area = np.array([50, 58, 64, 70, 76, 84, 90, 98, 106, 120], dtype=float)
beds = np.array([1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 3, 2, 4, 3, 4], dtype=float)
price = np.array([148, 153, 181, 171, 202, 213, 232, 236, 263, 291], dtype=float)

def report(name, X):
    model = LinearRegression().fit(X, price)
    coefs = ", ".join(f"{c:.2f}" for c in model.coef_)
    print(f"{name}: intercept {model.intercept_:.1f}, coefficients [{coefs}], R2 {model.score(X, price):.3f}")
    return model

report("beds only", beds.reshape(-1, 1))
report("area only", area.reshape(-1, 1))
X = np.column_stack([area, beds])
both = report("area + beds", X)

print("corr(area, beds) =", round(np.corrcoef(area, beds)[0, 1], 2))
print("coefficient x feature std:", np.round(both.coef_ * X.std(axis=0), 1))
print("80 m2: 2 beds ->", round(both.predict([[80, 2]])[0], 1), "| 3 beds ->", round(both.predict([[80, 3]])[0], 1))

Part 2. Area and bedrooms are strongly correlated (0.81): flats with more bedrooms are larger. The bedrooms-only coefficient, +31.14, answers "how much more do flats with one more bedroom cost on average?", and most of that difference is size. The two-feature coefficient, −10.37, answers "among flats of the same area, how does price differ with one more bedroom?". At a fixed area an extra bedroom means smaller rooms, and in this data those flats are cheaper. Flats 3 and 4 show it directly: 64 m² with one bedroom is priced at 181, while 70 m² with three bedrooms is priced at 171. Both statements are correct descriptions; they describe different comparisons.

Part 3. A raw comparison of 2.50 with −10.37 suggests bedrooms matter more, but the units differ. One standard deviation of area (21.1 m²) moves the prediction by 52.8, and one standard deviation of bedrooms (1.02) moves it by 10.6. Area has about five times the influence.

Part 4. The predictions are 210.2 and 199.8, which differ by exactly the bedroom coefficient because area is the same. The areas in the data run from 50 to 120 m², so 200 m² is far outside them and the model has no evidence that the same slope continues. The intercept, 30.5, is the prediction at zero area and zero bedrooms, a point that is equally outside the data; it is a fitting constant, not a price.

Part 5. First, the coefficient compares different flats, not the same flat before and after a change; whatever makes owners build fewer, larger rooms (a more expensive layout, a better building) may be what buyers pay for. Second, ten rows with two correlated features give an unstable estimate: the sign of a coefficient like this can change when a single flat is added. A causal claim needs a comparison in which only the wall changes.
