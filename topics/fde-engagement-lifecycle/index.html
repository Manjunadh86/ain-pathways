The FDE Engagement Lifecycle

The FDE engagement lifecycle is the sequence of phases a forward deployed engineer takes a customer project through: discovery, scoping, prototype, pilot, production deployment and handoff. Each phase produces a written artefact and ends at an exit criterion that both sides can check before the next phase starts.

1Overview

Why it matters

Customer projects often fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the code. They fail because nobody agreed what "done" meant, because a demo that worked on sample data was promised as a product, because a security review that takes four weeks was requested in the week of launch, or because the engineer left and nobody on the customer's side could run what was built. Each of those is a missed step in the lifecycle, and each is far cheaper to catch at the end of an early phase than after rollout.

A forward deployed engineer usually works with a lot of freedom and little supervision inside someone else's organisation. The lifecycle is the structure that replaces supervision: it tells you what evidence to produce next, when you are allowed to move on, and when the honest answer is to go back or stop. It also gives the customer something they can track that is not a feeling. Anyone who delivers software into another organisation needs it, including forward deployed engineers, solutions engineers, consultants and internal platform teams who build for other departments.

What you will be able to do

  • Name the six phases of an engagement and state the question each one exists to answer.
  • List the artefact and the exit criterion for each phase, and say who should sign it.
  • Write exit criteria that are measurable and agreed before the phase begins.
  • Identify the long-lead items in an enterprise (access, security review, change approval) and schedule them from the first week.
  • Work out which phase an engagement is really in from its artefacts instead of from its status report.
  • Decide at a gate between going forward, going back a phase and stopping, and explain the decision from evidence.

Key terms

Engagement
One time-bound piece of work that an engineer or team does with a single customer to deliver a defined outcome.
Phase
A stretch of an engagement that exists to answer one question, such as whether the idea works on real data.
Artefact
A document or working system produced by a phase that proves the phase happened, such as a scope brief or an evaluation report.
Exit criterion
A condition that a named person can check and that must be true before the next phase starts.
Gate
The check at the end of a phase where evidence is compared with the exit criteria and someone decides to go forward, go back or stop.
Prototype
A deliberately limited build made to test the riskiest assumption, usually on real data, and not meant to be kept as the final system.
Pilot
A trial in which a limited group of real users does real work with the system for a fixed period, measured against a baseline.
Go/no-go decision
The decision, taken against criteria written in advance, on whether a release to production goes ahead on the planned date.
Cutover
The planned switch from the old way of working to the new system, including data migration, user switch-over and the fallback if it fails.
Handoff
The transfer of ownership of a running system to the team that will operate and change it after the engagement ends.

2Explanation

Introduction

If you have had a kitchen fitted, you have seen a lifecycle. Someone visits, measures and asks how you cook. You receive a written quote that says what is included. You approve a sample door before forty are ordered. The work is inspected before you pay the final invoice, and you are shown how the appliances work and where the water shuts off. At every step there is something on paper, and a moment where either side can say "not yet".

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is an engineer who builds inside a customer's environment (see What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?). An engagement is one time-bound piece of work with one customer. The engagement lifecycle splits that work into six phases:

  1. Discovery: find the real problem and what success would look like.
  2. Scoping: agree the smallest thing worth building, and what is left out.
  3. Prototype: prove the risky part on the customer's real data.
  4. Pilot: put it in front of a limited group of real users.
  5. Production deployment: roll it out through the customer's approvals and change process.
  6. Handoff: transfer ownership so the customer runs it without you.

Each phase produces an artefact, a document or working system that proves the phase happened, and ends at an exit criterion, a condition both sides can check. The check itself is called a gate. Gates matter because of what a mistake costs later. A misunderstanding caught in a discovery summary costs one conversation. The same misunderstanding caught after rollout costs the rollout.

The arrows that point backwards are part of the design. A prototype that fails on real data sends you back to scoping. A pilot that misses its threshold sends you back to the prototype. Stopping is a valid result too.

Organisations name and count the phases differently. PostHog's FDE team uses four, and UK government digital teams use discovery, alpha, beta and live. This lesson uses six because each answers a different question. It covers the question, artefact and exit criterion of every phase, the enterprise realities that set the real timeline, and a worked example in which the order of two approval requests moves the handoff date by 28 working days.

How it works

Palantir, the company that pioneered the FDE role, defines its unit of delivery as a time-bound effort by a dedicated team to deliver new capabilities for a set of users, framed by whose working life improves and what can be measured to show success (Palantir (opens in a new tab)). The lifecycle is what keeps such an effort from drifting.

A phase is a question, an artefact and an exit criterion

Each phase exists to answer one question with evidence. The answer is written down as an artefact, and the phase ends when a named person confirms the exit criterion.

Three rules make the table work:

  • Check the artefact, not the calendar. FDE Academy's phase-gate reference gives the test case: an engagement that reports itself in production rollout but has no evaluation report is still in evaluation (FDE Academy (opens in a new tab)).
  • Every gate has three outcomes: go, go back, or stop. GOV.UK's guidance says stopping at the end of discovery is not a failure, and that a team still unsure at the end of alpha can stop or repeat a phase (discovery (opens in a new tab), alpha (opens in a new tab)).
  • Agree the criterion before the phase starts. A threshold chosen after the results are in is not a test.

1. Discovery: what is the problem, and what would success be?

You interview and watch the people who do the work, map the data and systems, and measure a baseline. Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering covers the method. The artefact is a discovery summary: the stated request, the problem you found, the constraints, the baseline and a success definition. Exit when the sponsor agrees to a success definition that could be shown false. Discovery is also the moment to request everything that has a queue in front of it: accounts, data access, a security review slot.

2. Scoping: what is the smallest thing worth building?

The artefact is a short brief. PostHog's FDE handbook requires three things in it before delivery starts (a measurable outcome, a rough timeline and a shared definition of done) and sends it to the customer to confirm before the team commits (PostHog (opens in a new tab)). Add what is out of scope and who signs each later gate. Exit when the customer confirms the brief in writing. Technical Scoping and Sequencing covers how to cut scope.

3. Prototype: can it work on this customer's real data?

A prototype is built to answer the riskiest question, not to be kept. GOV.UK's alpha guidance says to build things just complex enough to test ideas and to expect to throw the code away. Its example is the Register to vote team, whose riskiest assumption was an API that integrated with the registration systems of over 400 local councils, so that is what the alpha concentrated on. Microsoft's ISE playbook time-boxes the equivalent step, a feasibility study, at 4 to 16 weeks with a suggested first milestone at 4 weeks. Its output is a report that recommends moving on, re-scoping or collecting more data (ISE (opens in a new tab)).

For AI systems the exit criterion is an evaluation result (see Evaluating LLM Applications). Anthropic's documentation asks for criteria that are specific, measurable, achievable and relevant, and contrasts "the model should classify sentiments well" with an F1 score of at least 0.85 on a held-out test set of 10,000 posts (Anthropic (opens in a new tab)). The artefacts are the working prototype, a list of data issues and how each was handled, and an evaluation report. Exit when the result meets the threshold agreed beforehand. If it does not, return to scoping with what you learned.

4. Pilot: does it work for real users doing real work?

A pilot exposes the system to a limited group under real conditions. GOV.UK calls this private beta: a limited number of people are invited so the team can get feedback and improve, and a legacy service being replaced keeps running until the new one is live (beta (opens in a new tab)). Give the pilot a fixed end date and compare it with the discovery baseline. The artefact is a pilot report covering usage, the success metrics, support issues and failures. Exit when the metrics meet their thresholds on live use, the people who support users can cope, and the sponsor decides to roll out.

5. Production deployment: can the organisation run it at full scale?

This phase is mostly about the customer's organisation. Microsoft's Success by Design framework lists what a project has in place by its Prepare phase: final user acceptance testing and training, customer approvals, information security reviews, a cutover plan with go/no-go criteria, mock go-lives, a support model, and a deployment runbook with tasks, owners, durations and dependencies (Microsoft (opens in a new tab)). Your artefacts are that runbook, a rollback plan, monitoring and an incident plan. Exit when the go/no-go criteria are met and the system is live, stable and in use. Deploying in Customer Environments has the technical detail.

6. Handoff: can the customer run and change it without you?

PostHog's test for a finished deliverable is that a stranger on the customer's team could pick it up cold and not need to ask the FDE anything (PostHog (opens in a new tab)). Google's SRE teams take over services through training followed by a progressive transfer of responsibilities, with the original developers on backup for a period (Google SRE (opens in a new tab)). GOV.UK's guidance on the live phase adds that running a service does not have to mean a full team on it all of the time, and tells teams to work out before going live what level of continuing improvement to support and who is needed for it (live (opens in a new tab)). The artefacts are runbooks, a handover record with follow-on work as explicit tasks, and a closing summary of what your own product team should learn (see From Field Work to Product). Exit when a named owner accepts the system and has operated it for an agreed period. Production Handoff and Support goes deeper.

Short engagements merge phases. Every version still has a point where scope is confirmed, a point where evidence is checked before a larger commitment, and a point where ownership moves.

Enterprise workflow realities

Large organisations add work that no demo shows. Plan for it from the first week.

  • Access has a queue. The ISE playbook treats verifying that the full team has data access, setting up a restricted environment and de-identifying sensitive data as part of feasibility work.
  • Reviews have lead times. GOV.UK teams are told to book a phase assessment six weeks ahead. Security, privacy and legal reviews behave the same way, so submit them when the brief is agreed, not when the build is finished.
  • Production changes need approval. DORA notes that most IT organisations manage change through processes that often include external reviewers or a change approval board, and that teams who clearly understand the process perform better (DORA (opens in a new tab)). Learn the customer's process and release calendar during discovery.
  • Three different people sign. The sponsor pays, the users adopt, and an operations team inherits. Each has a gate, so name all three in the brief.
  • Scope meets reality. OpenAI's head of FDE says that what a customer describes in scoping often does not match the data and systems on the ground, which is why his team validates before delivery.
  • Scope changes are commercial events. PostHog quotes per milestone deliverable and treats a deliverable that doubles in scope as a new quote, not an informal extension.

Worked example: Claims-triage assistant: a schedule and a pilot gate you can recompute

This is a teaching scenario with invented numbers. Every figure can be reproduced with the script below, which uses only the Python standard library.

Scenario. A regional insurer asks for an assistant that reads incoming claims and routes each one to the routine, complex or urgent queue. Twenty claims adjusters will use it. The data sits in a claims database that needs an access approval. The insurer's security team reviews any new system that touches claims, and a change board approves production releases.

Step 2: compute the schedule two ways

Plan A requests each approval when the work reaches it: data access once the brief is agreed, and the security review after the evaluation, "because then there is something to review". Plan B asks for data access at kickoff and submits the security review as soon as the brief exists. In both plans the prototype needs the brief and data access, and the pilot needs the evaluation and the security sign-off.

python
WORK = {"discovery": 5, "scope_brief": 3, "prototype": 10, "evaluation": 5,
        "pilot": 20, "production": 10, "handoff": 5}
WAITS = {"data_access": 15, "security_review": 20, "change_approval": 10}


def finish_days(access_after, security_after):
    needs = {
        "discovery": [],
        "scope_brief": ["discovery"],
        "data_access": access_after,
        "security_review": security_after,
        "prototype": ["scope_brief", "data_access"],
        "evaluation": ["prototype"],
        "pilot": ["evaluation", "security_review"],
        "change_approval": ["pilot"],
        "production": ["change_approval"],
        "handoff": ["production"],
    }
    days = {**WORK, **WAITS}
    finish = {}

    def done(task):
        if task not in finish:
            start = max((done(dep) for dep in needs[task]), default=0)
            finish[task] = start + days[task]
        return finish[task]

    done("handoff")
    return finish


phase_work = sum(WORK.values())
for label, access_after, security_after in [
    ("A request when needed", ["scope_brief"], ["evaluation"]),
    ("B request early", [], ["scope_brief"]),
]:
    finish = finish_days(access_after, security_after)
    total = finish["handoff"]
    print(f"{label}: prototype done day {finish['prototype']}, pilot done day {finish['pilot']}, "
          f"handoff day {total} = {phase_work} work + {total - phase_work} waiting")

]
unmet = 0
for name, measured, rule, threshold in GATE:
    passed = measured >= threshold if rule == ">=" else measured <= threshold
    unmet += not passed
    print(f"{'PASS' if passed else 'FAIL'}  {name:<35} {measured:g} {rule} {threshold:g}")
print("decision:", "GO" if unmet == 0 else f"HOLD, {unmet} criterion unmet")

Nothing about the build differs between the plans. Plan A waits 45 working days and plan B waits 17, which is 28 fewer, because two requests were made earlier. Plan B's remaining wait is 7 days for data access before the prototype can start and 10 days for change approval.

Step 3: run the pilot gate

The GATE list holds the pilot evidence: 184 of 200 sampled claims routed as a senior adjuster would, 1 of 40 urgent claims sent to a slower queue, a 95th-percentile response time of 6.4 seconds, 17 of 20 adjusters active in the final week, median triage time down from 14.0 to 10.5 minutes, and a rollback that the service desk has not yet rehearsed.

What the result means. Every model-quality and adoption number passes and the decision is still HOLD, because one operational criterion is unmet. The fix is an afternoon's rehearsal, not more model work, and the gate found it before go-live instead of during the first incident. Had the criteria been written after the pilot, the rollback rehearsal is the one most likely to have been left off the list.

Edge cases and limitations

A threshold met on a sample too small to prove it
In the worked example 184 of 200 claims is 92%, above the 90% threshold. A 95% Wilson confidence interval for that result runs from about 87.4% to 95.0%, so the true rate could be below 90%. With 920 of 1,000 the interval is about 90.2% to 93.5%. Write the sample size into the criterion, and for a rare, costly failure such as a missed urgent claim, sample that category on purpose instead of relying on 40 cases.
The prototype quietly becomes the production system
A prototype that impresses in a demo creates pressure to "just put it live". It was built to answer one question and usually has no access control, monitoring, error handling or rollback. Say in the scope brief which parts are throwaway, and if a customer wants to keep prototype code, treat that as a scope change that still has to pass the production gate.
A pilot with no end date
A pilot without an end date, a baseline and a named decision-maker tends to drift on with a few users, never being stopped and never being rolled out. The sign is a pilot report that cannot be written because nobody agreed what it would be compared with. Fix the end date, the comparison and the person who decides before the first pilot user logs in.
The person who signed leaves or is replaced
Exit criteria are agreements with named people. When a sponsor changes role mid-engagement, the new sponsor has agreed to nothing. Walk them through the discovery summary and the brief and ask for written confirmation again. This is one reason the artefacts are documents and not memories of a meeting.
A week-long engagement
Small engagements do not need six documents. PostHog's handbook compresses the lifecycle to four phases and a short brief. What must survive the compression is the confirmed outcome before work starts, a check against evidence before the result goes to real users, and a named person who approves and owns what you leave.
Nobody on the customer's side can take the handoff
Some customers have no team able to operate what is built. That is a scoping fact, not a surprise for the last week. Options are to build on things their staff already maintain, to agree a paid support arrangement, or to reduce the scope to what they can own. PostHog's handbook tells its FDEs to prefer the product's built-in capabilities partly because that is what the customer can maintain after the FDE leaves.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The work will be judged by someone other than you, so both sides need written checks agreed before each stage instead of an argument at the end.
  • The build depends on the customer's data, systems or approvals, where access, security review or change control could take longer than the engineering.
  • The system will be used by people who did not ask for it and operated by a team that did not build it, so adoption and ownership need their own gates.
  • A wrong answer or an outage has a real cost, so exposure has to grow in steps from prototype to pilot to full rollout.

Think twice when

  • The change is small and reversible, such as a query, a dashboard or a configuration fix, where one confirmation message and a check afterwards are enough.
  • You are still exploring whether a problem exists at all. Do discovery first and decide afterwards whether there is an engagement to run.
  • Gates are being used to avoid a decision the evidence already supports. A phase that has met its exit criterion should end.

A simpler alternative. For work of a few days, a one-page brief with a measurable outcome, a rough timeline, a definition of done and a named approver, followed by one review against that brief before release. This stops being enough when a second team must operate the result, when the system touches production data or customers, or when being wrong would cost more than redoing the work.

Key takeaways

  • An engagement moves through discovery, scoping, prototype, pilot, production deployment and handoff, and each phase exists to answer one question with evidence.
  • A phase is finished when its artefact exists and a named person has confirmed an exit criterion that was agreed before the phase began, not when the date arrives.
  • Every gate has three outcomes, which are to go forward, go back a phase or stop, and stopping early on good evidence is a successful use of the lifecycle.
  • In an enterprise the timeline is usually set by access approvals, security review and change control, so request them in the first week instead of when the build needs them.
  • Passing model-quality thresholds is not enough to go live, because production gates also cover training, support, rollback and a named owner.
  • Handoff is complete only when someone on the customer's side can run and change the system without you, and what you learned has been passed to your own product team.

Example 1 of 5

PostHog's FDE handbook: intake, scope, execute and wrap-up for short paid engagements

PostHogDocumented in official docs

PostHog, a product analytics company, publishes its company handbook openly. The forward deployed engineering section describes how its FDE team runs short-term projects with the company's biggest customers, on pages titled How forward deployed engineers work, Working with customers, How to get an FDE involved and Working with sales and CS. [1][2][3][4]

The problem

The handbook says the FDE team is small and deliberate about where it spends time, that FDE work is often paid, and that most requests for help setting up PostHog do not need an engagement at all. The team therefore needs a way to decide what to take on, to commit only to something the customer has confirmed, and to leave without the customer depending on it. Roles are split as well: the account owner in sales or customer success keeps the commercial relationship, while the FDE owns the technical outcome.

Where this topic fits

The lifecycle is the team's whole operating model, in four phases: intake, scope, execute and wrap-up. Set against this lesson's six phases, intake and scope cover discovery and scoping, execute covers the build and rollout, and wrap-up is the handoff. There is no separately named prototype or pilot. The handbook instead tells FDEs to state the minimum answer before starting and to begin with the MVP.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Intake: a colleague or customer raises the need with the context the team asks for, namely the gap or pain point and its business setting, technical evidence such as audit reports, rough price expectations, and what access and timeline are available.
  2. Before anything is routed to the team, the requester tries the self-serve options first (the handbook's first suggestion is a read-only audit tool), so that engagements are spent on problems that need hands-on engineering.
  3. Scope: an FDE classifies the engagement, estimates the effort and writes a short brief with a measurable outcome, a rough timeline and a shared definition of done. This discovery and scoping time is free and time-boxed.
  4. The brief goes to the customer to confirm before the team commits. Pricing is quoted per milestone deliverable, and a deliverable that doubles in scope gets a new quote.
  5. Execute: do the hands-on work in a fixed order, which for analytics engagements is the data layer first, then dashboards and automations, then documentation and training, with a weekly sync and a weekly written status note.
  6. Wrap-up: confirm that the deliverable is shipped and approved by a named person on the customer side, record follow-on work as explicit tasks, and hand the relationship back to sales and customer success.
  7. Write down reusable findings for the next customer and report product gaps to the product team that owns the area.

Why this approach fits

A small team selling time-boxed work has two risks: taking on work that never ends, and delivering something the customer cannot maintain. A brief with the outcome, timeline and definition of done, confirmed by the customer, deals with the first, and per-milestone pricing turns any growth in scope into an explicit decision. The test that a stranger could pick the deliverable up cold deals with the second, because it defines "done" from the receiving team's side.

Limitation or tradeoff

Four phases suit short engagements on the company's own product. There is no named pilot or production-readiness gate, so a riskier build would need those added. The handbook states intended practice and gives no data on how often engagements reach their outcome. It also says the team is new and still working things out, so the pages may change.

What the sources document

  • PostHog's handbook states that every FDE engagement moves through four phases, which it names intake, scope, execute and wrap-up. [1]
  • Before delivery starts, every engagement needs a measurable outcome, a rough timeline and a shared definition of done, written as a short brief that is sent to the customer to confirm before the team commits. Pricing is quoted per milestone deliverable, not per hour, and a deliverable that doubles in scope is a new quote. [1]
  • The intake page asks requesters to try self-serve options first and then to bring the gap or pain point with its business context, technical evidence, rough price expectations, and the available access and expected timeline. [3]
  • A deliverable is described as finished when a stranger on the customer's team could pick it up cold, understand the scope, follow the recommendation and not need to contact the FDE team.   • Sales owns the deal and customer success owns the ongoing relationship. When the technical work is done, FDE hands the relationship back with a record of what was built, what the customer should do next and any follow-on work. [4]

Example 2 of 5

OpenAI's forward deployed engineers: a validation phase with evals between scoping and delivery

OpenAIDocumented in a case study

The Pragmatic Engineer's August 2025 article on forward deployed engineers reports how OpenAI's FDE team structures customer projects, as explained by Colin Jarvis, the team's head. A guide in OpenAI's Cookbook, Eval Driven System Design, written in collaboration with Fractional and built around a hypothetical receipt-processing problem, sets out an eval-centred project lifecycle for taking an LLM system from prototype to production. [5][6]

The problem

According to the article, OpenAI's FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure. It quotes Jarvis saying that FDEs work in a great deal of ambiguity and that what a customer describes during scoping often does not match the data and system reality on the ground. Committing to full delivery on the strength of a scoping conversation would therefore risk committing to the wrong thing.

Where this topic fits

The team's customer projects have three phases: early scoping, validation and delivery. Validation sits where this lesson puts the prototype phase and its gate. It ends with a final report that shows evaluation performance against the objectives, and delivery follows. The article's customer example is a John Deere project whose deadline was set by the next growing season.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Early scoping: spend a couple of days on site, sit with users, map their processes, identify the biggest areas of value, prototype with synthetic data and prioritise ideas on a whiteboard.
  2. Validation: go on site and ask whether what was scoped is the most valuable thing the team can do for the customer. If it is, agree validation criteria.
  3. Build evals, meaning quality checks for the LLM application, with input and labelling from users, and scale up the labelling process.
  4. Build features and improve them against those evals, looking for hard limits early and adjusting the scope around constraints that cannot be removed.
  5. Present a final report that shows eval performance compared with the objectives.
  6. Delivery: work on site a few days a week, get data from the customer, build the smallest unit that gives an end-to-end solution, and demo it.
  7. Take what the evals show back to OpenAI's research and product teams. The article describes a voice project in which this improved the model before the customer deployed it.

Why this approach fits

With language models the expensive unknown is whether quality on the customer's own cases is good enough, and a scoping conversation cannot answer that. Evals built with the customer's users, placed before delivery, give both sides a number to agree on and give the engineers a way to hit hard limits while changing scope is still cheap. The same evals later show the product and research teams where the model falls short.

Limitation or tradeoff

The account is a journalist's report of an interview, and the article is paywalled after the OpenAI section. It gives no durations for validation or delivery and no figures on how many projects pass. Validation takes time before any production value is delivered, which a customer who wants quick results may resist. Evals built on a small labelled set can pass while rare cases still fail in production, which is why the Cookbook guide says evaluation has to continue on production traffic.

What the sources document

Example 3 of 5

GOV.UK Chat: phased experiments from internal tests to a 1,000-user private pilot

Government Digital ServiceDocumented in an engineering article

GOV.UK is the UK government's website, run by the Government Digital Service (GDS). In January 2024 four GDS data scientists and user researchers published the findings of GOV.UK Chat, an experimental chatbot that answers questions from published GOV.UK content using retrieval-augmented generation. [7]

The problem

GDS wanted to learn whether a generative AI chatbot could help people find information on GOV.UK, a site where the post says factual accuracy is crucial. Releasing an unproven system to the public would put at risk the site's standing as a trusted source, so the team needed a way to show it to progressively more people while measuring whether its answers were accurate enough.

Where this topic fits

The work ran as phased experiments, each lasting a couple of weeks, with the data evaluated at the end of each phase to decide the next step. The first three phases were internal, the fourth involved a dozen users, and the fifth was a live private pilot with 1,000 invited users. That is the pilot phase of this lesson's lifecycle together with its gate.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Decide before each phase what outcome or learning it is for, and design the experiment and its analysis to produce that.
  2. Run the first three phases internally, including a red-teaming exercise in which colleagues from across government try to break the system or make it misbehave.
  3. At each stage, have human experts assess the answers and the GOV.UK sources retrieved for them, and use the results to choose the components that perform best.
  4. Work with data protection colleagues on a data protection impact assessment, add safeguards against personal data in queries, and remove pages that contain personal data from the system.
  5. Test with a dozen users. When the results are positive, invite 1,000 users into a live private pilot.
  6. Survey the users afterwards, and have experts assess the accuracy and completeness of a sample of answers.
  7. Compare the findings with the standard the site demands. Accuracy was not high enough, so the team kept iterating on the experiment instead of releasing it.

Why this approach fits

A wrong answer on a government site is costly and the cost falls on members of the public, so exposure had to grow only as the evidence did. Short phases with a decision at the end of each kept the price of a bad result low. Measuring two things mattered. Most surveyed users were satisfied even though experts judged accuracy too low, so satisfaction alone would have passed a system that was not ready.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post reports that accuracy fell short but publishes neither the accuracy figures nor a numeric threshold, so a reader cannot see how far short. The survey had 157 responses from 1,000 invited users, and people who answer surveys may differ from those who do not. A pilot with invited users also cannot show how the system behaves with the whole public.

Example 4 of 5

Microsoft Success by Design: a mandatory go-live readiness review for Dynamics 365 projects

MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

Success by Design is the framework Microsoft publishes for implementing its Dynamics 365 business applications. Its introduction on Microsoft Learn says it comes from the FastTrack for Dynamics 365 programme, which is run by the product engineering team, and a companion page, Prepare to go live, describes the go-live readiness review. [8][9]

The problem

An enterprise business application replaces processes a company runs on every day. Going live means migrating data, switching users over inside a cutover window and supporting them afterwards. The introduction says that pressure on time, budget and resources can stop teams from pausing to address technical and project risks before it is too late in the project. Its example is a call-centre project for a global corporate travel company, expected to support 4,000 users, that had left performance testing out of scope.

Where this topic fits

Success by Design maps an implementation into five phases: Discover, Initiate, Implement, Prepare and Operate. Reviews act as the gates. The Solution Blueprint Review starts in Discover and is mandatory, and its findings lead to Implementation Reviews. The Go-live Readiness Review in the Prepare phase is also mandatory and is called the last stop for assessing remaining risks before go-live. It corresponds to the production deployment gate in this lesson.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start the Solution Blueprint Review during Discover, so that architectural risks surface before design decisions become costly to reverse.
  2. Record each review's output as findings of three types (assertions, risks and issues) and attach a recommendation. In the travel-company example the recommendation was to add performance testing to the test cycle.
  3. By the Prepare phase, finish building and testing, and get ready for the final round of user acceptance testing and for training.
  4. Put in place what the customer's organisation requires, which the page lists as customer approvals, completed information security reviews, a cutover plan with go/no-go criteria, scheduled mock go-lives, a support model and a deployment runbook.
  5. Hold the go-live readiness review as a workshop with all parties, work through the go-live checklist, and write a mitigation plan with an owner for each action.
  6. Decide after the review whether the go-live date is feasible, then move to Operate, where the goal is stabilisation.

Why this approach fits

By the time a project reaches cutover, most remaining risks are organisational: untested volumes, untrained users, an unrehearsed migration, nobody staffed to support the first week. A fixed review with a checklist and named sign-offs forces those to be examined while the date can still move. Making the review mandatory matters because a team under deadline pressure is the one least likely to schedule it voluntarily.

Limitation or tradeoff

The framework is written for Dynamics 365 and assumes Microsoft's tooling and, usually, a partner-led project. Microsoft states that it does not guarantee implementation outcomes. A review is only as honest as the answers given in it. The guidance also warns about timing: started a few days before go-live it risks a delay, and started too early the project may not be mature enough to give reliable results.

What the sources document

  • By the Prepare phase, the page says, all necessary customer approvals are granted, information security reviews are completed, the cutover plan including go/no-go criteria is defined, mock go-lives are scheduled, the support model is readied and the deployment runbook is completed with tasks, owners, durations and dependencies. [8]
  • The Solution Blueprint Review and the Go-live Readiness Review are both described as mandatory, and the latter as the last stop for assessing any remaining risks before go-live. [8]
  • The go-live readiness plan should include signed-off system integration, user acceptance and performance testing, a signed-off cutover plan, completed user training and a signed-off production support plan. [9]
  • The FastTrack for Dynamics 365 team runs the go-live readiness review in a workshop format with all parties, and the page warns that starting the review just a few days before the go-live date risks a delay. [9]

Google SRE Production Readiness Reviews: handing a running service to the team that will operate it

GoogleDocumented in a case study

Chapter 32 of Google's Site Reliability Engineering book, The Evolving SRE Engagement Model, describes how a site reliability engineering (SRE) team takes over production responsibility for a service from the developers who built it, through a process called the Production Readiness Review (PRR). [10]

The problem

Few services begin life with SRE support, and the chapter says more development teams ask for it than SRE teams have capacity to serve. A team that accepts the pager for a service it does not understand, or for one that is not ready, inherits incidents it cannot handle. The chapter therefore describes the PRR as a prerequisite for an SRE team to accept responsibility for managing the production aspects of a service.

Where this topic fits

This is the handoff phase seen from the receiving team's side. In the Simple PRR Model the service has already launched, and ownership moves through six phases: engagement, analysis, improvements and refactoring, training, onboarding and continuous improvement.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Engagement: one to three SREs are chosen and agree with the development team on a service level objective, on any disruptive design changes needed for reliability, and on planning and training schedules.
  2. Analysis: the reviewers study the service against a checklist written for it, for example whether user-visible failures are monitored with suitable alerting, and look at recent incidents and postmortems.
  3. Improvements: findings are prioritised by their importance for reliability, negotiated with the developers, and fixed with both teams doing the work.
  4. Training: the reviewers train the whole SRE team through design overviews, deep dives on request flows, a description of the production setup and hands-on exercises.
  5. Onboarding: responsibilities move progressively, including parts of operations, the change management process and access rights, while the developers stay available to back up and advise.
  6. Continuous improvement: the SRE team keeps learning from operating the service and passes suggestions back to the developers.

Why this approach fits

The team that will be woken by the alerts decides, against its own checklist, whether the service is ready before it accepts ownership. That puts the gate where the consequences fall. Training followed by a staged transfer with the builders on backup recognises that knowledge moves more slowly than access rights do.

Limitation or tradeoff

It is expensive. The chapter reports that onboarding a service this way needed two or three SREs and typically lasted two or three quarters, one of the lessons that led Google to build shared frameworks and a common platform. A customer's operations team may have no comparable capacity, and an FDE engagement rarely has quarters to spend, so in practice the phases are compressed and the checklist is cut to what matters most.

What the sources document

  • The chapter states that a Production Readiness Review is considered a prerequisite for an SRE team to accept responsibility for managing the production aspects of a service. [10]
  • The Simple PRR Model proceeds through phases named engagement, analysis, improvements and refactoring, training, onboarding and continuous improvement. [10]
  • Onboarding is described as a progressive transfer of responsibilities and ownership, including parts of operations, the change management process and access rights, during which the development team must be available to back up and advise the SRE team for a period of time. [10]
  • In the chapter's Shakespeare service example, the review found that dashboards did not completely cover some metrics defined in the SLO. After the filed issues were fixed, SRE took over the pager while two developers remained in the on-call rotation. [10]
  • Among the lessons learned from the PRR-based engagement model, the chapter reports that onboarding each service required two or three SREs and typically lasted two or three quarters. [10]

4Common mistakes

  1. Reporting the phase from the calendar or the plan instead of from the artefacts that exist.

    Why it causes problems
    The status says pilot while the evaluation never happened, so real users are exposed to a system nobody measured, and the first failure is found by the customer's staff instead of by a test.
    How to recognise it
    Ask to see the artefact of the previous phase. If the signed brief or the evaluation report cannot be produced, the engagement is still in that earlier phase.
    How to fix it
    Report status as the last gate passed, with a link to the artefact and the name of the person who confirmed the exit criterion.

  2. Requesting access, security review and change approval at the moment the build needs them.

    Why it causes problems
    Each has a lead time of days or weeks, which then sits on the critical path. In the worked example this adds 28 working days with no change in engineering effort.
    How to recognise it
    The plan has no named approver or submission date for access, security or change control, or engineers are idle while they wait for credentials.
    How to fix it
    In the first week, list every approval with its owner, its lead time and the input it needs, and submit each one at the earliest point that input exists.

  3. Letting the prototype slide into production because the demo went well.

    Why it causes problems
    Code written to answer one question usually lacks access control, monitoring, error handling and rollback, and nobody has been trained to run it, so the first incident has no owner and no runbook.
    How to recognise it
    People outside the test group have the link, or real decisions are being taken from prototype output, before any go/no-go criteria have been written.
    How to fix it
    State in the brief that the prototype is disposable, and require the production gate (runbook, rollback, monitoring, named owner) for anything real users depend on.

  4. Choosing the pass mark after seeing the results.

    Why it causes problems
    Whatever was achieved becomes good enough, so the gate cannot fail and proves nothing. The disagreement it should have settled moves to go-live, when changing course costs most.
    How to recognise it
    The evaluation or pilot report states results, but the threshold appears in no document dated before the phase began.
    How to fix it
    Write the metric, the data set or sample size, the threshold and the person who signs into the brief before the phase starts, and change them only by written agreement.

  5. Running the pilot only with enthusiastic volunteers on easy cases.

    Why it causes problems
    The pilot passes, and the full rollout then meets sceptical users, unusual cases and peak load for the first time, just as the old process is being switched off.
    How to recognise it
    The pilot group was made up of people who asked to join, or the pilot report has no breakdown by case type or by user.
    How to fix it
    Choose pilot users and cases to match the real mix, including the hard case types and at least one sceptic, and report results for each group.

  6. Treating go-live as the end of the engagement.

    Why it causes problems
    The system runs until a dependency changes or the first incident happens, then stalls. The customer either calls the FDE back months later or stops using it.
    How to recognise it
    There is no named owner on the customer side, the runbook has been read only by its author, and follow-on work lives in chat threads.
    How to fix it
    Plan the handoff in the brief. Name the receiving team, train them before go-live, have them run a rollback themselves, and write a handover record with next steps as explicit tasks.

Exercise 1

Find the phase an engagement is really in

CodingBeginnerAbout 35 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Three engagements have sent status reports. Write a Python script, using only the standard library, that works out which phase each engagement is really in from its artefacts and sign-offs, and compares that with the phase it reported.

A phase's gate is passed only when its artefact exists and its exit criterion has been signed by someone. Start from this data:

python
PHASES = ["discovery", "scoping", "prototype", "pilot", "production", "handoff"]

ARTEFACT = {
    "discovery": "discovery summary",
    "scoping": "scope brief",
    "prototype": "evaluation report",
    "pilot": "pilot report",
    "production": "runbook",
    "handoff": "handover record",
}

ENGAGEMENTS = [
    {
        "name": "E1 contract search",
        "reported": "production",
        "artefacts": ["discovery summary", "scope brief", "runbook"],
        "signed": {"discovery": "sponsor", "scoping": "sponsor"},
    },
    {
        "name": "E2 ticket routing",
        "reported": "pilot",
        "artefacts": ["discovery summary", "scope brief", "evaluation report"],
        "signed": {"discovery": "sponsor", "scoping": "sponsor", "prototype": "ops lead"},
    },
    {
        "name": "E3 invoice matching",
        "reported": "handoff",
        "artefacts": ["discovery summary", "scope brief", "evaluation report",
                      "pilot report", "runbook"],
        "signed": {"discovery": "sponsor", "prototype": "finance lead",
                   "pilot": "sponsor", "production": "IT operations"},
    },
]

Requirements

  • Walk the phases in order and return the first phase whose gate has not been passed, together with what is missing (the artefact, the sign-off, or both).
  • Compare the actual phase with the reported phase and print OK when they match, or MISMATCH with the number of phases the report is ahead of the evidence.
  • Print, for every engagement, what is still needed to pass the gate of its actual phase.
  • Return the word closed when every gate has been passed.

Expected behaviour

E1 reports production but is really in prototype, two phases behind its report, because there is no evaluation report and no sign-off. E2 reports pilot and is in pilot. E3 reports handoff but is really in scoping, four phases behind, because the scope brief exists and was never confirmed by the customer.

Hint 1

A later artefact does not prove an earlier gate. E1 has a runbook, and that says nothing about whether an evaluation was done.

Hint 2

Loop over PHASES and return as soon as you find a phase that lacks its artefact or its sign-off. Do not keep going to later phases.

Hint 3

Use PHASES.index() on both phase names to count how far ahead the report is.

Show solution
python
# PHASES, ARTEFACT and ENGAGEMENTS as given in the prompt.

def actual_phase(engagement):
    """Return the first phase whose gate has not been passed, and what it lacks."""
    for phase in PHASES:
        missing = []
        if ARTEFACT[phase] not in engagement["artefacts"]:
            missing.append(f"no {ARTEFACT[phase]}")
        if phase not in engagement["signed"]:
            missing.append("exit criterion not signed")
        if missing:
            return phase, missing
    return "closed", []


for engagement in ENGAGEMENTS:
    phase, missing = actual_phase(engagement)
    reported = engagement["reported"]
    if phase == reported:
        verdict = "OK"
    else:
        ahead = PHASES.index(reported) - PHASES.index(phase)
        verdict = f"MISMATCH, report is {ahead} phase(s) ahead of the evidence"
    print(f"{engagement['name']}: reported={reported} actual={phase} -> {verdict}")
    print(f"    to pass the {phase} gate: {'; '.join(missing)}")

Output:

text
E1 contract search: reported=production actual=prototype -> MISMATCH, report is 2 phase(s) ahead of the evidence
    to pass the prototype gate: no evaluation report; exit criterion not signed
E2 ticket routing: reported=pilot actual=pilot -> OK
    to pass the pilot gate: no pilot report; exit criterion not signed
E3 invoice matching: reported=handoff actual=scoping -> MISMATCH, report is 4 phase(s) ahead of the evidence
    to pass the scoping gate: exit criterion not signed

Reading the result. E1 is the pattern from the lesson: a team writing a runbook for a system that was never evaluated. The right next action is the evaluation, not the deployment. E3 is the more dangerous case, because every later artefact exists and looks healthy. The customer never confirmed the brief, so nobody on their side has agreed what "done" means, and the dispute will surface at handoff. The fix is cheap today: take the brief back to the sponsor and get it confirmed, or find out what they disagree with.

The script as written treats an engagement with every gate passed as closed, and it would raise an error if such an engagement were compared with a reported phase, because closed is not in PHASES. Handle that case before using it on real data. It also only checks that evidence exists. Whether the evaluation report is any good still needs a person to read it.

Check your answer

  • Does your script report E1 as being in prototype and two phases ahead of the evidence?
  • Does E3 come out as scoping even though it has a pilot report and a runbook?
  • If you add "scoping" signed by "sponsor" to E3, does its actual phase become handoff and its verdict OK?
  • Can you say, for E2, which two things are needed before it may report production?
