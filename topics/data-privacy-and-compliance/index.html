Data Privacy and Compliance

Data privacy and compliance is the work of handling personal and sensitive data lawfully and provably: knowing what you hold, limiting where it goes, signing the right contract with every vendor that touches it, and keeping evidence that you did so. For LLM features it covers what a prompt contains, where a provider processes and stores it, and what the provider's contract and audit reports promise.

1Overview

Why it matters

Sending a customer's data to a language model is a disclosure to another company, under rules written before LLM APIs existed. A forward deployed engineer is usually the person who connects the model to the customer's systems, so the first security-review questions land on them: does the provider keep our prompts, in which country does it run them, is there a signed data processing agreement, can you show an audit report. When the answers are missing the project stalls in legal review, or it ships and later becomes a breach, a regulator inquiry or a lost contract.

Most of the work is concrete engineering and paperwork rather than law: limiting what each prompt contains, choosing a provider route whose retention and region settings match the contract, and recording the choice. This lesson covers the vocabulary of GDPR, HIPAA and SOC 2, what a data processing agreement and a business associate agreement contain, how residency and zero data retention are configured in real LLM APIs, and where each control stops working. It is an engineering introduction, not legal advice. The customer's counsel decides what the law requires.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify the data in a request as personal data, special-category data, protected health information or none of these, and say which rules each triggers.
  • Explain the six GDPR principles in Article 5 and apply data minimisation and pseudonymisation to an LLM prompt.
  • Distinguish a controller from a processor and list what a data processing agreement must contain under Article 28.
  • Explain what a HIPAA business associate agreement and Safe Harbor de-identification require, and what a SOC 2 report does and does not show.
  • Read a provider's retention and residency documentation and name what zero data retention and region pinning each leave uncovered.
  • Write a pre-flight check that blocks an LLM call whose route does not match the data's requirements.

Key terms

Personal data
Any information relating to an identified or identifiable person, whether directly (a name) or indirectly (an ID number, a location or an online identifier).
Special-category data
GDPR's most protected kinds of personal data, such as health, genetic, biometric and religious data, which Article 9 prohibits processing unless an exception applies.
Controller and processor
The controller decides why and how personal data is processed; a processor handles it on the controller's behalf, as an LLM API provider usually does for its customer.
Data processing agreement (DPA)
The contract between a controller and a processor that Article 28 of the GDPR requires, fixing what the processor may do with the data and what help it owes the controller.
Pseudonymisation
Processing personal data so it cannot be attributed to a person without extra information, such as a token map, that is kept separately and protected.
Protected health information (PHI)
Individually identifiable health information held by a HIPAA covered entity or its business associate, in any form.
Business associate agreement (BAA)
The HIPAA contract between a covered entity and a business associate, such as a cloud or model provider, that creates, receives, maintains or transmits PHI for it.
SOC 2 report
An independent CPA's report on a service organisation's controls, measured against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria, that customers request during vendor review.
Data residency
Configuration that fixes the geography where data is processed or stored, for example where model inference runs.
Zero data retention (ZDR)
A provider arrangement under which prompts and responses are not stored after the response is returned, apart from the exceptions the provider's contract lists.

2Explanation

Introduction

Think about posting a parcel through a courier. You decide what goes in the box, because the courier cannot lose what you never packed. You sign terms that say what the courier may do with it. You choose the route, and you can ask the courier not to keep a copy. Before you trust the courier with something valuable, you might ask to see its latest audit certificate. Sending data to an LLM API is the same transaction. Each step has a name in this lesson: data minimisation, the data processing agreement, data residency, zero data retention and the SOC 2 report.

Three regimes supply most of the vocabulary. The GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is the EU's law on personal data, meaning any information about an identifiable person. Whether it applies to a given deployment is a question for the customer's counsel, but if the data concerns people in the EU you should plan as if it does. HIPAA is the US framework for protected health information (PHI) held by health plans, health care providers and the companies that process data for them. SOC 2 is not a law at all. It is an auditor's report about a vendor's controls, and customers ask for it to judge whether a vendor is safe to use.

They differ in kind, which is why they are often confused. GDPR and HIPAA tell you what you must do with data. SOC 2 is evidence about how a vendor behaves. A data processing agreement and a business associate agreement are the contracts through which the two laws reach your vendors. Provider settings such as retention mode and region are the technical controls that make the contracts true.

The rest of the lesson follows one request on its way to a model: classify the data, apply the GDPR principles, check the contract, decide where the data may go, handle health data, collect assurance evidence, and pick the retention mode. A worked example then turns those steps into a small pre-flight check you can run yourself.

How it works

1. Classify the data first

Every later decision depends on what the data is. Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person, directly or through an identifier such as a name, ID number, location or online identifier (GDPR Article 4 (opens in a new tab)). Special-category data is the subset that Article 9 (opens in a new tab) prohibits processing unless an exception applies: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade-union membership, genetic data, biometric data used to identify a person, health data, and data about sex life or sexual orientation. PHI is individually identifiable health information handled by a HIPAA covered entity or business associate (45 CFR 160.103 (opens in a new tab)).

Special-category data usually enters an LLM application through free text. A ticket that says "I was in hospital last week" contains health data nobody planned to collect, and OWASP lists personal, financial and health data among what an LLM application can disclose (OWASP LLM02 (opens in a new tab)).

2. GDPR: six principles and accountability

Article 5 (opens in a new tab) sets six principles, and each becomes an engineering rule.

PrincipleIn an LLM feature
Lawfulness, fairness, transparencyChoose one Article 6 (opens in a new tab) lawful basis (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests) and disclose that an LLM is used.
Purpose limitationTickets collected to resolve a refund are not reused to tune an unrelated model.
Data minimisationSend only the fields the task needs.
AccuracyModel statements about a person can be wrong; do not store them as facts.
Storage limitationGive prompts, traces and embeddings a time limit.
Integrity and confidentialityAccess control (see Authentication and Authorization) and encryption; no secrets in prompts.

Article 5(2) adds accountability: you must be able to demonstrate compliance, which in practice means a written record of each data flow. Replacing a name with a token while the token map stays in your database is pseudonymisation (Article 4(5)). Because the map can re-attribute the data, treat a pseudonymised prompt as personal data that is better protected, not as data outside the rules.

3. Controller, processor and the DPA

The controller decides why and how personal data is processed; a processor processes it on the controller's behalf (Article 4). When a customer sends its data to an LLM API, the customer is typically the controller and the provider a processor. Article 28(3) (opens in a new tab) requires a contract under which the processor acts only on documented instructions, binds its staff to confidentiality, applies the Article 32 security measures, follows the sub-processor rules, helps with data-subject requests, deletes or returns data when the service ends, and allows audits. A sub-processor needs the controller's prior specific or general written authorisation, and the processor stays liable for it.

A real DPA shows these clauses in practice. Anthropic's DPA (opens in a new tab) limits processing to documented instructions (B.2), allows fifteen days to object to a new sub-processor (C.3(b)), promises breach notice within 48 hours (G.1) and deletion within thirty days of termination (H.1), and incorporates the EU Standard Contractual Clauses (I.1). Read the schedules as well as the clauses: Schedule 1 of that DPA lists special categories of personal data as "None", so a customer planning to send health data should ask how the schedule will be completed. Which company is the processor depends on the route: Anthropic's documentation (opens in a new tab) names Anthropic as the processor on its own API and on Claude in Microsoft Foundry but the cloud provider on Amazon Bedrock, while Microsoft says its own addendum (opens in a new tab) governs the models it sells itself in Foundry. The same model family can therefore sit under different contracts, retention rules and eligible features depending on where you call it.

Article 33 (opens in a new tab) gives the controller 72 hours, where feasible, to notify the supervisory authority after becoming aware of a breach, and requires a processor to tell the controller without undue delay, so the notice time in the DPA matters. Article 83 (opens in a new tab) caps fines, for an undertaking, at the higher of EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover for breaches of the principles, special-category rules, data-subject rights and transfer rules, and at the higher of EUR 10 million or 2% for duties such as Articles 25 to 39. With turnover TTT in EUR million, the upper cap is max⁡(20, 0.04 T)\max(20,\ 0.04\,T)max(20, 0.04T).

4. Where data may go: residency and transfers

Data residency is a setting; a transfer is a legal event, personal data moving to a third country. The Commission's adequacy decision for the EU-US Data Privacy Framework was adopted on 10 July 2023, lets data flow to participating US companies, and sits alongside tools such as standard contractual clauses (European Commission (opens in a new tab)). It is under challenge: the General Court dismissed an action on 3 September 2025 and an appeal was filed on 31 October 2025, so one law-firm analysis advises keeping standard clauses as a fallback (WilmerHale (opens in a new tab)).

LLM APIs usually split residency in two. Anthropic separates inference_geo, where inference runs per request, from the workspace geo, where data rests (Anthropic (opens in a new tab)). Azure separates where processing happens, which Global and DataZone deployments can move, from storage at rest, which stays in the designated geography. A region pin narrows the transfer question but does not replace the DPA.

5. HIPAA in engineering terms

HIPAA covers PHI held by covered entities (health plans, clearinghouses and certain providers) and by their business associates: companies that create, receive, maintain or transmit PHI on a covered entity's behalf (45 CFR 160.103). A model provider that accepts PHI is in that position, which is why providers offer a BAA. Data is de-identified under 45 CFR 164.514(b) (opens in a new tab) either by an expert's documented finding that re-identification risk is very small, or by Safe Harbor: remove 18 kinds of identifier and have no actual knowledge that what remains could identify someone. The 18 include names, telephone, fax and email, Social Security and record numbers, account and device identifiers, URLs, IP addresses, biometric identifiers, face images, all date elements except the year, and geography below state level (a three-digit ZIP prefix survives only where that area holds more than 20,000 people). Removing names alone is not Safe Harbor.

6. SOC 2 is evidence, not law

A SOC 2 report is an independent CPA firm's examination of a service organisation's controls against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. A Type 1 report does not look back over a period. A Type 2 report also tests whether controls operated effectively over one, a rolling 12 months for Office 365 (Microsoft (opens in a new tab)). AWS shares its SOC 2 reports through AWS Artifact under an NDA (AWS (opens in a new tab)). The report supports your vendor review. By itself it says nothing about whether your use of the service complies with GDPR or HIPAA.

7. Retention: not training is not the same as not keeping

"The provider does not train on our data" and "the provider does not keep our data" are different claims. OpenAI says API data is not used for training unless you opt in, yet abuse-monitoring logs are kept for up to 30 days, and zero data retention needs prior approval (OpenAI (opens in a new tab)). Anthropic enables zero data retention per organisation and still lists exceptions. Stateful features add copies of their own, as the next diagram shows.

Worked example: A pre-flight check for one support ticket bound for an LLM API

This is a teaching scenario with invented data. A software company with EU customers wants an LLM to draft replies to billing tickets. Before any call is made, a small gate minimises the ticket, flags sensitive content and checks the provider route. The rules in the gate are our teaching policy, not legal requirements, and you can reproduce every line of output with python3.

Input. One ticket whose body contains a name, an email address, a phone number, a first name in passing and a health remark. The CRM record also holds an email field and a ticket id that the model does not need.

Candidate routes. global-default is a provider route with a signed DPA, processing in any region and default retention. eu-zdr has a signed DPA, processing pinned to the EU and retention mode none.

python
import re
from datetime import datetime, timedelta, timezone

TICKET = {
    "ticket_id": "T-1042",
    "customer_name": "Marta Kowalska",
    "customer_email": "marta.kowalska@example.eu",
    "body": (
        "Hi, this is Marta Kowalska. Invoice INV-20931 was charged twice. "
        "Call me on +48 22 555 0147 or write to marta.kowalska@example.eu. "
        "My colleague Piotr saw it too. I was in hospital last week, so please be quick."
    ),
}

EMAIL = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+(?:\.[\w-]+)+")
PHONE = re.compile(r"\+\d{2}(?:[ -]?\d{2,4}){2,4}")
HEALTH_WORDS = ("hospital", "diagnos", "medication", "surgery")
ALLOWED_REGIONS = {"eu"}


def minimise(ticket):
    """Return the text to send and the token map to keep locally."""
    body = ticket["body"]  # only the body leaves; id, email field and CRM record stay home
    found = [
        ("NAME", [ticket["customer_name"]]),  # exact match on a structured field
        ("EMAIL", EMAIL.findall(body)),
        ("PHONE", PHONE.findall(body)),
    ]
    token_map, text = {}, body
    for kind, values in found:
        for number, value in enumerate(dict.fromkeys(values), start=1):
            if value in text:
                token = f"[{kind}_{number}]"
                token_map[token] = value
                text = text.replace(value, token)
    return text, token_map


def special_category_hits(text):
    return [word for word in HEALTH_WORDS if word in text.lower()]


def gate(route, hits):
    reasons = []
    if not route["dpa_signed"]:
        reasons.append("no DPA with this processor")
    if route["region"] not in ALLOWED_REGIONS:
        reasons.append(f"region {route['region']} not allowed")
    if hits and route["retention"] != "none":
        reasons.append("health data needs retention=none")
    return ("BLOCK", reasons) if reasons else ("ALLOW", reasons)


safe_text, token_map = minimise(TICKET)
hits = special_category_hits(safe_text)
print("SENT :", safe_text)
print("MAP  :", token_map)
print("SPECIAL-CATEGORY HITS:", hits)

ROUTES = {
    "global-default": {"dpa_signed": True, "region": "global", "retention": "default"},
    "eu-zdr": {"dpa_signed": True, "region": "eu", "retention": "none"},
}
for name, route in ROUTES.items():
    verdict, reasons = gate(route, hits)
    print(f"GATE {name:<15} -> {verdict} {reasons}")

reply = "Thanks [NAME_1]. We have refunded INV-20931 and will confirm to [EMAIL_1]."
for token, value in token_map.items():
    reply = reply.replace(token, value)
print("REPLY:", reply)

aware = datetime(2026, 3, 6, 16, 30, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
print("BREACH DEADLINE:", (aware + timedelta(hours=72)).isoformat())
text
SENT : Hi, this is [NAME_1]. Invoice INV-20931 was charged twice. Call me on [PHONE_1] or write to [EMAIL_1]. My colleague Piotr saw it too. I was in hospital last week, so please be quick.
MAP  : {'[NAME_1]': 'Marta Kowalska', '[EMAIL_1]': 'marta.kowalska@example.eu', '[PHONE_1]': '+48 22 555 0147'}
SPECIAL-CATEGORY HITS: ['hospital']
GATE global-default  -> BLOCK ['region global not allowed', 'health data needs retention=none']
GATE eu-zdr          -> ALLOW []
REPLY: Thanks Marta Kowalska. We have refunded INV-20931 and will confirm to marta.kowalska@example.eu.
BREACH DEADLINE: 2026-03-09T16:30:00+00:00

The deadline line shows the Article 33 clock. If you became aware of a breach at 16:30 UTC on Friday 6 March 2026, 72 clock hours end at 16:30 UTC on Monday 9 March. The weekend counts, because the text sets a number of hours rather than working days. When the clock starts is a question for counsel, but the notice time in your DPA, such as the 48 hours above, decides how fast you hear.

What the result means. The gate does not make the feature compliant. It makes three decisions explicit and testable: what leaves the building, which route may carry it, and what you must be able to show later. "Piotr" is the reminder that pattern-based redaction is a filter with misses, so the route still has to be safe for what the filter misses. Your own logs need the same care; see LLM and Agent Observability.

Edge cases and limitations

Legal footing changes after you read it
Two live examples: the EU-US Data Privacy Framework was upheld by the General Court on 3 September 2025 and, according to the December 2025 law-firm analysis cited above, was then appealed to the Court of Justice. The Italian regulator's December 2024 decision against OpenAI now carries a note that it was temporarily removed from the regulator's site after Court of Rome judgment No. 4153/2026, published on 18 March 2026, upheld the opposition to it. We did not read that judgment or check the appeal's current status on a court source, so verify both before relying on either. Record the date you checked each legal basis, keep a fallback transfer tool, and ask counsel before relying on one decision.
A pseudonymised prompt is still personal data
Replacing a name with a token and keeping the map means the provider sees less, but your side can still re-attribute the text, and rare details in free text such as a job title, a street or an unusual event can identify someone with no token at all. Redaction lowers exposure. It does not take the prompt outside the DPA, residency and retention decisions.
Zero data retention has documented exceptions
Anthropic says that even under ZDR it may keep data required by law or flagged by its safety systems, with flagged inputs and outputs kept for up to 2 years, and some models it designates require 30-day retention and are unavailable under ZDR unless it authorises it. On Bedrock a model that requires retention is blocked while the account is set to none. Check eligibility per model, feature and route before you promise a customer zero retention.
Stateful features keep data a ZDR setting on one endpoint may not cover
Azure's Responses API, Assistants threads and stored completions persist message history in your tenant and geography. OpenAI's store parameter on the Responses API decides whether response data is kept, with 30 days given as the period, and ZDR forces it to false. Anthropic's Managed Agents sessions persist until you delete them. Find out which of these your framework switches on by default.
Deletion requests must reach every copy
Article 28(3) requires the processor contract to cover help with data-subject requests and deletion or return of data when the service ends. A deletion request must therefore reach your logs, traces, vector store and embeddings as well as anything stored by provider features. Keep a record of where each customer's data can sit, so one request can be executed and proven.
Sensitive details hide outside the message text
Anthropic's guidance says PHI can appear in message content, attached files, and file names and metadata, and warns that JSON schemas for structured outputs are cached separately without the same protections. AWS advises never placing sensitive information in tags or free-form name fields, because these can reach billing or diagnostic logs. Review every field a request and its resources carry, not only the prompt.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • A prompt, document or tool result can contain personal, health or confidential customer data, which is true of almost any LLM feature built on real customer records.
  • A customer's security or legal team will review the deployment and asks for a DPA, a BAA, a SOC 2 report or a data-flow description before go-live.
  • You are choosing between provider routes, such as a direct API, a cloud marketplace or a self-hosted model, and the data's requirements decide which route is allowed.
  • The data crosses a border, includes special-category data or PHI, or must be deletable on request.

Think twice when

  • The feature handles only public documents or synthetic test data, where a full review adds cost without reducing risk. Keep a short note confirming that the data is public or synthetic.
  • You are tempted to let a vendor badge such as SOC 2 or HIPAA readiness replace reading the contract, the covered features and the retention settings.

A simpler alternative. For an internal pilot on synthetic or public data, a one-page data-flow sketch and the provider's default terms are enough. That stops being enough when real personal data, special-category data or PHI enters a prompt, because from then on the DPA or BAA, the retention mode and the region must be settled before the first call.

Key takeaways

  • Classify the data first, because personal data, special-category data and PHI trigger different rules, and free text is the usual way sensitive data slips into a prompt.
  • GDPR's six principles become engineering rules, which are to send only what the task needs, give every copy an expiry, and keep a record that proves what you decided.
  • A DPA or BAA is the contract that governs a vendor acting as a processor or business associate, and which contract applies depends on the route you call as well as on the model.
  • Not training on your data and not keeping your data are different promises, so check retention, abuse monitoring, stateful features and the stated exceptions before promising zero retention.
  • Residency is a setting and a transfer is a legal event, so pin the region for inference and storage while keeping the transfer tool and the DPA in place.
  • SOC 2 reports and compliance badges are evidence about a vendor, and their type, period, scope and exceptions decide how much they prove about your deployment.

Example 1 of 5

Enforcing zero data retention for model calls with Amazon Bedrock retention modes and Anthropic's ZDR arrangement

Amazon Web ServicesDocumented in official docs

The Amazon Bedrock User Guide documents a data retention setting with modes such as none and default, plus aws_review for models whose provider requires human review, applied per Region at account or project level. Anthropic's API and data retention page documents its zero data retention arrangement for the Claude API and the exceptions to it. [19][18][15]

The problem

A customer whose policy forbids storing prompts and completions needs a control it can switch on, verify, and stop others from switching off. Providers also have safety reasons to retain some content, so zero retention is not equally available for every model or feature, and the customer has to find that out before promising it.

Where this topic fits

Retention sits at the provider boundary. It is chosen when the route is chosen, after the prompt has been minimised and before any model call, and it is recorded in the data-flow register as the setting you rely on.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Decide the strictest provider-side retention the data class allows, for example no provider storage for health data.
  2. Set the account-level retention mode to none in every Region the application will call, because the setting is per Region and does not carry over to other Regions.
  3. Check each model's allowed modes. A model that requires retention, such as one that needs the aws_review mode, is blocked while the account is set to none, so the model choice and the retention decision are made together.
  4. For a direct API route, ask the provider to enable zero data retention for the organisation and confirm which endpoints and features are eligible.
  5. Add an organisation-level policy that denies setting any mode other than none, so a later change cannot loosen the control unnoticed.
  6. Record the documented exceptions, such as retention required by law or for flagged content, in the register and in what you tell the customer.

Why this approach fits

A retention promise is only as strong as the easiest place it can be changed. Putting the requirement in an organisation-level policy turns a convention into something enforceable, and making the setting per Region matches how the service stores data. Choosing the model and the mode together avoids discovering late that the preferred model needs retention.

Limitation or tradeoff

Zero retention is a property of the provider's storage, not of your system: your own logs, traces and caches are untouched. It is also limited by contract and by model. Anthropic documents that it may still retain data required by law or flagged by its safety systems for up to 2 years, and that some models require 30-day retention. The page's example policies are written per endpoint family, so a policy that covers only one leaves the other open. A policy that blocks calls to a model can also surprise the team that needs it.

What the sources document

  • Bedrock lets you configure data retention per Region at account or project level. The none mode means no request or response data is written to durable storage by AWS or shared with the model provider. [19]
  • The setting is per Region and does not propagate to other Regions. If an account in a Region is set to none and a model requires retention, Bedrock blocks the request and returns an error. [19]
  • The page shows example service control policies, one for the bedrock-mantle actions and one for the Bedrock control-plane action PutAccountDataRetention, that deny setting any retention mode other than none, and describes the effect as ensuring no inference data is ever retained. [19]
  • AWS says model providers have no access to the Bedrock accounts that host their models, so they have no access to Bedrock logs or to customer prompts and completions. [18]
  • Under Anthropic's ZDR arrangement prompts and responses are not stored at rest after the API response is returned, enabled per organisation. Data required by law or flagged by safety systems may still be kept, with flagged inputs and outputs kept for up to 2 years, and designated models require 30-day retention and are unavailable under ZDR unless authorised. [15]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

Processing PHI through the Claude API: BAA, eligible features and enforced restrictions

AnthropicDocumented in official docs

Anthropic's API and data retention page documents HIPAA readiness for the Claude API. AWS's HIPAA compliance page documents how its business associate addendum is accepted and which services may handle PHI, and 45 CFR 160.103 defines the business associate role. [8][15][13]

The problem

A health-care customer wants a model to summarise clinical text. HIPAA treats a company that handles PHI on the customer's behalf as a business associate, so a signed contract and a limited set of covered features are needed before any PHI is sent. The provider's product also has to stop developers from drifting into features the contract does not cover.

Where this topic fits

This is the contract-and-configuration gate that comes before the pre-flight check in the worked example. The organisation is enabled for HIPAA readiness under a signed BAA, and the API then enforces which features can be used with it.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Confirm that the workflow involves PHI and that the customer is a covered entity or business associate, so every provider that receives the PHI needs a BAA.
  2. Execute the BAA for the organisation that will send PHI. Anthropic's page describes downloading the BAA and implementation guide in its console, and AWS's page describes accepting its BAA in AWS Artifact.
  3. Keep PHI-bearing traffic in a dedicated organisation, because readiness is enforced at the organisation level and cannot be switched off by an administrator once enabled.
  4. Use only features the provider lists as eligible. The API rejects a request containing a non-eligible feature with a 400 error that names the feature.
  5. Place PHI only in message content, attached files and their file names, never in JSON schemas, workspace names, user information, billing data or support tickets, which the page does not expect to hold PHI.
  6. Keep third-party tools, beta features and consumer products out of the PHI path, since the page lists them as outside HIPAA readiness.

Why this approach fits

HIPAA obligations attach to the contract and to how PHI is handled, not to the model. A provider that enforces its own eligibility list in the API turns a policy into a hard error, which is more reliable than asking every developer to remember it.

Limitation or tradeoff

The BAA covers what it names. Ineligible features, beta features, integrations the customer connects and the provider's consumer products sit outside it. The page also says Anthropic's HIPAA readiness does not cover Amazon Bedrock or Google Cloud and is not available on Claude Platform on AWS or Microsoft Foundry, so each platform's own compliance documentation applies there. Enabling readiness is a vendor safeguard and does not make the customer's whole workflow compliant, because the customer's own safeguards, access controls and decisions about what to send remain its own. Enablement being permanent also makes a mistake about which organisation was enabled costly.

What the sources document

  • 45 CFR 160.103 defines a business associate to include a person that creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity for regulated functions, and a subcontractor that does the same on behalf of a business associate. [8]
  • With a signed BAA and a HIPAA-enabled organisation, the Claude API supports processing PHI for eligible features. Enablement is permanent and cannot be disabled by an administrator, and the page advises separate organisations for HIPAA-ready and general-purpose access. [15]
  • When a HIPAA-enabled organisation sends a request with a non-eligible feature, the API returns a 400 error that lists the feature, as in the page's example response naming code execution. [15]
  • Workspace names, user information, billing data and support tickets are not expected to hold PHI, and JSON schemas for structured outputs are cached separately without the same protections, so PHI must stay out of schema property names, enum values, const values and patterns. [15]
  • AWS states that its BAA is the contract HIPAA requires, accepted through AWS Artifact, and that customers may use any AWS service in a HIPAA account but should process PHI only in the HIPAA-eligible services defined in the BAA. [13]

Example 3 of 5

Pinning where LLM prompts are processed: Azure Foundry deployment types and the Claude inference_geo parameter

MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

Microsoft Learn's data, privacy and security page for Foundry Models sold by Azure documents where prompts are processed and stored for each deployment type. Anthropic's data residency page documents a per-request inference_geo parameter and a separate workspace geo for storage. [21][16]

The problem

A customer's policy says personal data must stay within one jurisdiction. The providers, for performance and capacity, can route traffic wherever the model is deployed. The customer needs to know separately where processing happens and where data is stored, and which option moves one without the other.

Where this topic fits

Residency is chosen when the route is chosen, before the first call, and verified from response metadata afterwards. It sits beside the DPA and the transfer mechanism rather than replacing them.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. List what must stay in the region, namely prompts and responses in flight, data stored by features such as files and stored responses, and logs.
  2. On Azure, choose a deployment type that matches the rule. A regional deployment keeps processing in the geography, Global allows any geography where the model is deployed, and DataZone allows the wider data zone, for example EU member states.
  3. Confirm the storage side. For Global and DataZone deployments, uploaded data and the abuse-monitoring store still sit in the designated geography.
  4. On the Claude API, set inference_geo on each request, or set the workspace default and the allowed geographies, so that an omitted parameter cannot send traffic somewhere unintended.
  5. Read usage.inference_geo in the response and log it, so you can show where inference actually ran.
  6. Account for cost and availability. The page lists a 1.1x price multiplier for US-only inference on Claude 4.6 and later models, and older models reject the parameter.

Why this approach fits

Separating processing from storage matches how these services work: capacity pooling changes where compute runs, while data at rest is a different decision. Per-request parameters and workspace allow lists let a team enforce the rule centrally and still override it deliberately.

Limitation or tradeoff

Residency is narrower than a transfer analysis. Anthropic currently offers only US and global inference and only a US workspace geo, so a customer needing EU-only inference on the first-party API cannot get it from these settings and has to look at another route, such as a cloud platform where the endpoint or inference profile fixes the region. Azure says processing may move between regions within the chosen geography for operational purposes. Support access, sub-processors and logs outside the provider remain questions for the DPA.

What the sources document

  • Azure states that prompts and responses are processed within the customer-specified geography unless a Global or DataZone deployment type is used, and may be processed between regions within that geography for operational purposes. [21]
  • For Global deployments processing may occur in any geography where the model is deployed. For DataZone deployments it may occur anywhere in the specified data zone, so a resource in an EU member state may process in that or any other EU member nation. [21]
  • For Global and DataZone deployments, data at rest such as uploaded data and the abuse-monitoring data store stays in the customer-designated geography, and only the location of processing changes. [21]
  • Anthropic provides two independent settings, inference geo (set per request with inference_geo, values global or us) and workspace geo for storage at rest, where only us is currently available. [16]
  • Anthropic prices US-only inference at 1.1x the standard rate for Claude 4.6 and later models, and sending inference_geo to older models returns a 400 error. [16]

Example 4 of 5

Using SOC 2 reports in vendor review: AWS Artifact and Microsoft's Type 2 attestation

Amazon Web Services and MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

AWS's Artifact documentation and SOC FAQ describe how customers obtain AWS's SOC reports. Microsoft's SOC 2 Type 2 compliance page describes what its report evaluates and where customers should look in it. [12][11][10]

The problem

A customer's security team cannot audit every cloud and AI vendor itself. It needs independent evidence about each vendor's controls that it can show to its own auditors, and it needs to know exactly what that evidence leaves out.

Where this topic fits

SOC 2 evidence arrives during vendor review, before the contract is signed or production data is sent, and is renewed when the vendor publishes its next report. It feeds the register next to the DPA and the retention settings.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Request the vendor's current SOC 2 report, for example by downloading it from AWS Artifact, and accept the NDA the vendor requires.
  2. Check the report type and period. A Type 1 report does not look back over a period, while a Type 2 report evaluates operating effectiveness over a period such as 12 months.
  3. Check scope. Confirm that the service and feature you will use are among the in-scope services and which Trust Services Criteria categories the report covers.
  4. Read the exceptions and the management responses near the end of the report, and decide whether any touch the controls you rely on.
  5. Read the user entity responsibilities and map each to a control on your side, because the report assumes customers do their part.
  6. Record the report, its period and any bridge letter in the register, and set a reminder for the next report.

Why this approach fits

A report from an independent CPA firm gives the customer's auditors something they accept as evidence, at far lower cost than a bespoke audit of each vendor. Reading exceptions and user entity responsibilities turns the document into a list of concrete follow-ups instead of a badge.

Limitation or tradeoff

A SOC 2 report is shared under an NDA and describes the vendor's controls during one period. It can be months old when you read it, may not include a newer AI product in its scope, and says nothing about whether your configuration or your data use meets GDPR or HIPAA. AWS states plainly that customers remain responsible for documents that demonstrate their own compliance.

What the sources document

  • AWS Artifact provides on-demand downloads of AWS security and compliance documents, including SOC reports, which customers can submit to auditors or regulators as audit artifacts. AWS says customers remain responsible for developing or obtaining documents that demonstrate their own compliance. [12]
  • AWS describes its SOC 2 report as covering controls against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria for Security, Availability, Confidentiality and Privacy, available through AWS Artifact, and says an NDA is required to review it. [11]
  • Microsoft says a SOC 2 Type 2 report evaluates whether controls were designed appropriately, in operation on a specified date, and operating effectively over a specified period, and that Type 1 audits do not look back over a period. [10]
  • Microsoft's Office 365 Type 2 examinations cover a rolling 12-month window, reports are usually issued a few months after the period ends, and bridge letters are self-attestations that cover the gap. [10]
  • Microsoft points readers to the end of the report for management responses to exceptions and for user entity responsibilities, the control duties customers need to meet for the system as a whole to satisfy the SOC 2 standards. [10]

Example 5 of 5

Masking personal data in prompts and responses with Amazon Bedrock Guardrails and the open-source Presidio library

Amazon Web ServicesDocumented in official docs

The Amazon Bedrock User Guide documents sensitive information filters in Bedrock Guardrails that block or mask PII in prompts and responses. Presidio's documentation describes an open-source library, moving from Microsoft to community governance, for detecting and anonymising PII in text, which you can run inside your own boundary. OWASP's LLM02 entry lists the mitigation. [20][26][27][25]

The problem

Free-text prompts contain personal data the task does not need, and model responses can repeat or invent it. Data minimisation requires removing what the task does not need before it reaches the provider, and checking what comes back, without relying on every user to type carefully.

Where this topic fits

This control sits on the request path, after classification and before the provider call for input, and again on the model's response. It implements the minimisation principle and complements the DPA and retention settings instead of replacing them. See Guardrails and Content Safety for the wider pattern.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. List the PII types the feature must never send, such as names, email addresses, phone numbers and national ID numbers, plus custom patterns such as internal order numbers.
  2. For each type choose block, which rejects the request or response, or mask, which replaces the value with a type label. Configure input and output separately.
  3. Apply the filter to every prompt and every model response. With Presidio, the analyzer finds entities and the anonymizer applies an operator such as replace, redact, mask, hash or encrypt.
  4. Give the detector context. The Bedrock page says the detector performs better with surrounding text than with isolated words.
  5. Plan for the gaps. The Bedrock page says tool arguments, tool results, tool definitions and invocation logs are not masked, so cover them with other controls.
  6. Test the filter on labelled examples with known PII and count the misses, then decide whether the remaining risk is acceptable for the route and contract in use.

Why this approach fits

A filter in the request path applies the rule uniformly, including to text users paste in. The choice between block and mask lets low-risk fields be masked while high-risk ones stop the request. An open-source analyzer that you run yourself keeps the raw text inside your boundary until it has been cleaned.

Limitation or tradeoff

Detection is statistical. Bedrock calls its filter probabilistic, Presidio warns it cannot guarantee finding all sensitive information, and the worked example let a first name in passing through. Masking does not reach logs: when invocation logging is enabled the log keeps the original request, and the guardrail trace returns the original matched value. Masked text can also remove detail the task needs.

What the sources document

  • Bedrock Guardrails sensitive information filters detect PII in input prompts and model responses with a probabilistic, context-dependent ML approach, and can be set to block or to mask, replacing a masked value with its type such as NAME or EMAIL. Built-in types and custom regular expressions are supported, with separate input and output actions. [20]
  • Masking applies only to content sent to and returned from the model. It does not apply to model invocation logs, where the input field keeps the original request when that logging is enabled, and the trace match field contains the original PII value. [20]
  • In tool-use workloads the filter does not evaluate tool call arguments, tool results or tool definitions, so PII in those fields is neither blocked nor masked. [20]
  • Presidio's analyzer finds PII in text using named-entity recognition, regular expressions, rules and checksums, its anonymizer offers operators including replace, redact, mask, hash and encrypt, and its documentation says there is no guarantee that it finds all sensitive information. [26][27]
  • OWASP's LLM02 entry lists sanitisation of data, access controls, and tokenisation or redaction of sensitive content before processing among its mitigations for sensitive information disclosure. [25]

4Common mistakes

  1. Treating "the provider does not train on our data" as "the provider does not keep our data".

    Why it causes problems
    Providers can keep prompts for abuse monitoring or for stateful features even when they never train on them. OpenAI's page gives abuse-monitoring logs as kept for up to 30 days by default, and Anthropic may keep flagged content for up to 2 years. A customer told that nothing is stored finds out otherwise during an audit.
    How to recognise it
    Your claim to the customer cites a training policy rather than a retention setting, a ZDR approval, or console or API output that shows the setting is on.
    How to fix it
    Write the retention claim from the contract and the verified setting, name the documented exceptions, and check the setting on the account before go-live. Azure, for example, shows a ContentLogging value of false when abuse-monitoring storage is off.

  2. Redacting the prompt but leaving the raw text in your own logs, traces and vector store.

    Why it causes problems
    The copies you control are the easiest to leak and the hardest to delete. Bedrock documents that its invocation log, when you enable it, keeps the original request even when a guardrail masks the model's input, so masking in the request path does not clean up what your platform records.
    How to recognise it
    Send a test email address through the system, then search the observability and storage tools for it. Any hit is a copy that needs an expiry or a redaction step.
    How to fix it
    Log the redacted copy, give every store a retention time, use your platform's log-masking features, and list each store in the data-flow register so deletion requests can reach it.

  3. Assuming a SOC 2 report or a HIPAA-ready label means your deployment is compliant.

    Why it causes problems
    A SOC 2 report is an auditor's opinion on a vendor's controls for one period, and HIPAA readiness covers the features the provider lists. Neither judges your configuration, your lawful basis or the data you send, so the gap stays open while the checkbox looks closed.
    How to recognise it
    The compliance section of your design cites a vendor page, and nobody has read the report's scope and exceptions or the provider's list of eligible features.
    How to fix it
    Read scope, period and exceptions, map the user entity responsibilities to your own controls, restrict requests to eligible features, and have the customer's counsel confirm the lawful basis and the agreements.

  4. Signing an agreement for one route and calling the model through another.

    Why it causes problems
    The processor and the contract differ by route. A DPA with a cloud provider does not cover calls sent directly to the model developer's API, and the retention settings and eligible features differ between the two. The data flow then has no contract behind it.
    How to recognise it
    A production configuration uses an endpoint, key or deployment that does not appear in the register beside a signed agreement.
    How to fix it
    Record the route, endpoint, region and contract together, and block endpoints that are not in the register at the gateway.

  5. Putting sensitive data where no protection covers it, such as JSON schemas, tags and free-form name fields.

    Why it causes problems
    Anthropic says schemas used for structured outputs are cached separately without the same PHI protections, and AWS warns that tags and free-form name fields may reach billing or diagnostic logs. The data then sits in a place the BAA or DPA never contemplated.
    How to recognise it
    Resource names, tags, tool definitions or schema enums contain a customer name, a patient identifier or a sample record.
    How to fix it
    Use opaque identifiers, keep patient-specific text in message content only, and review every field that a request and its resources carry.

  6. Pinning inference to a region and assuming nothing leaves it.

    Why it causes problems
    Residency settings cover specific things. Azure says processing can move between regions within a geography, Anthropic's storage geo currently has one option, and logs, support access, sub-processors and your own copies are separate questions.
    How to recognise it
    The residency claim is a single sentence such as "we use the EU region", with no list of where prompts, logs and stored data actually rest.
    How to fix it
    Draw the data path, list every place a copy can rest, and confirm each location in the provider's documentation or contract.

Exercise 1

Lint a data-flow register for LLM features

CodingIntermediateAbout 40 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

A team records every LLM data flow in a register. Write a Python script, standard library only, that checks each record against these teaching rules and prints one result per flow. The rules are ours, not legal requirements.

  • R1 ERROR: the flow sends personal data and has no DPA.
  • R2 ERROR: the flow sends personal data and its region is not in the allowed set, which is just eu.
  • R3 ERROR: the flow carries PHI and has no BAA.
  • R4 ERROR: the flow carries special-category data and the provider retention mode is not none.
  • R5 WARN: the team's own logs are kept longer than the purpose needs.
  • R6 ERROR: the record is missing a required field, in which case report that and skip the other rules.

Use this register. The required fields are every key shown in F1.

REGISTER = [

Requirements

  • Print `PASS` or `FAIL` for each flow id, where any ERROR means FAIL and a WARN alone still passes.
  • Print each finding on its own indented line with its level and a clear message.
  • Do not flag flow F4 for region or DPA, because it carries no personal data.
  • Keep the rules in a function that returns a list of findings, so the rules can be tested without printing.

Expected behaviour

F1 and F4 pass. F2 fails because it has PHI and no BAA. F3 fails on region and also warns that its own logs are kept 90 days when the purpose needs 30. F5 fails on the missing DPA and on retention for special-category data. F6 fails as an incomplete record because it has no processor.

Hint 1

Check for missing keys first and return early, so a record without a processor never reaches the other rules.

Hint 2

Compare the allowed region only when the flow has personal data, since a flow without personal data may legitimately run anywhere.

Hint 3

Return tuples of level and message, then derive PASS or FAIL from whether any level is ERROR.

Show solution
python
ALLOWED_REGIONS = {"eu"}
REQUIRED = ("id", "purpose", "processor", "region", "personal", "special", "phi",
            "dpa", "baa", "provider_retention", "own_log_days", "purpose_days")


def lint(flow):
    missing = [field for field in REQUIRED if field not in flow]
    if missing:
        return [("ERROR", "incomplete record, missing " + ", ".join(missing))]
    findings = []
    if flow["personal"] and not flow["dpa"]:
        findings.append(("ERROR", "personal data sent to a processor without a DPA"))
    if flow["personal"] and flow["region"] not in ALLOWED_REGIONS:
        findings.append(("ERROR", f"region {flow['region']} is not allowed for personal data"))
    if flow["phi"] and not flow["baa"]:
        findings.append(("ERROR", "PHI without a BAA"))
    if flow["special"] and flow["provider_retention"] != "none":
        findings.append(("ERROR", "special-category data needs provider retention none"))
    if flow["own_log_days"] > flow["purpose_days"]:
        findings.append(("WARN", f"own logs kept {flow['own_log_days']} days, purpose needs {flow['purpose_days']}"))
    return findings


for flow in REGISTER:  # REGISTER as given in the prompt
    findings = lint(flow)
    status = "FAIL" if any(level == "ERROR" for level, _ in findings) else "PASS"
    print(f"{flow['id']} {status}")
    for level, message in findings:
        print(f"   {level}: {message}")
text
F1 PASS
F2 FAIL
   ERROR: PHI without a BAA
F3 FAIL
   ERROR: region global is not allowed for personal data
   WARN: own logs kept 90 days, purpose needs 30
F4 PASS
F5 FAIL
   ERROR: personal data sent to a processor without a DPA
   ERROR: special-category data needs provider retention none
F6 FAIL
   ERROR: incomplete record, missing processor

The function returns findings and does no printing, so each rule can be unit tested with a one-field change to a copy of a passing record. F4 passes despite its global region and missing DPA because the region and DPA rules only apply when personal is true. F1 passes with default provider retention because it carries no special-category data. The linter checks that the register is consistent, not that it is true: a wrong dpa: True still passes, which is why the register needs a named owner and evidence links.

  • Does your output mark exactly F2, F3, F5 and F6 as FAIL and F1 and F4 as PASS?

  • If you change F4 to personal true, does it fail on both region and DPA?
  • Can you call the rules function without any output being printed?

Exercise 2

Write a vendor decision memo for a clinical note summariser

AnalysisIntermediateAbout 45 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

A US group of three clinics wants an LLM to summarise visit notes for clinicians. The notes contain patient names, dates of birth and diagnoses. You review a fictional vendor, Vendor X, whose documentation states:

  • HIPAA readiness is available with a standard BAA signed in the console. It is enforced per organisation and cannot be switched off once enabled.
  • Zero data retention is available on request per organisation, but flagged content may still be kept for up to 2 years.
  • Inference runs in the US or globally. The storage geography is US only.
  • Batch processing and code execution are marked not eligible for HIPAA organisations. Structured outputs are eligible only if no PHI appears in schemas.
  • The latest SOC 2 Type 2 report covers 12 months that ended 7 months ago. It lists one exception about quarterly access reviews and says customers must run their own user access reviews.
  • The vendor's DPA promises breach notice within 48 hours.

The developers propose a shared organisation for all products, a JSON schema containing an enum of patient names for extraction, overnight batch summaries, and logging full prompts to the tracing tool for 90 days.

Write a one-page decision memo that says what can proceed, what must change, and what you will ask the vendor and the clinic group's counsel.

Requirements

  • Identify at least four proposed design choices that conflict with the vendor's documented limits or with data minimisation, and say how to change each one.
  • State which agreement is the primary one for this data and say who decides whether a DPA is also relevant.
  • List what you will ask for about the SOC 2 report and what the clinic must do about the user entity responsibility.
  • End with open questions for counsel that the engineering team cannot answer.

Expected behaviour

A good memo approves the BAA route with a dedicated organisation and rejects four proposals as written: the shared organisation, patient names in the schema, batch processing and 90-day full-prompt tracing. It asks for a bridge letter for the 7-month gap, reviews the access-review exception, and assigns the clinic its own access reviews. It leaves the legal questions to counsel.

Hint 1

For each proposal ask which documented statement it contradicts, then find the smallest change that removes the conflict.

Hint 2

A report period that ended 7 months ago leaves a gap. Ask what evidence covers it and who produces that evidence.

Hint 3

Your own logs are not covered by the vendor's retention arrangement, so decide what the tracing tool should store.

Show solution

Decision: proceed on the BAA route, with four changes.

  1. Shared organisation. Readiness is enforced per organisation and is permanent, so create a dedicated organisation for the summariser and keep other products out of it. The vendor's other products keep general-purpose access.
  2. Patient names in a schema enum. Schemas are handled differently from message content, so remove the enum. Extract names from message content, or send an opaque patient token and keep the mapping inside the clinic's systems.
  3. Batch processing. It is marked not eligible, and a HIPAA organisation would receive an error. Use the synchronous eligible path with a queue on the clinic side, and ask the vendor whether batch is planned.
  4. Ninety-day full-prompt tracing. The vendor's retention arrangement does not reach the clinic's own logs. Store a redacted copy or only metadata, set a short expiry matched to the clinical purpose, restrict access, and list the tracing store in the register.

Contracts and settings. The BAA is the primary agreement because the notes are PHI. Whether a DPA also applies depends on whether any patient data is subject to GDPR, which is for counsel to decide. Zero data retention is a separate arrangement. Ask the vendor whether HIPAA readiness already covers retention, and record the 2-year flagged-content exception.

SOC 2. The period ended 7 months ago, so nothing in the report covers the last 7 months. Ask for a bridge letter and for the in-scope service list to confirm the summariser's features are included. Read the access-review exception and the vendor's response, and decide whether it touches controls the clinic relies on. The user entity responsibility means the clinic must run its own user access reviews on the accounts that can reach PHI, on a schedule its security officer sets, and assign an owner.

Questions for counsel. Does the 48-hour breach notice fit the clinic's own notification duties? Is the lawful use of the notes for summarisation covered by existing patient notices? Would a de-identification route that meets Safe Harbor be realistic for free-text notes, or should the notes always be treated as PHI?

Check your answer

  • Did you reject or change all four proposals and tie each to a documented limit or to data minimisation?
  • Did you keep patient names out of schemas, tags and resource names?
  • Did you treat the clinic's own tracing logs as outside the vendor's retention arrangement?
  • Did you leave legal conclusions to counsel instead of declaring the design compliant?
