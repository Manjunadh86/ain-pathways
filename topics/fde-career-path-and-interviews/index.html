Becoming an FDE (Career Paths and Interviews)

Becoming a forward deployed engineer means building the mix of production engineering, customer-facing skill and proof of shipped work that FDE employers screen for, then showing it through a hiring process that tests how you handle vague problems as well as how you code.

1Overview

Why it matters

Interest in the role is high and the entry points are unclear. Salesforce, which has said it will build a team of 1,000 forward deployed engineers, cites an analysis by Indeed and the Financial Times that FDE job postings rose by more than 800% between January and September 2025, and describes competition for the jobs as fierce (Salesforce (opens in a new tab)). At the same time the title covers very different jobs, from Palantir's engineers who own whole customer projects to roles that are closer to technical consulting (Pragmatic Engineer (opens in a new tab)). A candidate who prepares only for a standard software interview can miss what these employers actually screen for.

This lesson is for people who want the job and are deciding what to build, learn or change first. It is also for engineers, solutions engineers and consultants who work beside FDEs and want to know what a good hire looks like. The practical point is that FDE hiring asks for evidence that an ordinary CV does not carry: something you shipped that someone used, and a record of working out what to build when the problem was unclear.

Before you start

  • What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?Beginner

What you will be able to do

  • Match your own background to an entry route (software, data, solutions, consulting, founder or career changer) and name the usual gap that route leaves.
  • Build a skills matrix from employer-written sources and score yourself against it using evidence rather than impressions.
  • Calculate a gap priority for each skill and turn it into an hour-by-hour preparation plan.
  • Describe the interview stages that employers document or that preparation guides report, and say what each stage is testing.
  • Apply published rules about AI use in applications, take-homes and live interviews.
  • Design a portfolio project that shows deployed work, with a live URL, a measured result and a short written case study.

Key terms

Entry route
The kind of earlier job or experience, such as software engineering, data work, solutions engineering, consulting or founding a company, from which a person moves into an FDE role.
Skills matrix
A table that lists the skills employers name and records, for each one, how many sources name it and how strong your own evidence is.
FDSE
Forward Deployed Software Engineer, the title Palantir uses for engineers who work directly with customers to understand their problems and design and implement solutions that use data.
Deployment strategist
A colleague who works beside FDEs on customer engagements. Salesforce describes the role as identifying the best use case and shaping the overall AI strategy while the FDEs design, build and deploy.
Open-ended question
An interview problem with no single best answer, where the interviewer wants to see the alternatives you consider, the trade-offs you name and the concrete approach you commit to.
Take-home assessment
A task you complete on your own time and submit for review. Some employers state that AI tools are not allowed on it unless the instructions say so.
Customer simulation
An interview round, reported by preparation guides, in which the interviewer plays a customer and watches how you ask questions, explain and take ownership.
Portfolio of deployed work
A small set of projects that are running somewhere a reviewer can open, each with a stated user, a measured result and a short account of the decisions you made.
Gap priority
In this lesson, a skill's weight multiplied by the distance between your current level and the target level; the larger the number, the sooner the skill deserves practice time.

2Explanation

Introduction

If you have applied for a software job, you know the usual shape: a CV, a coding test and a few interviews. Applying to be a forward deployed engineer (FDE) uses the same parts, but each one is checking for something different. The job, as described in What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?, is production software engineering done inside a customer's situation. An employer therefore wants two things that a normal CV does not show: that you can build something that works in someone else's environment, and that you can work out what to build when the problem arrives vague.

This lesson follows the order a candidate meets things in. First come entry routes, the earlier jobs people come from and what each one brings and lacks. Next is a skills matrix, a table of what employers write down that you can score yourself against. Then come the interview stages, what a loop usually contains and what each part tests. Last is the portfolio of deployed work: evidence, public where possible, that you have shipped something real.

A note on evidence. Hiring practice is documented unevenly. Palantir publishes interview guides, Ramp and Anthropic publish parts of their approach, and most other companies publish only job postings. Where this lesson leans on interview-preparation sites that collect candidate reports, it says so and treats them as checklists rather than facts about any one company. Job postings change and disappear, so every posting cited here carries an access date of 11 October 2026.

The diagram above is a map, not a ranking. None of these routes is the correct one, and the gap column is our reading of the postings rather than a finding from them. What matters is that you can say which row you are in, because the gap decides what to practise first.

How it works

1. What employers write down

Put several employer-written descriptions side by side and the same requirements keep appearing. Palantir's new-graduate Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting compares the job to a startup CTO's: small teams, little supervision and end-to-end ownership of high-stakes projects (Palantir posting (opens in a new tab)). Ramp evaluates drive, engineering fundamentals, customer empathy and communication (Ramp (opens in a new tab)). Anthropic's posting asks for Python, shipped production applications, production experience with large language models and customer discovery skills (Anthropic posting, London (opens in a new tab)).

Three requirements recur: engineering fundamentals shown by shipped code, comfort with ambiguity and with systems you did not write, and communication with people who are not engineers. The seniority bar differs. The Pragmatic Engineer's survey of FDE adverts reports that almost every recruiter wants a solid software engineering background plus some real-world experience, that Ramp prefers 5 or more years for senior FDE roles but hires some exceptional new graduates, and that Palantir hires people with as little as a year of post-college experience (Pragmatic Engineer (opens in a new tab)).

2. Entry routes

Every route carries something over and leaves a gap; the gaps are our reading, not a statistic.

  • Software engineer. The most common base. The roadmap.sh FDE roadmap says FDE is not a beginner role and that you will most probably already be a full-stack developer (roadmap.sh (opens in a new tab)). Usual gap: evidence of working with users.
  • Data engineer or data scientist. Palantir's posting lists data science among preferred backgrounds and asks for interest in large-scale data. Usual gap: deployable services and web apps.
  • Solutions or sales engineer. The Pragmatic Engineer says solutions architect, sales engineer and technical delivery roles sit close to FDE; the difference is that an FDE also contributes to the product. Salesforce describes a technical architect who moved into its FDE team through a six-week onboarding programme (Salesforce (opens in a new tab)). Usual gap: owning production code rather than demos.
  • Consultant. Anthropic's FDE postings accept a software engineer with consulting experience (the London posting says consulting or product experience), and the US copy lists professional-services experience as a plus. Usual gap: hands-on engineering you can show.
  • Technical founder. Seven of Ramp's 16 FDEs were previous founders when it wrote its post, and Anthropic encourages former technical founders to apply.
  • Career changer. Natalie Meurer of Sierra describes moving from technology policy, by learning to code on the side, to an engineer role on Palantir's privacy team, then business school and Sierra (Latent Space (opens in a new tab)). Usual gap: a first credible engineering role.

New graduates are a real route at some employers: Palantir's new-graduate posting prefers an engineering background such as computer science, mathematics, software engineering, physics or data science. That US government posting also requires an active clearance, so check constraints such as clearance and travel early.

3. The skills matrix

A skills matrix turns scattered postings into a plan. Collect three to six employer-written sources, list the skills they name, and group them into a handful of areas. For each area record how many sources name it, its weight www. Then score yourself from 0 to 3. Level 0 means no exposure, 1 a tutorial or notebook, 2 used once on something real with a user, and 3 used repeatedly so that you could teach it. Give yourself 2 or 3 only when you can point to an artefact.

With a target level ttt of 3 and your current level cic_ici​, the priority of skill iii is:

priorityi=wi×(t−ci)\text{priority}_i = w_i \times (t - c_i)priorityi​=wi​×(t−ci​)

A skill many sources name and where you are weak scores highest; a skill you already have scores zero however often it is named. The weight is a crude proxy: it counts mentions in a convenience sample, not importance.

Coding, customer communication and ownership appear in all four columns. Large language model experience is explicit for Salesforce and Anthropic, whose FDEs build agents and applications on language models, and is not among the Palantir interview-guide competencies or the Ramp hiring axes. Palantir's guides add one thing the others only imply: working inside existing systems. Palantir says developers spend far more time modifying existing code than writing new code (Palantir (opens in a new tab)).

4. Interview stages

What employers document. Palantir says everyone starts with one or two phone interviews and, if there is a fit, comes on site, in person or virtually, for interviews built around engineering competencies (Palantir (opens in a new tab)). Phone interviews typically last 20 to 45 minutes and, for engineers, include a coding question (Palantir (opens in a new tab)). On site it asks how you have executed, challenged yourself, motivated others, made judgments and failed. Anthropic says interviews run over Google Meet, technical roles use live coding tools such as Colab and CodeSignal, and you may look things up (Anthropic (opens in a new tab)).

What preparation guides report. Aced, an interview-preparation platform, describes a typical FDE loop of 5 to 8 stages: recruiter screen, hiring-manager screen on past projects, coding, system design, an open-ended case, a role-play where the interviewer plays the customer, a behavioural round and sometimes a take-home (Aced (opens in a new tab)). Most of its figures are unsourced, so use it as a list of things to prepare for, not as any company's process.

Palantir's guide to open-ended questions gives the advice that matters most for that round. It says Palantir rarely works on problems with a single optimal solution, tells candidates to name alternatives and trade-offs and still reach a concrete approach, and says to deliver a functioning idea first and expand it afterwards (Palantir (opens in a new tab)).

AI rules differ by stage. Anthropic asks candidates to write the first draft of an application themselves and then use Claude to refine it, to complete take-homes without Claude unless told otherwise, and to use no AI help in live interviews unless told otherwise. It lists using AI to invent experiences as not allowed (Anthropic (opens in a new tab)). Read each employer's rules before each stage. Formats also move. Anthropic's performance-engineering team, which is not an FDE team, redesigned its take-home more than once as newer models beat or matched strong candidates, so last year's interview report may be out of date (Anthropic engineering (opens in a new tab)).

5. A portfolio of deployed work

A portfolio is evidence you can hand to a stranger. The word that matters is deployed: running where a reviewer can open it, used by someone, and judged against something. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Commure and Matta each value real-world experience of building and shipping projects from start to finish. Anthropic's careers page tells applicants to put independent research, a thoughtful blog post or open-source work at the top of the CV. Palantir's open-ended guide suggests building an app for a problem in your own life or doing something interesting with a public dataset. A training provider recommends integration projects, deployment case studies and write-ups with diagrams (FDE Academy (opens in a new tab)); it sells courses, so treat that as one view.

Our own way to judge a project is an evidence ladder. Rung 1 is a tutorial notebook, which shows you can follow instructions. Rung 2 adds a public repository with tests and a README, so others can run your code. Rung 3 is a live deployment with a URL: you can ship and keep something running. Rung 4 adds a real user and their feedback. Rung 5 adds a result measured against a baseline, with a write-up. Aim for rungs 3 to 5 on one project rather than rung 2 on ten. Use public or synthetic data and never publish an employer's or customer's data. Describe only what you did and measured, because the Anthropic guidance that forbids invented experience applies to a portfolio too.

Worked example: Scoring a skills matrix for a data analyst moving toward an FDE role

This is a teaching scenario with an invented person, built so that every number can be reproduced. The weights come from the skills-matrix grid in the explanation; the scores are the invented person's own.

The person. Priya has three years as a data analyst. She writes SQL and pandas notebooks, presents findings to business teams every week, and has called a large language model API once, in a notebook. She has never deployed a service. She wants to apply for FDE roles in six months and can give about 10 hours a week, which is 60 hours.

Step 3: compute the priorities

python
TARGET = 3
HOURS = 60

# name, weight (columns naming it), current level
SKILLS = [
    ("Production code and shipping", 4, 1),
    ("Open-ended problems", 3, 2),
    ("Working inside existing systems", 3, 1),
    ("LLM and agent experience", 2, 1),
    ("Customer discovery and communication", 4, 3),
    ("Learning and ownership", 4, 2),
]


def priorities(skills, target=TARGET):
    """Return new rows of (name, weight, current, gap, priority); the input is not modified."""
    return [(name, w, c, target - c, w * (target - c)) for name, w, c in skills]


def hours_plan(rows, budget=HOURS):
    total = sum(row[4] for row in rows)
    if total == 0:
        return {row[0]: 0.0 for row in rows}
    return {row[0]: budget * row[4] / total for row in rows}


rows = priorities(SKILLS)
plan = hours_plan(rows)
print(f"{'skill':<38}{'w':>2}{'c':>3}{'gap':>4}{'priority':>9}{'hours':>7}")
for name, w, c, gap, pr in sorted(rows, key=lambda r: -r[4]):
    print(f"{name:<38}{w:>2}{c:>3}{gap:>4}{pr:>9}{plan[name]:>7.1f}")
print("total priority:", sum(r[4] for r in rows), " total hours:", round(sum(plan.values()), 1))

Step 4: read the result

Production code and shipping takes 8 of the 25 priority points, so it gets 8/25 × 60 = 19.2 hours. Customer discovery and communication is named by all four sources yet scores 0, because Priya is already at the target. She should spend no practice time there and should use it as the strength she leads with in interviews.

Step 5: turn the hours into one project

One project can use all five budgets. Priya plans a small service that answers questions about a public dataset through a language model, with a fixed list of 20 test questions. About 19 hours go to building and deploying the service with tests, 14 to integrating a public data API, 10 to the language model call and the 20-question check, 10 to showing it to one real user and finishing a write-up, and 7 to writing down how she scoped the problem and which alternatives she rejected. That targets rung 4 or 5 of the evidence ladder.

What the result means. The output is a plan, not a verdict. The weights come from four sources, the levels are self-reported, and 60 hours will not guarantee an offer. Re-score after six weeks with the same rule, that a level of 2 or 3 needs an artefact, and add a new employer's posting as a fifth column so the weights do not go stale.

Edge cases and limitations

The title promises more than the job delivers
Employers define FDE differently. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that some employers stress helping close sales, others helping customers succeed and others contributing to the core product, and Sierra's Natalie Meurer says the skill sets under the label have become so broad that it can almost seem nonsensical. Sierra itself chose the title agent engineer. Before applying, read the responsibilities: whether you would write production code, how much of the week is on site, and who owns the result. A posting with no engineering in it is a different job.
Seniority bars make the entry-level route narrow
Anthropic's postings ask for 4 or more years in a technical, customer-facing role, and Ramp prefers 5 or more for senior FDE roles but hires some exceptional new graduates. Palantir has a new-graduate FDSE posting and, per the Pragmatic Engineer, hires people with as little as a year of experience. Students should target employers with early-career postings and otherwise take an adjacent role. Check constraints that rule you out whatever your skill, such as the active clearance in Palantir's US government posting or travel (an estimated 25% in the US copy of Anthropic's posting, 25 to 50% in its London posting, 25 to 50% preferred in Palantir's).
You cannot publish the work you are proudest of
Work done for an employer or a customer is usually confidential, and a screenshot of it can breach an agreement. Rebuild the problem on public or synthetic data, describe the method and the measured result without the data, and ask permission before naming anyone. Palantir's own advice for building evidence includes using a public dataset. Never pad the gap with invented projects: Anthropic's guidance lists using AI to create experiences you have not had as not allowed.
AI help is allowed at some stages and banned at others
Anthropic asks for a self-written first draft that Claude may refine, no Claude on take-homes unless the instructions say so, and no AI help in live interviews unless told otherwise. Other employers may differ and are not obliged to say so loudly, so read the brief and ask the recruiter. In a portfolio, say which parts were AI-assisted and be ready to explain every line, because a follow-up question will test it.
Interview formats change faster than interview reports
Anthropic's performance-engineering team described redesigning its take-home more than once as newer models beat or matched strong candidates. Preparation guides collect candidate reports and give figures such as stage lengths and timelines, mostly without sources. Prefer the employer's own pages, treat a guide as a checklist of things to practise, and confirm the actual loop with your recruiter.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You enjoy building a working thing end to end and then explaining it to the person who will use it, and you can accept travel to customer sites, which Anthropic's postings put at 25% in one and 25 to 50% in another.
  • You have, or can build in the next six weeks, one deployed and measured project, and you want a specific list of gaps instead of general advice.
  • You work in an adjacent role (software, data, solutions or consulting) and want an evidence-based plan for moving toward customer-facing engineering.

Think twice when

  • You want to go deep on one codebase or one research area with little customer contact. A product engineering role is a better fit.
  • A constraint rules you out, such as required travel, a security clearance or a location, and you cannot change it.
  • The posting's responsibilities are mostly pre-sales demos or ticket support with no production code you would own.

A simpler alternative. Take a software, data or solutions engineer role at a company that already has an FDE team, and ask to join customer calls and one deployment. Salesforce describes a technical architect who moved from that role at Salesforce into its FDE team, and the Pragmatic Engineer notes that many Palantir FDEs moved into product engineering after 2016. This stops being enough when you need the customer-facing record quickly, or when your employer has no customer-facing engineering work to join.

Key takeaways

  • FDE hiring tests two things a standard software loop tends to under-test, which are code that works inside someone else's environment and a record of deciding what to build when the problem was vague.
  • Employers' own pages converge on production coding, comfort with ambiguity and inherited systems, communication with non-engineers and ownership, while large language model experience is explicit mainly at AI product companies.
  • Each entry route leaves a different gap, so score yourself against sourced requirements, require an artefact for any level above 1, and spend time where weight times gap is largest.
  • A typical loop combines a phone or video screen, live coding, an open-ended problem round and behavioural questions, with take-homes and customer role-plays at some employers, and each employer sets its own rules on AI use.
  • A portfolio persuades when it is deployed and legible, with a live URL, a user, a result measured against a baseline and an honest short write-up, not a pile of notebooks.
  • Titles and formats vary and change, so read each posting's responsibilities and treat interview reports as checklists rather than guarantees.

Example 1 of 5

Palantir's competency-based interview guides for engineers, including FDSEs

PalantirDocumented in official docs

Palantir's careers site has a Getting Hired page and guides to the competencies it interviews for: the phone interview, writing good code, analysing the efficiency of code, navigating open-ended questions, solving technical problems and working inside existing systems. The writing-good-code guide names Forward Deployed Software Engineers as one of its audiences. [1][2][3][4][5]

The problem

Palantir puts small teams on unfamiliar customer problems with little supervision. A CV shows which languages someone has used. It does not show how they behave when a problem has no single answer, when the code is someone else's, or when the interviewer is the only person they can ask. Palantir therefore describes the competencies it values and how to practise them, so that candidates can show them.

Where this topic fits

The guides sit between the application and the interviews. Everyone starts with one or two phone interviews, and candidates who fit are invited to an on-site round, in person or virtual. The competency guides tell a candidate what the questions are probing, so that preparation can match.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Expect one or two phone interviews of roughly 20 to 45 minutes that cover your background and interest in Palantir and, for engineers, include a coding question.
  2. Prepare by competency instead of by trick. Write real code, think about edge cases and how to test them, check the efficiency of what you wrote, and know what each library call you use costs.
  3. For open-ended questions, practise naming two or three alternatives with their trade-offs, then committing to one concrete approach and delivering a working idea before expanding it.
  4. Build evidence for that competency before the interview, such as an app for a problem in your own life, a larger project with friends, or something interesting with a public dataset.
  5. Practise the existing-systems competency on someone else's code. Pick an open-source project, choose a small bug, and note how you found the right place to change it and which assumptions you tested.
  6. For the on-site stage, prepare stories about execution, challenge, motivating others, judgment and failure, and ask clarifying questions when a problem is under-specified.

Why this approach fits

The skills that matter most in the field, such as handling ambiguity and working in code you did not write, are the hardest to see on a CV. Publishing the competencies lets candidates practise them in the shape in which they will be tested, which is fairer than guessing. It also shows that Palantir weighs engineering fundamentals and judgment together. This is our explanation, not a Palantir statement.

Limitation or tradeoff

The pages describe competencies, not question banks, pass criteria or timelines, and Palantir says the number and format of interviews are personalised to the candidate's skills and the role. Parts of the guides read as aimed at students and engineers in general rather than at FDSE candidates in particular, and none of the pages we read describes a customer role-play stage. Anything beyond what is on the pages, including how rounds are named in preparation guides, should be treated as unconfirmed.

What the sources document

  • Palantir says its interview process is personalised to the candidate's skills and role, that everyone starts with one or two phone interviews, and that candidates who fit are brought on site for in-person or virtual interviews. It lists six competency guides, from the phone interview to working inside existing systems. [1]
  • The phone interview guide says phone interviews typically last 20 to 45 minutes, cover your background, experiences and interest in Palantir, and include a coding question for every engineering candidate whatever their specialty. [2]
  • The writing-good-code guide says Palantir will not ask about obscure language features, says the best preparation is to write code, and names a Forward Deployed Software Engineer prototyping an extension with a deadline next week as someone who needs to know what quality code looks like. [3]
  • The open-ended guide says Palantir rarely works on problems with a single optimal solution, advises naming alternatives and trade-offs while still reaching a concrete approach, and suggests building real things such as an app for a personal problem or a project on a public dataset. [4]
  • The existing-systems guide says developers spend far more time debugging, modifying and extending existing code than writing new code, and suggests practising by finding and fixing a bug in an open-source project. [5]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

Ramp's four hiring axes for forward deployed engineers

RampDocumented in an engineering article

Ramp Builders, Ramp's engineering blog, published Forward Deployed Engineering on 5 August 2025, with a section titled How we hire FDEs. The Pragmatic Engineer's article of 12 August 2025, written with input from Ramp's head of FDE, adds Ramp's view on seniority. [7][8]

The problem

Ramp's FDEs work with customers from the moment they are prospects to implementation, rollout and long-tail support. An interview that scored only algorithms would miss the traits that decide whether an FDE gets a customer live and keeps them. Ramp therefore says which qualities it evaluates beyond coding.

Where this topic fits

The four axes are what Ramp says its hiring process evaluates. The post does not set out the stages of the process or which round examines which axis, so the steps below describe how a candidate can prepare evidence for each axis, not how Ramp scores it.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Read the role as the post describes it. FDEs work with customers across their whole lifecycle, so the evidence you offer should span building and contact with customers.
  2. Gather evidence of drive, which is the first axis the post lists. Choose occasions where you found out what was needed and did what it took to finish.
  3. Gather evidence of engineering fundamentals that a coding exercise could confirm. Ramp says it validates this without over-indexing on perfection.
  4. Gather evidence of customer empathy, such as a time you understood how a user felt and changed how you worked with them.
  5. Gather evidence of communication, such as a time you listened first and then explained a technical decision clearly to a non-technical person.
  6. Note the team's makeup. When the post was written, 7 of Ramp's 16 FDEs were previous founders. The post does not make founding a requirement; our reading is that owning an outcome end to end is familiar on the team, so evidence of it should land well.

Why this approach fits

A job that runs from sales conversations to long-tail support rewards persistence, listening and clear explanation as well as technical skill, and the four axes reflect that. Former founders have practised owning an outcome end to end, which matches the first axis. This is our analysis.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post names the axes but gives no weights, no pass bar and no stage structure, and it reports the founder count as a snapshot of 16 people, not a hiring rule. It is one fintech's model, in which, according to a Ramp job advert quoted by the Pragmatic Engineer, FDEs also work with sales and embed in core product teams. It describes Ramp's intent and does not report outcomes.

What the sources document

  • Ramp says its FDEs work with customers through their entire lifecycle, from prospects in the sales funnel to implementation and rollout to long-tail support. [7]
  • The post names four things it evaluates in FDE candidates, which are drive and work ethic, engineering fundamentals, customer empathy and communication. [7]
  • On fundamentals, Ramp says it validates coding competency and core technical skills but avoids over-indexing on perfection, noting that AI tooling makes technical problems easier to learn and execute. [7]
  • The post says that of the 16 FDEs on Ramp's team, 7 were previous founders. [7]
  • The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Ramp prefers 5 or more years of experience for Senior FDE roles but does hire some exceptional new graduates as FDEs. [8]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 3 of 5

Anthropic's FDE posting and its rules for AI use in hiring

AnthropicDocumented in official docs

Anthropic's Applied AI team lists Forward Deployed Engineer roles. Two postings were read: a US copy on a venture investor's job board (posted August 2026, since deactivated) and the London posting on Anthropic's own Greenhouse job board (first published 14 September 2026). Anthropic also publishes a careers page that describes interviews and a guidance page on how candidates may use AI tools when applying and interviewing. [9][10][11][12]

The problem

Where this topic fits

  2. Map each requirement to an artefact you can show, including Python and shipped production applications, production experience with language models including agents and evaluation, and customer-discovery communication.
  3. Treat the plus points (an enterprise vertical such as financial services or healthcare, enterprise IT knowledge and, in the US copy, prior FDE or professional-services experience) as tie-breakers, because the postings encourage applying without meeting every qualification.
  4. Write the first draft of the application yourself, then use an AI assistant to refine the wording, and do not use it to create experience you do not have.
  5. Prepare for video interviews with live coding tools such as Colab and CodeSignal, where you may look things up, and expect no AI help in live interviews or take-homes unless the instructions say otherwise.
  6. Put independent research, a thoughtful blog post or open-source work at the top of the CV, as the careers page advises.

Why this approach fits

The posting is concrete enough to turn into a checklist, and the AI guidance is explicit enough to plan around: draft first, refine with AI, and prepare to think unaided in live sessions. Together they show what an AI lab asks of the experienced route. This is our analysis.

Limitation or tradeoff

4Common mistakes

  1. Preparing only for algorithm puzzles and ignoring the open-ended and customer rounds

    Why it causes problems
    Palantir's competency guides cover open-ended questions, problem solving and working in existing systems alongside code, and preparation guides report design, case-study and customer role-play rounds. A candidate who has drilled only data structures can pass a coding screen and then stall when asked to scope a vague customer problem.
    How to recognise it
    Your practice log is all coding problems with known answers. You have never practised asking clarifying questions aloud or stating trade-offs to another person.
    How to fix it
    Split practice time using the gap priority. Add weekly sessions in which you take one vague prompt, ask clarifying questions, name two alternatives and commit to a first slice, ideally with a friend playing the customer.

  2. Showing notebooks and tutorials instead of deployed work

    Why it causes problems
    A notebook does not show that you can ship. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Commure and Matta value projects built and shipped from start to finish, and Baseten's FDE describes a world of difference between a weekend project and a service that scales.
    How to recognise it
    None of your projects has a URL that works today, a user other than you, or a number measured against a baseline.
    How to fix it
    Pick one project and climb the evidence ladder to rung 3, 4 or 5. Deploy it, show it to a real user, measure it against the old way of doing the task and write the one-page case study.

  3. Claiming experience or results you cannot back up, or letting an AI assistant invent them

    Why it causes problems
    Anthropic lists using AI to create experiences as not allowed, and a live interview or a follow-up on a CV line tests depth quickly. A claim you cannot explain costs more than a smaller claim that is true.
    How to recognise it
    You cannot explain the design decisions behind a CV bullet without reading it, or its number has no file, log or date behind it.
    How to fix it
    Write each claim as system, action and result, keep the evidence (repository, log, message) beside it, and note which parts were AI-assisted.

  4. Treating every FDE posting as the same job

    Why it causes problems
    The Pragmatic Engineer reports that employers stress closing sales, helping customers or core product work, and Sierra's Natalie Meurer says the skill sets under the label have become very broad. You can prepare for the wrong job or accept a role with no engineering in it.
    How to recognise it
    You cannot say from the posting who owns the production code, how much travel to expect, or whether the role feeds back into the product.
    How to fix it
    Make a short grid for each posting covering code ownership, travel, customer contact, product feedback and language model requirements. Ask the recruiter about the blanks before the first interview.

  5. Staying silent, or hunting for a perfect answer, in an open-ended round

    Why it causes problems
    Palantir tells candidates to think aloud, ask questions, name alternatives and deliver a working idea first. Silence hides your reasoning, and a perfect answer that arrives late leaves the interviewer nothing to assess.
    How to recognise it
    In mock rounds you think silently for ten minutes, never ask a question, or finish with a design too large to explain.
    How to fix it
    Use a fixed opening. Restate the problem, ask five to eight questions, state two alternatives with trade-offs, pick a first slice that could run next week and say how you would know it works.

Exercise 2

Deploy a small data service, measure it and write the case study

Hands-onIntermediateAbout 3 h (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Build a portfolio piece that reaches rung 4 or 5 of the evidence ladder, using free tools only.

  1. Choose a small public dataset you are allowed to reuse, such as a list of public holidays, bus stops or open data from your city. Write a Python program that answers one kind of question about it as JSON over HTTP.
  2. Write a check script with at least five cases (two successes, an unknown key, a missing parameter and an unknown path) that prints a pass rate and exits non-zero if any case fails.
  3. Deploy the service to any free host that gives a public URL. Hugging Face Spaces is one option; follow your host's documentation for the port and start command. Run the check script against the public URL.
  4. Ask one real person to try three questions and record any question the service could not answer.
  5. Write a one-page case study using the eight-part outline in the Baseten example.

Requirements

  • The service uses data you may reuse, names the data source and its licence in the README, and returns JSON with sensible status codes.
  • The check script runs against a local address and the public URL, prints the pass rate, and returns a non-zero exit code on any failure.
  • The public URL works when opened by someone else on a different network.
  • The case study states at least one thing the service cannot do, one number you measured with its date, and which parts were AI-assisted.

Expected behaviour

Running the check script against the service prints one PASS or FAIL line per case and a summary such as 5/5 checks passed (100%). The public URL answers the same requests as the local one. The case study is one page and a reviewer who opens only the README and the case study can tell what the service does, who tried it, what was measured and what failed.

Hint 1

Start with the standard library `http.server`, so there is nothing to install. Move to a framework only when you need one.

Hint 2

If a local connection hangs, use `127.0.0.1` instead of `localhost`. Some machines resolve `localhost` to an IPv6 address on which a different program may be listening.

Hint 3

Many free hosts provide the port in a `PORT` environment variable. The starter code reads it and falls back to 8000 locally; set `PORT` if 8000 is busy. Docker Spaces on Hugging Face instead expose port 7860 unless `app_port` in the Space's README says otherwise, so match the two.

Show solution

A minimal service, using only the standard library. Replace the small table with rows from your dataset.

python
import json
import os
from http.server import BaseHTTPRequestHandler, HTTPServer
from urllib.parse import parse_qs, urlparse

HOLIDAYS = {
    "2026-01-01": "New Year's Day",
    "2026-12-25": "Christmas Day",
    "2026-12-26": "Boxing Day",
}


class Handler(BaseHTTPRequestHandler):
    def _send(self, status, body):
        payload = json.dumps(body).encode("utf-8")
        self.send_response(status)
        self.send_header("Content-Type", "application/json")
        self.send_header("Content-Length", str(len(payload)))
        self.end_headers()
        self.wfile.write(payload)

    def do_GET(self):
        url = urlparse(self.path)
        if url.path != "/holiday":
            return self._send(404, {"error": "unknown path"})
        date = parse_qs(url.query).get("date", [""])[0]
        if not date:
            return self._send(400, {"error": "missing date parameter"})
        name = HOLIDAYS.get(date)
        if name is None:
            return self._send(404, {"error": f"no holiday on {date}"})
        return self._send(200, {"date": date, "holiday": name})

    def log_message(self, *args):  # keep the console quiet
        pass


if __name__ == "__main__":
    port = int(os.environ.get("PORT", "8000"))
    HTTPServer(("0.0.0.0", port), Handler).serve_forever()

import json
import sys
from urllib.error import HTTPError
from urllib.request import urlopen

BASE = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "http://127.0.0.1:8000"
CASES = [
    ("/holiday?date=2026-12-25", 200, "Christmas Day"),
    ("/holiday?date=2026-01-01", 200, "New Year's Day"),
    ("/holiday?date=2026-03-03", 404, None),
    ("/holiday", 400, None),
    ("/nothing", 404, None),
]


def fetch(path):
    try:
        with urlopen(BASE + path, timeout=5) as response:
            return response.status, json.load(response)
    except HTTPError as error:
        return error.code, json.load(error)


passed = 0
for path, want_status, want_name in CASES:
    status, body = fetch(path)
    ok = status == want_status and body.get("holiday") == want_name
    passed += ok
    print(("PASS" if ok else "FAIL"), path, status)
print(f"{passed}/{len(CASES)} checks passed ({100 * passed / len(CASES):.0f}%)")
sys.exit(0 if passed == len(CASES) else 1)

Exercise 3

Run a 20-minute mock open-ended round

DesignBeginnerAbout 30 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Practise the round that Palantir's guide describes, alone or with a friend who plays the customer. If you are alone, write the customer's answers yourself as you go.

The invented prompt: a regional hospital's bed manager says patients wait too long for a bed after the emergency department decides to admit them, and she wants AI to fix it. You have 20 minutes.

Produce the following, in this order: your clarifying questions, two alternative approaches, the one first slice you would build, and a measurable success check.

Requirements

  • Write at least eight clarifying questions, with at least three about how the work is done today and at least two about data and systems.
  • Name two alternative approaches, each with one advantage and one risk.
  • Commit to one first slice that could run within two weeks and say what it will not do.
  • State one measurable success check and the baseline you would collect first.

Expected behaviour

A strong answer separates the stated request (AI) from the underlying problem (delay between steps), asks about the current process before proposing anything, picks a small slice that fits real data and permissions, and states a baseline such as the median minutes from the admit decision to a bed being assigned. It ends with a working idea first and expansions second, as Palantir's guide advises.

Hint 1

Ask about the last specific delay the manager can remember, not about what she wants.

Hint 2

Write the first slice so that one person could check it by hand within a week.

Hint 3

A success check without a baseline cannot be met or missed, so name how you would measure today's numbers.

Show solution

This is a sample answer, not the only good one.

Clarifying questions. What happened in the last case that took too long, hour by hour? Who decides a bed is ready, and who tells whom? Where is bed status recorded today, and how often is it updated? Who can approve read access to it, and how long does approval take? Do timestamps exist for the admit decision and for bed assignment? Which wards matter most? What would a good day look like in minutes? What must never happen, such as showing patient names on a shared screen?

Alternatives. (A) A read-only board that joins existing bed-status and admission timestamps and shows where each waiting patient is in the pipeline. Advantage: it uses data that already exists and can be checked by hand. Risk: it shows delays without removing them. (B) A model that forecasts bed demand. Advantage: it may help plan staffing. Risk: it needs months of history and trust before anyone acts on it.

First slice. Alternative A for one ward, read-only, refreshed every few minutes, with no patient identifiers on screen. It will not predict anything, write to any system or cover other wards.

Success check. Median minutes from admit decision to bed assignment on that ward. Collect two weeks of existing timestamps first as the baseline, then compare two weeks with the board running, and report both numbers and the sample sizes.

The sample follows the order in Palantir's advice: name alternatives and trade-offs, then settle on a concrete approach that works before expanding it.
