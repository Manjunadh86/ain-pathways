Natural Language Processing Basics

Natural language processing (NLP) is the set of methods that let programs work with human language: split text into tokens, turn the tokens into numbers, and use the numbers to classify, search, label or generate text. Classic pipelines count words; modern ones start from a language model pre-trained on large amounts of text.

1Overview

Why it matters

Search boxes, spam and abuse filters, ticket routing, review analysis, clinical-note mining and chat assistants all begin the same way: a string arrives and a program must decide what its parts are and what they mean. How the string is cut into tokens, which differences are erased and how the result becomes numbers decide what a model can ever learn from it. A preprocessing step that deletes the word "not" or merges two different words cannot be repaired by a better classifier further down the line.

Most failures sit in the plumbing rather than in the model. The vocabulary is learned on the test set, the serving system tokenises differently from the training job, a stop-word list removes a negation, or a large pre-trained model is bought for a problem that a TF-IDF baseline already solves. Engineers who add search or classification to a product, data scientists who must justify a model choice, and anyone reading an NLP vendor's documentation need this vocabulary, because the same tokens, count matrices and task shapes appear in all of them.

  • Recognise the preprocessing mistakes that silently hurt a model, such as leaking the vocabulary, mismatched pipelines and deleted negations.

Key terms

Token
A unit of text that a program treats as one piece, usually a word, a piece of a word or a punctuation mark, produced by splitting the string.
Vocabulary
The set of distinct terms a model knows. Words outside it are dropped or mapped to a special unknown token.
Stop word
A very common word such as "the" or "is" that some pipelines remove before counting because it says little about a document's topic.
Stemming
Chopping word endings by rules so that variants share one form, for example "ponies" to "poni". The result need not be a real word.
Lemmatization
Mapping a word to its dictionary form with vocabulary and grammar, for example "am", "are" and "is" to "be".
Bag of words
A representation that counts how often each vocabulary term occurs in a document and ignores the order of the words.
TF-IDF
A weight equal to a term's frequency in a document times an inverse document frequency, so that terms found in few documents score higher than terms found in most.
n-gram
A run of n consecutive tokens, such as the bigram "not good". Adding n-grams lets a bag of words keep some word order.
Subword token
A piece of a word, such as "annoy" + "ing" + "ly", taken from a learned vocabulary so that rare words can be spelled from known pieces.
Fine-tuning
Continuing to train a pre-trained model on a small labelled dataset for one task, usually with a small new output layer on top.

2Explanation

Introduction

In the naive Bayes lesson you classified a message by counting the words in it, and in the embeddings lesson you met vectors that place similar items close together. Both lessons began after a step that this lesson makes explicit. A message arrives as a string of characters, and a program has to cut it into pieces, decide which differences between pieces matter, and turn the result into numbers. That work, and the tasks built on top of it, is natural language processing (NLP).

Think of a library card catalogue. The librarian does not file a book under every spelling of every word in it. The librarian decides what counts as one entry, so that "Colour" and "color" meet, and which words are so common that they earn no entry at all. Classic NLP does the same job for a computer in a handful of small steps: clean the text, split it into tokens, normalise the tokens, count them, weight the counts, and hand the numbers to a model.

Every step discards something. Lowercasing erases the difference between Apple the company and apple the fruit, stop-word removal can erase "not", and stemming can merge words that should stay apart. The skill is knowing which losses are cheap for the task in front of you.

Since late 2018 the pipeline has changed. Models such as BERT are first trained on huge amounts of unlabelled text and then adapted to a task with a small labelled set, which replaced most hand-built features. This lesson covers the classic pipeline first, because it is still the baseline that every newer approach has to beat. It then lists the standard tasks and ends with what the pre-trained route changed and what it left alone. The mechanism of those models belongs to attention and transformers and to transfer learning and fine-tuning.

How it works

Step 1: from characters to tokens

A tokenizer cuts a string into tokens. The Stanford information retrieval textbook (opens in a new tab) separates three words that are easy to blur: a token is one occurrence of a character sequence in a document, a type is the class of all tokens with the same characters, and a term is a type, perhaps normalised, that the system keeps in its dictionary. Splitting on white space is rarely enough. The same page lists the awkward cases: apostrophes (O'Neill, aren't), hyphens (Hewlett-Packard), strings such as C++, German compounds written without spaces, and Chinese, which has no spaces between words at all.

Libraries therefore ship a default rule. scikit-learn's CountVectorizer (opens in a new tab) extracts words of at least two letters and splits we've into we and ve, which matters as soon as you supply a stop-word list.

Step 2: normalisation

Normalisation makes different spellings of the same thing meet. The cheapest step is case folding (lowercasing). Three more steps are optional, and each one deletes information.

Stop-word removal drops very common words. The textbook's section on stop words (opens in a new tab) describes a move from lists of 200 to 300 terms, to lists of 7 to 12, to no list, because compression and idf weighting already cope with common words, and because phrases such as "President of the United States" or "flights to London" lose meaning when the small words go. scikit-learn's documentation warns that its English list has known problems and can contain informative words, and the list itself (opens in a new tab) includes not, no, nor, never, cannot and nothing, so removing it can reverse a negation.

Stemming chops endings by rules so that variants share a form. Lemmatization looks words up and uses grammar to return the base form, the lemma: "am, are, is" become "be" (textbook (opens in a new tab)). The Porter algorithm, the usual English stemmer, applies five phases of suffix rules, for example sses to ss and ies to i, which is why "ponies" becomes "poni". The NLTK book (opens in a new tab) runs a stemmer and a lemmatizer over one sentence, and the differences are instructive:

The textbook reports that either kind of normalisation tends not to improve English retrieval in aggregate, and that stemming raises recall while harming precision. The NLTK book suggests the Porter stemmer when you index texts and want search to accept other forms of a word, and the WordNet lemmatizer when you want a list of valid base words; the lemmatizer is slower because it strips an ending only if the result is in its dictionary.

Step 3: bag of words and n-grams

After normalisation, a document is a list of terms. A bag of words keeps only how many times each term occurs. The result is a matrix with one row per document and one column per vocabulary term, where the cell for document ddd and term ttt is the count of ttt in ddd (scikit-learn (opens in a new tab)). Order is gone, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get identical rows. Adding n-grams, runs of nnn consecutive tokens such as the bigram not good, restores short phrases at the price of a much larger vocabulary. Nearly all cells are zero, so libraries store the matrix in a sparse format.

Step 4: TF-IDF and cosine similarity

Raw counts overweight words that are common everywhere. TF-IDF multiplies a term's frequency in a document by an inverse document frequency (idf) that shrinks as more documents contain the term. With nnn documents and df(t)\text{df}(t)df(t) of them containing term ttt, scikit-learn's default is

followed by dividing each document vector by its Euclidean length (L2 normalisation). The 1s inside the logarithm behave as if one extra document containing every term had been seen, so the formula never divides by zero, and the final +1 keeps a term found in every document from getting weight 0. Lucene's ClassicSimilarity (opens in a new tab) defines idf by the same expression, with tf as the square root of the count and a length norm of 1/length1/\sqrt{\text{length}}1/length​. The textbook (opens in a new tab) states the intended effect: the weight is highest when a term occurs many times in a small number of documents, and lowest when it occurs in nearly all of them.

Step 5: the classic tasks

Hugging Face's course (opens in a new tab) groups NLP work into classifying whole sentences (spam, review sentiment), classifying each word (parts of speech, named entities), generating text, extracting an answer from a text, and generating a new sentence from an input such as a translation or summary. Several have a cheap count-based starting point and a pre-trained counterpart. Retrieval is added because search is the oldest application:

Translation and summarisation produce new text and need the sequence models of later lessons.

Step 6: what pre-trained language models changed

In the classic pipeline you build every stage and train a new model from scratch for each task, so quality is limited by your labelled examples and by the features you thought of. The pre-trained route splits the work. Someone trains a language model once on very large amounts of unlabelled text. You then add a small output layer and fine-tune on your own labelled examples. The BERT paper (opens in a new tab) describes this recipe and reports state-of-the-art results on eleven NLP tasks, among them a GLUE score of 80.5% and a SQuAD v1.1 test F1 of 93.2.

Tokenization changes too. Word-level vocabularies grow huge because "love", "loving" and "loved" are separate entries, and unseen words become an unknown token. Character-level tokens have no unknown words but make sequences very long. Hugging Face's tokenizer summary (opens in a new tab) describes the middle path, subword tokenization: frequent words stay whole and rare ones split into pieces from a learned vocabulary, so "annoyingly" may become annoying + ly. The tokenizer is part of the model, because ids mean something only under the vocabulary the model was trained with. Since such a model was pre-trained on ordinary text that still contained stop words and inflections, you normally give it text in that form rather than stemmed or stop-listed text.

What does not change is the discipline around the model: a clear task definition, clean labels, a fair train/test split, a metric (see classification metrics) and a baseline. The count-based pipeline is that baseline, and it takes a few lines:

python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

model = make_pipeline(
    TfidfVectorizer(lowercase=True, ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2),
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
)
model.fit(train_texts, train_labels)       # vocabulary and idf come from training texts only
predictions = model.predict(test_texts)    # the fitted vectorizer is reused on new text

Worked example: Ranking four support messages for the query "battery drain" with TF-IDF and cosine similarity

Take four short support messages (invented for this lesson) and the query "battery drain". Which message answers the query best, and how do the preprocessing choices change the answer?

Rules. Lowercase the text. Take the matches of [a-z]+ as tokens. Remove the stop words the, is, and and all. Map four inflected words to their lemma with a lookup table (drains to drain, lasts to last, cracked to crack, swelled to swell); the table stands in for a real lemmatizer such as WordNet's or spaCy's. Weights follow scikit-learn's defaults: smoothed idf, then L2 normalisation.

Here is the whole computation as code (standard library only), followed by what it prints:

python
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

DOCS = [
    "The battery drains fast.",
    "Battery life is great, the battery lasts all day.",
    "The screen cracked and the battery swelled.",
    "Great screen, great price.",
]
STOP_WORDS = {"the", "is", "and", "all"}
LEMMAS = {"drains": "drain", "lasts": "last", "cracked": "crack", "swelled": "swell"}


def preprocess(text):
    tokens = re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())
    return [LEMMAS.get(t, t) for t in tokens if t not in STOP_WORDS]


def unit(weights):
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(w * w for w in weights.values()))
    return {term: w / norm for term, w in weights.items()}


docs = [Counter(preprocess(d)) for d in DOCS]
n = len(docs)
df = Counter(term for doc in docs for term in doc)
idf = {term: math.log((1 + n) / (1 + df[term])) + 1 for term in df}

doc_vectors = [unit({t: c * idf[t] for t, c in doc.items()}) for doc in docs]
query = Counter(t for t in preprocess("battery drain") if t in idf)
query_vector = unit({t: c * idf[t] for t, c in query.items()})

for i, vec in enumerate(doc_vectors, start=1):
    cosine = sum(w * vec.get(t, 0.0) for t, w in query_vector.items())
    print(f"d{i}  cosine = {cosine:.4f}")

The same arithmetic reproduces the matrix that scikit-learn's documentation prints for its default settings, for example the first row 0.85151335, 0, 0.52433293 for the counts [3, 0, 1].

What the result means. The textbook's overlap score (opens in a new tab), which adds up how often each query word occurs in a document, gives d1 and d2 the same 2, because "battery" appears twice in d2. TF-IDF breaks the tie correctly: "battery" occurs in three of four messages and carries little weight, while "drain" is specific to d1.

Skip the lemma step and the answer flips. With LEMMAS = {} the documents contain drains but the query contains drain, a word the vocabulary has never seen, so the query keeps only "battery". The cosines become d1 0.4114, d2 0.5571, d3 0.3667, d4 0.0000, and d2, a message about battery life that never mentions draining, now ranks above d1, the message about a draining battery. Normalisation is not decoration: it decides which words can meet. The same count matrix could also feed multinomial naive Bayes instead of a cosine ranking.

Edge cases and limitations

Stop-word lists can delete negations and meaning-bearing phrases
scikit-learn's built-in English list, taken from the Glasgow Information Retrieval Group, contains not, no, nor, never, cannot and nothing. Remove them and "not good" becomes "good", so a sentiment model sees the opposite of what was written. The IR textbook also shows phrases such as "flights to London" and "President of the United States" that lose meaning when small words are stopped out, and scikit-learn warns that its English list has known problems. Print the list you use, delete the negations from it, or run without a list and let idf down-weight common words.
A bag of words cannot see word order
"Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" have identical counts. Bigrams recover short phrases such as "not good" at the cost of a vocabulary several times larger, so pair them with a minimum document frequency that drops rare pairs. Dependencies across a whole sentence ("the battery, which I replaced twice, still drains") need models that read the sequence, which is what the pre-trained route provides.
Stemmers produce non-words and still miss variants
The Porter stemmer turns "strange" into "strang" and "quickly" into "quickli", which is harmless for matching but unusable for display, and it leaves "women" untouched, so "woman" and "women" stay separate. A lemmatizer repairs those cases only when the word is in its dictionary and it knows the part of speech. The IR textbook reports no improvement in aggregate for English retrieval from either, so test the step on your own data before keeping it.
Words missing from the vocabulary are silently ignored
A fitted count vectorizer maps a word it never saw in training to nothing. New product names, typos and inflections that preprocessing failed to merge simply contribute zero, as the query word "drain" did in the worked example when the lemma step was skipped. Track the share of live tokens outside the vocabulary; a rising share signals drift. Subword tokenizers avoid the unknown token for most words but split rare words into many pieces, which uses up context length.
Languages without spaces and compound words defeat simple splitting
Chinese has no spaces between words and German writes compounds as one word, so a regular expression for runs of letters produces the wrong units. The IR textbook describes word segmentation, character k-grams and compound splitters as responses. SentencePiece, described in Hugging Face's tokenizer guide, avoids the problem by treating the input as a raw character stream that includes the space.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You have a few hundred to a few tens of thousands of labelled texts, need predictions on a CPU in milliseconds, and want to read which words drove a decision. Start with TF-IDF and a linear model or naive Bayes.
  • Users search documents by keywords, part numbers or names, and exact terms matter. Use an inverted index with an analyzer and BM25 ranking, as Lucene-based engines do.
  • The label depends on context, word order or paraphrase, or you need entities and answer spans rather than one label per text. Fine-tune a pre-trained model or call a hosted API, and compare it with the TF-IDF baseline on the same held-out set.
  • Free text must become structured fields such as entities, dates or codes at ingestion time. Use an extraction pipeline, with a pre-trained tagger when names are open-ended or a dictionary when the vocabulary is fixed.

Think twice when

  • Do not stem or remove stop words by default when the task depends on negation, names, codes or identifiers, or when the model is a pre-trained transformer that expects text in its normal form.
  • Do not rely on a bag of words when the label hinges on word order or on distant context, such as sarcasm or a negation several words away from what it negates.
  • Do not add a large pre-trained model before measuring a TF-IDF baseline on the same split; without the baseline you cannot say what the extra cost and latency bought.

A simpler alternative. Keyword rules or regular expressions are enough when the categories are defined by a few exact phrases. TF-IDF with logistic regression is the next step when rules start to multiply, and it stops being enough when the baseline's errors are paraphrases, negation and context rather than missing keywords.

Key takeaways

  • NLP starts by turning a string into tokens and then numbers, and every preprocessing choice (case, stop words, stemming, lemmatization) deletes information that the model can never recover.
  • Stemming chops endings by rules and may produce non-words, lemmatization uses a dictionary and grammar to return real base forms, and neither reliably improves English search, so test them on your own data.
  • A bag of words counts terms and ignores order, TF-IDF multiplies each count by an inverse document frequency so that terms found in few documents outweigh common ones, and cosine similarity compares the resulting vectors.
  • Classic tasks such as classification, tagging, entity recognition and retrieval differ in what goes in and what comes out, and each has a cheap count-based baseline.
  • Pre-trained language models replaced most hand-built preprocessing and features by using subword tokenizers and a model trained on unlabelled text, but they still need clean labels, a fair test set and a baseline to beat.

Elasticsearch and Lucene: analyzers and TF-IDF to BM25 scoring for keyword search

Elastic and Apache LuceneDocumented in official docs

Elasticsearch's text-analysis documentation describes how text is split into tokens and normalised before it is indexed. The Apache Lucene API documentation and an Elastic article on BM25 describe how matching documents are scored with classic TF-IDF and with its successor BM25. [11][12][14][15][13]

The problem

A search engine must match a query such as "foxes jumping" to documents that say "fox jumps", and then rank thousands of matches so that the most relevant come first. Raw strings cannot be compared that way. The engine needs one consistent set of terms for documents and queries, and a score that rewards rare query terms without letting long documents or repeated words dominate.

Where this topic fits

The analyzer is the preprocessing stage. It runs on each text field when a document is indexed, and a typical setup applies the same analysis to the query text so that the terms line up. The terms go into an inverted index. The similarity function, classic TF-IDF or BM25, is the weighting stage that turns term counts, document frequencies and field length into a score for each candidate document.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. At index time, optional character filters clean the character stream, exactly one tokenizer splits it into tokens and records their positions and offsets, and token filters then lowercase, remove stop words or stem the tokens.
  2. The surviving terms are stored in an inverted index that records, for each term, the documents that contain it. The number of documents per term is the document frequency used later in the idf.
  3. At query time the query text passes through an analyzer, so that "foxes jumping" becomes terms such as fox and jump that can match the indexed terms.
  4. Classic Lucene scoring uses tf equal to the square root of the term count, idf equal to log((docCount+1)/(docFreq+1)) + 1, and a length norm of 1/sqrt(length), so rare terms in short fields score highest.
  5. BM25 replaces the square-root tf with a curve that saturates, controlled by k1, and normalises for field length with b. Its idf is log(1 + (docCount - docFreq + 0.5)/(docFreq + 0.5)), which shrinks towards 0 for a term that occurs in every document.
  6. The engine sorts candidates by score. Because the analyzer decides which terms exist, changing the stemmer or stop list normally means indexing the documents again.

Why this approach fits

Keyword search is the case where exact-term matching at scale is wanted. An analyzer makes spelling variants collapse onto one indexed term, the inverted index finds candidates cheaply, and idf promotes the rare query terms, which are the discriminating ones. BM25 improves on plain TF-IDF in two documented ways: repeated occurrences add less and less to the score, and long fields are penalised.

Limitation or tradeoff

Matching is lexical. A document that says "automobile" is not found for the query "car" unless synonyms are configured, and a stem such as "quickli" is invisible to users but still merges or splits terms. Analysis choices are baked into the index, so changing a stemmer or a stop list normally requires reindexing, and stemming tends to raise recall at some cost to precision.

What the sources document

  • An Elasticsearch analyzer is built from zero or more character filters, exactly one tokenizer and zero or more token filters. The tokenizer also records each term's position and character offsets, and token filters may not change those positions or offsets. [11]
  • In the stemmer token filter's example, the standard tokenizer followed by the stemmer filter turns "the foxes jumping quickly" into the tokens the, fox, jump and quickli, and the English default uses the Porter algorithm. [12]
  • Lucene's ClassicSimilarity implements idf as log((docCount+1)/(docFreq+1)) + 1, tf as sqrt(freq) and length normalisation as 1/sqrt(length); its documentation calls it a historical implementation and suggests considering BM25Similarity instead. [14]

Apache cTAKES: a pipeline that finds diseases, drugs and symptoms in clinical notes

Apache cTAKESDocumented in an open-source project

Apache cTAKES is an open-source NLP platform for clinical text. Its GitHub README, the cTAKES 4.0 Component Use Guide, the guide's pages on LVG and Fast Dictionary Lookup, and the Default Clinical Pipeline page on the project wiki describe the processing stages and what they output. The wiki pages describe cTAKES 4.0, while the README refers to later releases. [16][17][19][20][18]

The problem

Clinical notes are free text full of abbreviations, fragments and negations such as "no chest pain", and the same condition can be written many ways. Researchers and hospitals want structured facts instead: which symptoms, diseases, procedures and medications a patient has, and whether each one is present. A keyword search cannot tell a mention of a disease from a denial of it, or connect two names for one concept.

Where this topic fits

cTAKES is a chain of annotators. Core components split the text into sentences and tokens, a part-of-speech tagger and a chunker mark up its grammar, LVG normalises word forms, a dictionary lookup finds the spans that name clinical concepts and maps them to codes such as UMLS concept identifiers, and later components decide attributes such as negation. It is the classic pipeline of this lesson applied to a specialised vocabulary, with a dictionary doing the entity-finding step that a learned tagger does in other systems.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The core components split the note into sentences and tokens. In the component guide's dependency description, the core output is what the part-of-speech tagger and LVG both consume.
  2. The part-of-speech tagger labels each token and the chunker groups tagged tokens into phrases. According to the guide, the chunker's output is one of the inputs of the dictionary lookup.
  3. LVG wraps the National Library of Medicine's SPECIALIST lexical tools and generates a canonical form and variants of each word, such as singular forms of plurals and capitalisation variants, which the dictionary lookup uses to find terms whose exact form is not in the dictionary.
  4. The dictionary lookup identifies terms in the text and normalises them to codes in an ontology such as a UMLS concept identifier, SNOMED-CT or RxNorm. Fast Dictionary Lookup can use a whole sentence as its lookup window and tries both the text as written and its lexical variants. The default dictionary needs a UMLS licence.
  5. The assertion component then sets attributes on each entity that was found, including negation (polarity), history and subject, so that a denied symptom is not recorded as a present one.
  6. The default clinical pipeline outputs annotations for anatomical sites, signs and symptoms, procedures, diseases and disorders, and medications, with normalised UMLS concept identifiers plus values for negation, uncertainty and subject.

Why this approach fits

A fixed clinical vocabulary makes dictionary lookup precise and auditable, because every match is traceable to a concept code that a research team can review. The tokenization, tagging and variant-handling stages exist to bring messy note text into a form the dictionary can match, and the assertion stage handles the case where plain word matching fails worst, which is negation.

Limitation or tradeoff

A dictionary finds only what it contains, so new drug names, misspellings and unusual abbreviations are missed until the dictionary or normaliser is extended. LVG's variants raise the number of terms found but, as its wiki page warns, can also bring false positives from the dictionary. The default dictionary requires a UMLS licence, and negation or history handling can be wrong on unusual phrasing, so extraction quality has to be measured on a sample of the site's own notes before the output is trusted. The component pages cited describe cTAKES 4.0, so check names and options against the release you install.

The service puts classic NLP tasks behind separate methods: sentiment analysis, entity analysis, entity sentiment analysis, syntactic analysis (sentences, tokens, lemmas, parts of speech and a dependency parse) and content classification.   3. For sentiment, the response holds a score from -1.0 to 1.0 and a magnitude of 0.0 or more for the whole document, and the same two numbers for each sentence.

The problem

Bug reports are free text in which stack-trace file names, URLs, hex numbers and crash identifiers vary endlessly but mean little, and team jargon such as "steps to reproduce" appears in several spellings. Someone has to decide whether a report is a defect or a feature request, whether it is a regression, and which product component should look at it.

Where this topic fits

bugbug's component classifier is the classic pipeline end to end. Regular-expression cleanup replaces noisy strings such as file names and URLs with placeholder tokens and merges variant spellings, a TF-IDF vectorizer processes the report title and comments (with a spaCy lemmatizer as its tokenizer when lemmatization is on), and a gradient-boosted tree classifier makes the decision. Structured fields such as severity and keywords are added as separate features beside the text columns.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Cleanup functions rewrite the bug text before vectorizing. The component model applies three of them: URLs become __URL__, source-file names become __FILE_REFERENCE__, and variant terms such as "safe mode" and "safemode" are mapped to one form.
  2. Each model picks its text vectorizer: scikit-learn's TfidfVectorizer, or, when lemmatization is turned on, a TfidfVectorizer whose tokenizer returns spaCy lemmas.
  3. The component model vectorizes the bug title and the comments separately, each with a minimum document frequency of 0.0001, so words found in a tiny share of reports do not become columns.
  4. The TF-IDF columns are combined with structured features of the bug, such as severity, keywords and whether steps to reproduce are present, and passed to an XGBoost classifier.
  5. The target of the component model is the product component, restricted to components that have enough bugs to learn from.
  6. The 2019 Mozilla Hacks post reports that the tool was deployed in production at the end of February 2019 to assign a product and component to each new untriaged bug, and that it acts only when its confidence is at least 60%.

Why this approach fits

Bug reports are short, jargon-heavy and plentiful, and only part of them carry a human label. That suits sparse TF-IDF features with a tree ensemble, a combination that is cheap to train and to evaluate. The cleanup step does much of the modelling work: replacing one-off strings (URLs, file names) with placeholder tokens turns thousands of rare words into a few features that generalise.

Limitation or tradeoff

Counting words cannot read order or context, so "works after the patch" and "broken after the patch" look alike, and new jargon or a renamed component appears as unseen vocabulary until the model is retrained. The classifier also learns from past human triage, including its inconsistencies. The model has changed since the 2019 post, which lists the title, first comment and keywords as its features, so read the repository for the current feature set.

4Common mistakes

  1. Fit the vectorizer, vocabulary and idf on all the data before splitting into training and test sets.

    Why it causes problems
    Document frequencies from the test texts leak into the training features and test-only words enter the vocabulary, so the held-out score is optimistic and the model looks better than it will on new text.
    How to recognise it
    The score drops when the vectorizer is moved inside a pipeline that refits it for each cross-validation fold, or when you count how many vocabulary terms occur only in the test texts.
    How to fix it
    Fit the vectorizer on the training texts only and call transform on the validation and test texts. Put the vectorizer and the model in one scikit-learn pipeline so that cross-validation refits both on each fold.

  2. Preprocess text one way at training time and another way at serving time.

    Why it causes problems
    The model meets tokens it never learned or misses ones it did, so accuracy falls quietly with no error message. A different lowercasing rule, stop list or tokenizer regular expression is enough.
    How to recognise it
    Run the same twenty sentences through the training code and the serving code and compare the tokens. A higher share of out-of-vocabulary tokens in live traffic than in the test set is another sign.
    How to fix it
    Package the cleanup, tokenizer, vectorizer and model as one versioned artefact, and keep a small set of example texts with their expected tokens as a regression test.

  3. Remove stop words and stem every word by default because tutorials do.

    Why it causes problems
    Negations and short phrases disappear and different words can merge, while the textbook reports no improvement in aggregate for English retrieval and pre-trained models expect text in its normal form.
    How to recognise it
    Print which tokens your list removes and look for words such as "not" or "no". Run an ablation on the validation set with each step switched off and compare the metric.
    How to fix it
    Begin with lowercasing and tokenization only, add one step at a time, and keep a step only if the validation metric improves. Remove negations from any stop list and try bigrams for short phrases.

  4. Reuse an English text pipeline for codes, part numbers, other languages or domain jargon.

    Why it causes problems
    A default tokenizer splits "X-200B" into pieces, drops one-letter tokens, and cuts languages without spaces in the wrong places, so identifiers that users type exactly can no longer be matched.
    How to recognise it
    Print the tokens for twenty typical inputs from your own data and look for broken identifiers, missing short tokens and merged compounds.
    How to fix it
    Write a tokenizer pattern or analyzer for the domain, mask noisy strings with placeholder tokens as bugbug does, and use a language-specific segmenter or a subword tokenizer where words are not separated by spaces.

  5. Use a pre-trained model with a different tokenizer from the one it was trained with, or let long inputs be cut off without noticing.

    Why it causes problems
    Token ids only mean something under the vocabulary the model was trained with, so a mismatched tokenizer feeds it nonsense, and silent truncation drops the end of a long text where the answer may be.
    How to recognise it
    Accuracy near chance after swapping a tokenizer, or token counts of long inputs above the model's maximum length.
    How to fix it
    Load the tokenizer and the model from the same checkpoint, check the maximum input length, and split long texts into chunks (see tokens and context windows).

Show solution

One script implements all four parts:

python
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

TITLES = [
    "Reset your password",
    "Password reset email not arriving",
    "Change your billing address",
    "Update your billing card",
    "Export your data as CSV",
    "Delete your account and data",
]
STOP_WORDS = {"your", "as", "and", "not"}


def tokenize(text, stem=None):
    tokens = [t for t in re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower()) if t not in STOP_WORDS]
    return [stem(t) for t in tokens] if stem else tokens


def compute_idf(token_lists):
    n = len(token_lists)
    df = Counter(t for tokens in token_lists for t in set(tokens))
    return {t: math.log((1 + n) / (1 + df[t])) + 1 for t in df}


def unit_vector(tokens, idf):
    counts = Counter(t for t in tokens if t in idf)
    raw = {t: c * idf[t] for t, c in counts.items()}
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in raw.values()))
    return {t: v / norm for t, v in raw.items()} if norm else {}


def search(query, titles, stem=None):
    token_lists = [tokenize(title, stem) for title in titles]
    idf = compute_idf(token_lists)
    q = unit_vector(tokenize(query, stem), idf)
    scores = []
    for title, tokens in zip(titles, token_lists):
        d = unit_vector(tokens, idf)
        scores.append((sum(w * d.get(t, 0.0) for t, w in q.items()), title))
    return sorted(scores, key=lambda pair: -pair[0])


def toy_stem(word):
    for suffix in ("ing", "s"):
        if word.endswith(suffix) and len(word) - len(suffix) >= 3:
            return word[: -len(suffix)]
    return word


def better_stem(word):
    stem = toy_stem(word)
    if len(stem) >= 3 and stem[-1] == stem[-2] and stem[-1] not in "ls":
        stem = stem[:-1]
    return stem


def show(label, query, stem=None):
    print(label, repr(query))
    for score, title in search(query, TITLES, stem)[:3]:
        print(f"  {score:.4f}  {title}")


show("A", "reset password email")
show("B", "resetting passwords")
print("toy_stem   :", toy_stem("resetting"), toy_stem("billing"), toy_stem("address"))
show("C", "resetting passwords", toy_stem)
print("better_stem:", better_stem("resetting"), better_stem("billing"), better_stem("address"))
show("D", "resetting passwords", better_stem)

SituationFirst pipelineStep to skip or customiseDecision check
A. Support emailsLowercase, word and bigram counts with a minimum document frequency, TF-IDF, logistic regression or naive BayesKeep negations and product names; do not stem. The linear model's weights give the agents the words behind each decisionMacro-averaged F1 on a held-out split or cross-validation. Move on only if errors are paraphrases that no keyword captures, and the gain justifies a GPU or API cost
B. Part-number searchAn analyzer that keeps hyphenated codes as one token, lowercase, BM25 over an inverted index, synonyms for brand spellingsDo not stem or split part numbers; stem only the free-text description fieldBuild a small judged set from search logs and compare recall at 10 and ranking quality; add embeddings later only if exact-match search misses meaning
C. Entity extractionA pre-trained token-classification model fine-tuned on the 5,000 sentences, with a gazetteer only for fixed listsDo not lowercase if the model is cased, and use its own tokenizer; no stemming or stop-word removalEntity-level precision, recall and F1 on held-out sentences with names not seen in training; a dictionary baseline shows how many new names it misses
D. Review sentimentWord and bigram TF-IDF with logistic regression as the baseline, then a fine-tuned small pre-trained encoderRemove negations from any stop list, or use no listF1 on held-out reviews, and a separate score on the subset containing negation or sarcasm; move to the encoder if the baseline fails on that subset

The common thread is that the classic pipeline is the starting point whenever the label is carried by keywords and the team needs to inspect decisions, that identifiers and negations must survive preprocessing, and that a pre-trained model is justified by a measured gain on the cases the baseline gets wrong. Situation C is the clearest case for a pre-trained model from the start, because new names cannot be listed in advance, while Situation B is mostly a tokenization and analyzer problem.

Check your answer

  • Does each answer name a representation and a model, and a baseline that could be run on the same held-out set as the alternative?
  • Does each answer protect the information the task depends on, such as identifiers, names and negations, from the preprocessing steps?
  • Is every proposal to adopt a pre-trained model tied to a measurable failure of the baseline rather than to a preference?

  • 8.2. Feature extraction — scikit-learn 1.9.1 documentation (opens in a new tab)

    scikit-learn developers

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 40 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    How CountVectorizer and the TF-IDF transformers build a count matrix and TF-IDF weights, with the exact smoothed idf and L2 normalisation formulas, a numeric example, and the warnings about stop-word lists, stemming and custom tokenizers.

    NLTK Project (Bird, Klein and Loper)

    BookBeginnerFreeAbout 45 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    Hands-on text normalisation in Python: regular-expression tokenizers, the Porter and Lancaster stemmers and the WordNet lemmatizer on one sample sentence, with the authors' advice on when each is appropriate.
    When to use it
    When you want to try stemmers and lemmatizers on your own text and compare what they return before deciding whether a normalisation step is worth keeping.

  • Stemming and lemmatization (Introduction to Information Retrieval) (opens in a new tab)

    Cambridge University Press (online edition hosted by Stanford NLP Group)

    BookIntermediateFreeAbout 20 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The textbook definitions of stemming and lemmatization, the rule groups of the Porter algorithm, and the evidence on how normalisation affects recall and precision in English retrieval.
    When to use it
    When you need to decide whether to stem for a search or classification task, or want the reasoning behind the Porter rules rather than the library call.

  • Tokenization algorithms (opens in a new tab)

    Hugging Face

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 30 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    Why word-level and character-level tokenization both fall short, and how byte pair encoding, Unigram, SentencePiece and WordPiece build subword vocabularies, with small worked examples of each merge step.
    When to use it
    When you start working with pre-trained models and need to understand why their tokenizer is part of the model and how rare words are split.

  • Natural Language Processing and Large Language Models (LLM Course, chapter 1) (opens in a new tab)

    Hugging Face

    CourseBeginnerFreeAbout 20 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A short definition of NLP and the main families of tasks (sentence classification, word classification, text generation, answer extraction and sentence generation) with an example of each.
    When to use it
    When you want a quick map of the task landscape before choosing between the classic pipeline and a pre-trained model.
