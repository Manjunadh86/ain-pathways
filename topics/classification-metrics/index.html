Classification Metrics

A classification metric is a number computed from a model's predictions and the true labels that answers one question about the model: how often it is right, how trustworthy its positive calls are, how many real positives it finds, how well it ranks cases, or how good its probabilities are.

1Overview

Why it matters

A classifier is almost always judged by one number, and the wrong number hides the failure that matters. A fraud model that approves every payment is 99% accurate when 1% of payments are fraudulent. A screening test that finds 90% of cases still produces mostly false alarms when the condition is rare. A model with an excellent ranking score can output probabilities that are useless for pricing a risk.

Choosing a metric is choosing which mistakes you can afford. Data scientists, product owners, clinicians and reviewers who sign off on a classifier all need to read a confusion matrix, tell precision from recall, and understand that the cut-off applied to a score is a business decision rather than a constant 0.5. The same few numbers return in model monitoring, in experiments on ranking systems and in regulatory submissions, so the vocabulary pays off well beyond the first project.

Key terms

Confusion matrix
A table that counts predictions by true class and predicted class. For two classes it holds the true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives.
Prevalence (base rate)
The share of examples that truly belong to the positive class. It changes precision but not recall or the false positive rate.
Precision
Of the examples the model flags as positive, the share that really are positive. Medicine calls it positive predictive value.
Recall (sensitivity)
Of the examples that really are positive, the share the model flags. Also called the true positive rate.
Specificity
Of the examples that really are negative, the share the model leaves alone. The false positive rate is 1 minus specificity.
F1 score
The harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is high only when both are high, and it ignores true negatives.
Decision threshold
The score at or above which the model predicts the positive class. Moving it changes every threshold-based metric.
ROC AUC
The area under the curve of true positive rate against false positive rate over all thresholds. It equals the probability that a random positive is scored above a random negative.
Average precision
A summary of the precision-recall curve that adds up the precision at each recall step. A model that scores at random gets about the prevalence.
Log loss
The average negative log of the probability the model gave to the true class. It punishes confident wrong predictions heavily.

2Explanation

Introduction

The logistic regression lesson ended with a model that outputs a probability and a rule that turns it into a label: predict "yes" when the probability reaches 0.5. This lesson is about judging that model. The judging is harder than it looks, because "how good is it?" has several different answers depending on which mistake you are afraid of.

Start with a smoke alarm. It can fail in two ways. It can ring when nothing is burning (a false alarm), or stay silent while something is burning (a miss). A very sensitive alarm rings at burnt toast and rarely misses a fire. A very quiet one never annoys anyone and sometimes misses a fire. Neither is "more accurate" in any useful sense. They make different mistakes, and which one you want depends on what each mistake costs.

Now scale it up. A fraud model scores 10,000 card payments, 100 of which are fraudulent. A "model" that approves every payment is right 9,900 times, which is 99% accuracy, and it catches no fraud at all. Accuracy is the share of correct calls, and when one class is rare it mostly measures how well you handle the common class. The fix is to count the two kinds of mistake separately. Call the thing you are looking for the positive class (fraud, disease, spam). Every prediction then falls into one of four boxes: a true positive, a false positive, a false negative or a true negative. The table of those four counts is the confusion matrix, and almost every classification metric is a ratio built from it.

The diagram shows the three families you will meet. Threshold metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) describe the model after one cut-off has been chosen. Curve metrics (ROC AUC, average precision) sweep the cut-off over every value and describe how well the model orders cases. Log loss ignores the cut-off and judges the probabilities themselves. The end of the lesson ties them together by choosing a cut-off from the cost of each kind of error. For splitting data so that these numbers are honest, see train/test splits and cross-validation. For what to do when the positive class is very rare, see handling imbalanced data.

How it works

The confusion matrix

Each example gets one prediction and one true label. Counting the combinations gives four numbers:

scikit-learn uses the same orientation, rows for the true class and columns for the predicted class, but lists the negative class first. For 0/1 labels confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred).ravel() therefore returns tn, fp, fn, tp in that order, as its metrics guide (opens in a new tab) shows. Other tools transpose or reorder the table, so check the axes before quoting a number.

Accuracy, and why a baseline comes first

accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}accuracy=TP+TN+FP+FNTP+TN​

Accuracy is the share of correct calls. In the 10,000-payment example, always approving gives 9,900 true negatives, 100 false negatives and an accuracy of 0.99 with no fraud caught, a point Google's Machine Learning Crash Course (opens in a new tab) also makes. Always compute the accuracy of the trivial baseline that predicts the majority class, and read your model's accuracy as the gain over it. Balanced accuracy, the mean of recall and specificity, scores that baseline at 0.5.

Precision, recall and specificity

precision=TPTP+FP,recall=TPTP+FN,specificity=TNTN+FP\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad \text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}precision=TP+FPTP​,recall=TP+FNTP​,specificity=TN+FPTN​
  • Precision answers "when the model says positive, how often is it right?" Low precision means wasted reviews and blocked customers.
  • Recall, also called sensitivity or the true positive rate (TPR), answers "of the real positives, how many did the model find?" Low recall means missed fraud or missed disease.
  • Specificity answers "of the real negatives, how many were left alone?" The false positive rate is FPR=FP/(FP+TN)=1−specificity\text{FPR} = FP/(FP+TN) = 1 - \text{specificity}FPR=FP/(FP+TN)=1−specificity.

Recall and FPR are each computed inside one true class, so they do not change when the mix of classes changes, provided the positives and negatives themselves look the same as before. Precision mixes the classes, so it does. With prevalence π\piπ (the share of examples that are positive),

precision=TPR⋅πTPR⋅π+FPR⋅(1−π)\text{precision} = \frac{\text{TPR}\cdot \pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot \pi + \text{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)}precision=TPR⋅π+FPR⋅(1−π)TPR⋅π​

A model that finds 80% of the positives (TPR 0.8) and wrongly flags 10% of the negatives (FPR 0.1) has precision 0.89 when positives and negatives are equally common, and only 0.07 when there are 100 negatives for every positive:

F1 and F-beta

When you need one number for the positive class, the F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision PPP and recall RRR. The general F-beta score weights recall β\betaβ times as heavily as precision:

A harmonic mean is dragged toward the smaller value: precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 gives F1=0.0198F_1 = 0.0198F1​=0.0198, where the plain average would say 0.505. F1 never looks at true negatives, so a large pool of easy negatives cannot inflate it. With β=2\beta = 2β=2 recall counts more, with β=0.5\beta = 0.5β=0.5 precision does.

Scores, thresholds and operating points

Most models output a score, not a label. A decision threshold ttt turns the score into a call: predict positive when the score is at least ttt. scikit-learn's guide to tuning the decision threshold (opens in a new tab) states the defaults: 0.5 for predict_proba and 0 for decision_function. Each threshold gives its own confusion matrix, called an operating point. Lowering ttt flags more cases, so recall and FPR can only rise or stay level. Precision usually falls but can move either way, because the added cases may be better or worse than those already flagged. Google's crash course (opens in a new tab) warns that 0.5 is a poor choice when one error is costlier than the other or the classes are imbalanced.

ROC curve and ROC AUC

The ROC curve plots TPR (vertical) against FPR (horizontal) at every threshold. It starts at (0, 0), where nothing is flagged, and ends at (1, 1), where everything is. A model that ranks at random follows the diagonal, and a perfect one passes through the top-left corner. The area under the curve (AUC) has a direct meaning: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative (Machine Learning Crash Course (opens in a new tab)). A value of 0.5 is random ranking, 1.0 is perfect ordering, and below 0.5 the ranking is reversed. That meaning also gives a way to compute it: count the (positive, negative) pairs the model orders correctly, giving a tied pair one half.

ROC AUC uses only the order of the scores. Any strictly increasing rescaling of the scores leaves it unchanged, so it says nothing about whether a score of 0.8 really means "80% likely". Because TPR and FPR are within-class rates, AUC does not move when prevalence changes, as long as the model behaves the same way inside each class. That makes it comparable across datasets, and it can also flatter a model when positives are rare.

Precision-recall curve and average precision

The precision-recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall as the threshold moves. Its usual summary, average precision (AP), adds up the precision reached at each step, weighted by how much recall grew at that step:

AP=∑n(Rn−Rn−1) Pn\text{AP} = \sum_n (R_n - R_{n-1})\,P_nAP=n∑​(Rn​−Rn−1​)Pn​

scikit-learn computes exactly this step sum with no interpolation, and its guide notes that linear interpolation of the PR curve can be over-optimistic. A random model scores about the prevalence, so the baseline moves with the class mix, unlike ROC's fixed 0.5. Saito and Rehmsmeier (opens in a new tab) show the consequence: with 1,000 positives and either 1,000 or 10,000 negatives, the ROC curves and AUC of their simulated classifiers stayed the same while the PR curves changed sharply. Prefer PR curves and AP when positives are rare and the quality of the flagged list is what matters. Prefer ROC AUC when both classes matter and you want a ranking quality that does not depend on prevalence.

Log loss: judging the probabilities

log loss=−1n∑i=1n[yiln⁡p^i+(1−yi)ln⁡(1−p^i)]\text{log loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \ln \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\ln(1-\hat{p}_i)\Big]log loss=−n1​i=1∑n​[yi​lnp^​i​+(1−yi​)ln(1−p^​i​)]

Each example costs the negative log of the probability the model gave to its true class: −ln⁡0.9=0.105-\ln 0.9 = 0.105−ln0.9=0.105, −ln⁡0.5=0.693-\ln 0.5 = 0.693−ln0.5=0.693, −ln⁡0.1=2.303-\ln 0.1 = 2.303−ln0.1=2.303, −ln⁡0.01=4.605-\ln 0.01 = 4.605−ln0.01=4.605. Confident mistakes are punished steeply, and a probability of exactly 0 on the true class would cost infinity. Log loss is the quantity logistic regression minimises, and scikit-learn calls it a strictly proper scoring rule: it is lowest, in expectation, when the reported probabilities equal the true ones. Its calibration guide (opens in a new tab) adds that log loss mixes calibration, discriminating power and data uncertainty, so a better score does not prove better calibration.

The absolute value depends on how hard the data are. Predicting the prevalence π\piπ for every example scores −πln⁡π−(1−π)ln⁡(1−π)-\pi\ln\pi-(1-\pi)\ln(1-\pi)−πlnπ−(1−π)ln(1−π), which is 0.562 for π=0.25\pi = 0.25π=0.25 and 0.098 for π=0.02\pi = 0.02π=0.02. Compare against that constant baseline, not against zero. The Brier score, the mean squared difference between probability and outcome, is a close relative.

From error costs to a threshold

Give each mistake a cost, cFPc_{FP}cFP​ for a false alarm and cFNc_{FN}cFN​ for a miss, and let correct calls cost nothing. Suppose the score sss is a calibrated probability that the case is positive. Flagging it costs (1−s) cFP(1-s)\,c_{FP}(1−s)cFP​ in expectation, and leaving it alone costs s cFNs\,c_{FN}scFN​. Flag it when the second is larger:

s>t∗=cFPcFP+cFNs > t^{*} = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}}s>t∗=cFP​+cFN​cFP​​

Equal costs give 0.5. A miss five times worse than a false alarm gives 1/6≈0.1671/6 \approx 0.1671/6≈0.167. Two cautions apply. First, the formula holds only for calibrated probabilities. When the scores are not calibrated, compute the total cost at every candidate threshold on labelled data and take the cheapest. This is what scikit-learn's TunedThresholdClassifierCV automates; by default it maximises balanced accuracy with 5-fold stratified cross-validation, and you can give it your own scorer. Second, the threshold is itself fitted to data, so choose it on data the model was not trained on or it will look better than it is. When the limit is capacity rather than cost, such as a team that can review 200 cases a day, set the threshold so that about 200 cases are flagged and report the precision among them.

import math

# (model score for "fraud", true label: 1 = fraud) for 20 card payments
payments = [
    (0.95, 1), (0.92, 0), (0.90, 1), (0.74, 1), (0.66, 0),
    (0.55, 0), (0.45, 1), (0.38, 0), (0.35, 1), (0.33, 0),
    (0.28, 0), (0.25, 0), (0.22, 0), (0.20, 0), (0.18, 0),
    (0.15, 0), (0.12, 0), (0.10, 0), (0.07, 0), (0.04, 0),
]
COST_FP, COST_FN = 1, 5
positives = sum(label for _, label in payments)
negatives = len(payments) - positives

def counts(threshold):                      # flag every payment with score >= threshold
    tp = sum(1 for s, y in payments if s >= threshold and y == 1)
    fp = sum(1 for s, y in payments if s >= threshold and y == 0)
    return tp, fp, positives - tp, negatives - fp          # tp, fp, fn, tn

tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(0.5)
print(f"t=0.5  TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn} TN={tn}")
print(f"accuracy={(tp + tn) / 20:.2f}  always-legit accuracy={negatives / 20:.2f}")
print(f"precision={tp / (tp + fp):.3f}  recall={tp / (tp + fn):.3f}  F1={2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn):.3f}")

pairs_won = sum(1.0 if sp > sn else 0.5 if sp == sn else 0.0
                for sp, yp in payments if yp == 1 for sn, yn in payments if yn == 0)
print(f"ROC AUC={pairs_won / (positives * negatives):.3f}  ({pairs_won:.0f} of {positives * negatives} pairs)")

average_precision, previous_recall = 0.0, 0.0
for t in sorted({s for s, _ in payments}, reverse=True):    # lower the threshold one score at a time
    tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(t)
    average_precision += (tp / positives - previous_recall) * (tp / (tp + fp))
    previous_recall = tp / positives
print(f"average precision={average_precision:.4f}  (random scores would give {positives / 20:.2f})")

log_loss = -sum(math.log(s if y == 1 else 1 - s) for s, y in payments) / 20
print(f"log loss={log_loss:.4f}  constant-prevalence log loss="
      f"{-(0.25 * math.log(0.25) + 0.75 * math.log(0.75)):.4f}")

def cost(threshold):
    tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(threshold)
    return fp * COST_FP + fn * COST_FN

best = min({s for s, _ in payments}, key=cost)
print(f"cost at t=0.5: {cost(0.5)}; cheapest threshold={best} with cost {cost(best)}; "
      f"flag nobody: {positives * COST_FN}; flag everybody: {negatives * COST_FP}")

Edge cases and limitations

Metrics that divide by zero
Precision is 0/0 when nothing is flagged, and recall is 0/0 when the evaluation set has no positives. scikit-learn's zero_division parameter decides what is returned, and unless you set it the metrics give 0, which can drag down a per-class average without anyone noticing. A precision of 1.0 from a threshold that flags a single payment is not evidence of anything. Report how many cases were flagged next to precision, and how many positives the evaluation set holds next to recall.
Multiclass averages answer different questions
With more than two classes, scikit-learn offers macro, weighted and micro averages. Macro averaging gives every class equal weight, weighted averaging weights by class frequency, and micro averaging pools the counts, which equals accuracy when each example has exactly one label. A rare class that the model handles badly barely moves a micro or weighted score and can dominate a macro score. State which average you report, and keep the per-class numbers beside it.
High AUC, unusable flagged list
ROC AUC does not depend on prevalence, so a model can have a respectable AUC and still hand reviewers a list in which most flagged cases are false alarms when positives are one in a hundred. In the bar chart above, an operating point of TPR 0.8 and FPR 0.1 gives a precision of 0.074 at a 1 to 100 mix. When positives are rare, report average precision or the precision among the number of cases you can actually review, and look at the part of the ROC curve where you will operate.
Choosing the threshold on the evaluation data
A threshold is a fitted value. If you pick it on the same data you use to report precision and recall, the report is optimistic, and a threshold chosen on few positives is fragile. In the worked example, one fraud scoring 0.35 moves the best threshold from 0.74 to 0.35. Tune the threshold with cross-validation or a separate validation set, report on a third set, and re-check it when prevalence shifts.
Labels that arrive late or only for some cases
Every metric here needs ground truth. If only flagged cases are investigated, recall cannot be measured because missed positives are never discovered, and if labels arrive weeks later the metrics describe an older model. The FDA review of IDx-DR reports a worst-case analysis for participants whose reference grading was missing, and sensitivity drops from 87.4% to 80.7% under it. Record how many cases were unlabelled and bound the effect.
Ranking quality and probability quality are separate
A model can order cases perfectly, with an AUC of 1.0, while its probabilities are squeezed into a narrow band around 0.5, so a threshold derived from costs lands in the wrong place. scikit-learn's calibration guide notes that calibration generally leaves ranking metrics such as ROC AUC alone, with a caveat for isotonic calibration, which introduces ties. Check calibration with a reliability diagram or with log loss before trusting a cost-derived threshold.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • A model's output must be turned into an action such as block, review or treat, and the two errors cost different amounts. Use threshold metrics at a threshold set from those costs.
  • You are comparing several models before any threshold is fixed. Use ROC AUC when the classes are balanced and average precision when positives are rare.
  • The score itself is consumed downstream, for pricing, bidding or a risk estimate. Use log loss or the Brier score together with a calibration check.
  • You monitor a deployed classifier and labels arrive later. Recompute recall, precision and the confusion matrix on each labelled window and compare with a baseline.

Think twice when

  • Do not report accuracy alone when one class is much rarer than the other. Compare with the majority-class baseline and read precision and recall for the rare class.
  • Do not use ROC AUC alone to approve a rare-event detector. It ignores prevalence, so it cannot tell you how many flagged cases will be wrong.
  • Do not compare precision, average precision or log loss across datasets with different class mixes without a baseline for each, because their floors differ.
  • Do not quote any of these numbers from a few dozen rows. The uncertainty can be larger than the differences between candidate models.

A simpler alternative. Compare against a trivial baseline (always predict the majority class, or predict the prevalence as the probability) and read the confusion matrix at one sensible threshold. That is often enough to decide whether a model helps. It stops being enough when the threshold is not settled, when several models must be ranked, or when the predicted probabilities are used directly.

Key takeaways

  • Every threshold-based metric is a ratio of the four confusion-matrix counts, so start by writing the matrix and naming the positive class.
  • When one class is rare, accuracy mostly measures the common class, so compare it with the majority-class baseline and read precision and recall for the class you care about.
  • Recall and false positive rate do not depend on how common the positive class is, but precision does, so a test that looks strong on a balanced sample can produce mostly false alarms in the field.
  • ROC AUC is the probability that a random positive outranks a random negative and ignores calibration, and average precision follows the precision-recall curve and is the more informative of the two when positives are rare.
  • Log loss scores the probabilities themselves and punishes confident mistakes, so judge it against the constant-prevalence baseline rather than against zero.
  • The threshold is a decision, not a model parameter, so derive a starting value from the costs of the two errors, confirm it on labelled data the model was not fitted on, and re-check it when the class mix changes.

Wikipedia ORES: one edit-quality model, two thresholds for two jobs

Wikimedia Foundation (ORES)Documented in official docs

ORES (Objective Revision Evaluation Service) is Wikimedia's machine-learning service that scores edits and pages. Its MediaWiki thresholds page shows how a tool builder asks for the threshold that meets a stated precision or recall target, and its overview page describes the models it hosts. [8][9]

The problem

Wikipedia patrollers need to find damaging edits in a large stream of good ones. A human review queue and an automatic revert bot want opposite things from the same model. A queue can tolerate many false alarms but should miss little, while a bot that reverts edits with no human in the loop must almost never be wrong. A fixed threshold of 0.5 serves neither job: on the thresholds page's table for the English damaging model it gives a precision of 0.347 and a recall of 0.566.

Where this topic fits

ORES returns class probabilities for each edit or page alongside its yes or no prediction. The thresholds page sits between those probabilities and the tool. The tool builder states an operating constraint, such as a minimum precision or a minimum recall, and ORES answers with the threshold and the statistics expected at it. This is the step from a curve metric to a deployed decision.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The tool builder decides which error is more expensive for the job, missing damage (a review queue) or reverting a good edit (a bot).
  2. The builder states the decision as a constraint on the precision-recall trade-off, for example "maximum recall @ precision >= 0.9" for a bot or "maximum precision @ recall >= 0.95" for screening new pages.
  3. A request carrying that constraint returns the score threshold that meets it, together with the precision, recall, false positive rate and share of cases that would match.
  4. For the English Wikipedia damaging model, the page's example bot constraint returns a threshold of 0.936, which gives high precision but finds only a small share of damaging edits.
  5. For the draftquality model, the example screening constraint returns a threshold of 0.016, which finds 95% of vandalism at the price of low precision, so reviewers read only a fraction of new pages.
  6. For a single model such as damaging, the page says the same scores can serve a human review queue at a low threshold and a revert bot at a high one, so only the threshold differs.

Why this approach fits

A probability plus a threshold lets one model serve tools with opposite error tolerances. Writing the target as a constraint, best recall subject to a precision of at least 0.9, turns the precision-recall curve into a lookup, so the tool builder does not have to read a curve by eye. The stated precision also becomes a promise that the bot's maintainers can check against later reverts.

Limitation or tradeoff

The reported statistics are estimates, and the page says they have a margin of error. It also warns that thresholds may change when a model is updated, so an application has to track the model version and ask for the threshold again. If the mix of good and damaging edits changes, the precision at a fixed threshold can drift. The bot threshold in the page's example finds only 5.5% of damaging edits, which is the price of its 92.3% precision. The thresholds page was last edited in 2022 and the overview page warns that the ORES infrastructure is being deprecated, so the exact API and numbers may be out of date even though the method is not.

What the sources document

  • The thresholds page lets a tool ask for the threshold that satisfies an operating constraint such as "maximum recall @ precision >= 0.9" or "maximum precision @ recall >= 0.95", and the response includes statistics such as precision, recall, false positive rate, filter rate, match rate and accuracy at that threshold. [8]
  • In the page's example for the English Wikipedia damaging model and the constraint "maximum recall @ precision >= 0.9", the returned threshold is 0.936 with precision 0.923 and recall 0.055, and the page adds that the statistics have some margin of error. [8]
  • In the example for the draftquality model, the constraint "maximum precision @ recall >= 0.95" returns a threshold of 0.016 with recall 0.95 and precision 0.042, and the page says a reviewer can look at pages scoring above it and expect to catch 95% of the vandalism. [8]

FDA review of IDx-DR: sensitivity, specificity and predictive values for an AI retinal screen

IDx (IDx-DR), reviewed by the U.S. FDADocumented in a case study

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's De Novo decision summary for IDx-DR (DEN180001) describes the pivotal clinical study of software that detects more than mild diabetic retinopathy in retinal images taken in primary care. It lists the performance goals, the observed results and the counts behind them. [10]

The problem

The software must flag adults with diabetes who have more than mild diabetic retinopathy so that they are referred, using images taken by operators with no prior imaging experience. A regulator has to decide whether it performs well enough to authorise. Missing disease and referring healthy people are different harms, so a single accuracy figure would not answer the question.

Where this topic fits

The metrics define the acceptance test. A reading centre's grading of fuller imaging gives each participant a true label, the device gives a call, and the confusion matrix of the two yields sensitivity, specificity and the positive and negative predictive values. The goals were set on sensitivity and specificity separately, and the predictive values show what a positive or negative result meant in that study's population.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Each participant is imaged by a novice operator using the device and, in the same visit, by a certified photographer with a different camera, so that each participant can be compared against a reference grading.
  2. A reading centre grades the reference images with three masked readers using majority voting, and each participant is labelled as having more than mild disease or not.
  3. Performance goals are pre-specified as 85.0% for sensitivity and 82.5% for specificity.
  4. Of 819 participants who could be fully analysed, 198 had the disease by the reference standard and 621 did not. The device detected disease in 173 of the 198 and gave a not-detected output for 556 of the 621.
  5. From those counts come sensitivity 173/198, specificity 556/621, positive predictive value 173/238 and negative predictive value 556/581.
  6. Cases the device could not grade because image quality was insufficient are reported separately as an imageability rate, and a worst-case analysis shows how far the results move if missing reference gradings had all been disease.

Why this approach fits

Sensitivity and specificity each condition on the true class, so they usually carry over to other populations better than predictive values do, and they are the quantities the performance goals were set on. The predictive values depend on prevalence and therefore describe this study's population. Reporting all four, plus the share of people who could not be graded, shows the regulator and the clinician where the errors fall.

Limitation or tradeoff

A reader should not carry the 72.7% positive predictive value to a population where the disease is rarer, because precision falls with prevalence: by our calculation it would be about 31% at a prevalence of 5% for the same sensitivity and specificity. The headline rates apply to people the system could grade, and the worst-case analysis shows how much missing reference gradings could move them. The summary's text also gives the prevalence as 23.8% for 198 of 819 participants, a fraction that works out to 24.2%; the counts used here come from the other figures, which agree with each other.

  • The primary outcomes were sensitivity and specificity, with performance thresholds defined at 85.0% for sensitivity and 82.5% for specificity, reflecting pre-specified regulatory requirements. [10]
  • The device correctly identified 173 of the 198 fully analysable participants with more than mild disease (observed sensitivity 87.4%), and 556 of the 621 participants without it received a not-detected output (observed specificity 89.5%). [10]
  • Positive predictive value is reported as 72.7% (173/238) and negative predictive value as 95.7% (556/581). [10]

Example 3 of 5

Stripe's fraud guide: a break-even precision from margins and chargeback costs

Stripe (Radar)Documented in an engineering article

Stripe's guide "A primer on machine learning for fraud detection" explains how card-fraud models are evaluated with precision and recall, works through a break-even precision from a merchant's margin and chargeback costs, and notes that Stripe Radar's machine-learning thresholds trade off merchants' margins against stable block rates. [11]

The problem

A payments business loses money in two ways: on fraud that gets through, where the goods are gone and a chargeback fee is paid, and on legitimate customers who are blocked, where the sale and its profit are lost. The guide states that preventing more disputes means blocking more legitimate customers, so there is no single best threshold. Each business has to ask how often its blocks may be wrong before blocking stops paying for itself.

Where this topic fits

The metric sits after the model and before the rule that blocks a payment. A risk score becomes block or allow through a threshold, the guide defines precision and recall for the blocked set, and it then turns a business's margin and fraud loss into a break-even precision, the lowest precision at which blocking still pays.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The model gives each payment a risk score and a threshold decides which payments are blocked. Precision is the share of blocked payments that were fraud, and recall is the share of all fraud that was blocked.
  2. The business writes down what each mistake costs. In the guide's example an average sale is $26 with an 8% margin, so a legitimate sale earns $2.08 of profit.
  3. A fraudulent sale loses the cost of the goods, $23.92, plus a $15.00 chargeback fee, which is $38.92. One fraud therefore wipes out the profit of 38.92 / 2.08 = 18.71 legitimate sales.
  4. In our reading, blocking a payment pays for itself when its chance of being fraud, p, satisfies p x 38.92 >= (1 - p) x 2.08. The guide states the same break-even as a precision, 1 / (1 + 18.71) = 5.07%.
  5. The business then adjusts its threshold or adds rules so that what it blocks stays above the break-even precision while catching as much fraud as possible.
  6. Because margins differ, a low-margin and a high-margin business should sit at different points on the precision-recall trade-off.

Why this approach fits

Precision and recall are in units a risk team already tracks, payments blocked and fraud caught, and the break-even calculation converts margins into a target for them. It is the cost-based rule from this lesson, t∗=cFP/(cFP+cFN)t^* = c_{FP}/(c_{FP}+c_{FN})t∗=cFP​/(cFP​+cFN​), applied to money. Because fraud is rare, accuracy would hide the trade-off completely, and a break-even precision near 5% shows how permissive the best policy can be when one fraud costs as much as many sales.

Limitation or tradeoff

The model is approximate, as its heading says, and in our reading it leaves out effects such as customers who never return after a wrongful block or repeat attempts by the same fraudster. It also applies to the marginal payment, so the precision of the whole blocked set sits above the break-even value. The guide is a vendor document that describes Radar's thresholds in general terms, so it cannot be used to check any particular merchant's results.

What the sources document

  • The guide defines precision as the fraction of transactions blocked that are actually fraudulent and recall as the fraction of all fraud that is caught by the policy. [11]
  • It states that there is a trade-off between preventing more disputes (false negatives) and reducing the blocking of legitimate customers (false positives). [11]
  • Under an approximate model for break-even precision, it uses an average sale of $26 with an 8% margin, a profit per sale of $2.08, a loss of $38.92 per fraudulent sale ($23.92 of production cost plus a $15 chargeback fee), and a break-even precision of 1 / (1 + 18.71) = 5.07%. [11]
  • It states that Radar's machine-learning thresholds trade off optimising for merchants' margins and keeping block rates stable across Stripe's users, and it includes a section on precision-recall and ROC curves. [11]

Google ad click prediction: log loss, AUC loss and calibration as a monitoring set

GoogleDocumented in a paper

"Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches" (KDD 2013), written by Google engineers, describes a deployed click-through-rate prediction system for online ads. Section 5 explains how they evaluate model changes and Section 7 how they calibrate predictions. [12]

The problem

The system predicts, for every ad shown, the probability that it will be clicked, and those probabilities feed an auction. A model change that ranks ads slightly better but misstates probabilities would distort the auction, and at this scale a gain of a fraction of a percent matters. The team needs numbers that detect small changes reliably, are not fooled by the day's click rate and do not hide trouble in one country or query type.

Where this topic fits

Here the metrics judge probability predictions rather than hard calls, so no threshold is involved. Log loss measures the probabilities directly, AUC loss measures the ordering, and a calibration check compares average predicted and observed click rates. All are computed on logged data as a model change is tested.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The model outputs a probability p = sigmoid(w . x) for each query and ad, and each prediction suffers a log loss once the click or no-click label is known.
  2. Engineers track several metrics at once, including AucLoss (1 minus AUC), because different metrics respond differently to a model change. All are defined so that smaller is better.
  3. Progressive validation scores each prediction just before the model trains on that example, so all the data serve for both training and testing, and results are aggregated hourly.
  4. The same metrics are computed on slices such as country, query topic and layout, because an aggregate gain can hide positive and negative changes in different groups.
  5. Absolute values are not compared across days or markets, since the best achievable log loss depends on the click rate. Changes are reported as a percent change against a baseline model on exactly the same data.
  6. A calibration layer corrects systematic bias, the gap between average predicted and observed click rate on a slice of data, because well-calibrated predictions are needed to run the auction.

Why this approach fits

Ad auctions consume the probability itself, so log loss, which is lowest only for honest probabilities, is the natural measure for it, while AUC loss shows whether the ordering improved. Reporting relative change handles the fact that raw log loss depends on how hard the problem is. As an illustration, a model that only knows the average click rate would score 0.693 per example at a 50% click rate and 0.098 at 2% (our calculation from the entropy formula), so raw values from different markets cannot be compared. Slicing and calibration guard against a metric that improves overall while one market or one probability range gets worse.

Limitation or tradeoff

These are offline metrics on logged data. The paper notes that evaluating models on live traffic is important but more expensive. Its remark that AucLoss reductions of 1% count as large describes this system and is not a general standard for AUC changes. A good aggregate log loss also does not guarantee calibration on every slice, which is why the calibration layer exists.

What the sources document

  • The paper defines the per-example loss as LogLoss, the negative log-likelihood of the label given the predicted probability, for a logistic model that predicts p as the sigmoid of the weights dotted with the features. [12]
  • Section 5 says model changes are evaluated across several metrics, including AucLoss defined as 1 minus AUC, because the metrics respond differently to model changes, and that the metrics are designed so that smaller values are always better. [12]
  • It uses progressive validation, scoring each example before training on it, computes the metrics on slices such as country, query topic and layout, and warns that an aggregate result can hide opposing changes in sub-populations. [12]
  • It says absolute metric values are often misleading because LogLoss depends on the difficulty of the problem, for example a click rate near 50% against one near 2%, so the team looks at relative changes against a baseline model on exactly the same data. [12]
  • The paper states that a detriment of 0.6% in AucLoss is significant for the application, that AucLoss reductions of 1% are considered large, and that accurate, well-calibrated predictions are essential to run the auction. [12]

4Common mistakes

  1. Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data without the majority-class baseline.

    Why it causes problems
    With 1% fraud, a model that approves everything is 99% accurate and catches nothing. A real model at 99.2% looks like an improvement when it may be barely above the baseline, and the team ships a detector whose recall they never measured.
    How to recognise it
    Compute accuracy for a constant prediction of the majority class and print the confusion matrix. If the model's accuracy is within a point or two of the baseline, or recall on the rare class is low, accuracy is hiding the problem. In the worked example the model's 0.75 equals the baseline's 0.75.
    How to fix it
    Report recall and precision for the positive class, balanced accuracy or average precision, and the baseline next to every headline number.

  2. Leaving the decision threshold at the default of 0.5.

    Why it causes problems
    0.5 is optimal only when both errors cost the same and the probabilities are calibrated. When a miss costs five times a false alarm, the cost-based threshold is about 0.17, and a model run at 0.5 misses more positives than the business intended. The opposite holds when a false alarm is the costly error.
    How to recognise it
    Ask what a false positive and a false negative each cost, and compute the total cost at several thresholds on held-out data. If the cheapest threshold is far from 0.5, the default is wrong for this problem.
    How to fix it
    Set the threshold from the costs or from a capacity or precision constraint, using cross-validation or a validation set, and document the value. scikit-learn's TunedThresholdClassifierCV does the search.

  3. Choosing the threshold or the metric on the same data used to report performance.

    Why it causes problems
    A threshold tuned on the test set is fitted to its noise, so the reported precision and recall are optimistic. With few positives the effect is large, as the worked example shows, where one payment moves the best threshold from 0.74 to 0.35.
    How to recognise it
    Check whether the data used to pick the threshold also appear in the final report. A large drop in precision or recall when the same threshold is applied to fresh data is the symptom.
    How to fix it
    Split the data three ways, or tune with nested cross-validation. Fit the model on training data, choose the threshold on validation data and report once on a test set that was not touched.

  4. Quoting precision, average precision or log loss from one dataset as if it would hold on another class mix.

    Why it causes problems
    Precision depends on prevalence. The same operating point of TPR 0.8 and FPR 0.1 gives precision 0.89 at a 1 to 1 mix and 0.07 at 1 to 100. A model validated on a balanced sample can bury a review team in false alarms in production.
    How to recognise it
    Compare the positive rate of the evaluation set with the rate in live traffic. If they differ, the precision you measured does not apply, even though recall and FPR still do.
    How to fix it
    Evaluate on data with the production class mix, or recompute precision from recall, FPR and the real prevalence using the formula in this lesson, and compare average precision with its prevalence baseline.

  5. Treating a high ROC AUC as proof that the predicted probabilities can be used as probabilities.

    Why it causes problems
    AUC depends only on the order of the scores. A model can have an AUC near 1 and still output 0.6 for cases that are positive 95% of the time, so a threshold derived from costs, or a price or bid computed from the probability, is wrong.
    How to recognise it
    Plot a reliability diagram and compare the average predicted probability with the observed positive rate in each bin, or compare log loss with the constant-prevalence baseline. A good AUC with poor log loss signals miscalibration.
    How to fix it
    Calibrate the scores on held-out data, for example with sigmoid or isotonic calibration, and judge them by log loss or the Brier score as well as by AUC.

python
import math

emails = [  # (model's spam score, true label: 1 = spam)
    (0.97, 1), (0.92, 1), (0.86, 0), (0.81, 1), (0.64, 0), (0.57, 1),
    (0.46, 0), (0.31, 0), (0.22, 0), (0.14, 0), (0.09, 0), (0.03, 0),
]
COST_FP, COST_FN = 10, 1     # a lost real email is ten times worse than a spam message in the inbox
spam = sum(label for _, label in emails)
legit = len(emails) - spam


def counts(t):
    tp = sum(1 for s, y in emails if s >= t and y == 1)
    fp = sum(1 for s, y in emails if s >= t and y == 0)
    return tp, fp, spam - tp, legit - fp          # tp, fp, fn, tn


tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(0.5)
print(f"t=0.5: TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn} TN={tn}")
print(f"accuracy={(tp + tn) / 12:.3f}  always-legit accuracy={legit / 12:.3f}")
print(f"precision={tp / (tp + fp):.3f}  recall={tp / (tp + fn):.3f}  F1={2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn):.3f}")

pairs_won = sum(1.0 if a > b else 0.5 if a == b else 0.0
                for a, y in emails if y == 1 for b, z in emails if z == 0)
print(f"ROC AUC={pairs_won / (spam * legit):.3f}  ({pairs_won:.0f} of {spam * legit} pairs)")
print(f"log loss={-sum(math.log(s if y == 1 else 1 - s) for s, y in emails) / 12:.4f}")

for t in sorted({s for s, _ in emails}, reverse=True):
    tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(t)
    print(f"t={t:.2f}  TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn}  cost={fp * COST_FP + fn * COST_FN}")

Exercise 2

Why a good screening test gives mostly false alarms

AnalysisBeginnerAbout 30 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

A screening test for a condition has a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 95%. Consider 10,000 people screened. A flagged person is sent for a confirmatory test that costs 200 units and is assumed to be perfect. A case that is never confirmed costs 5,000 units later.

(a) Build the confusion matrix and compute precision and negative predictive value when 1% of the people have the condition, and again when 10% do. (b) Explain in two sentences why the same test gives such different precision. (c) For each prevalence compare the total cost of three policies: no screening, screening followed by confirmation of the flagged people, and confirming everybody. (d) Compute the break-even precision, c_FP / (c_FP + c_FN), taking c_FP as the 200-unit confirmation and c_FN as the 5,000-unit cost of a missed case, and compare it with the precision from (a).

Requirements

  • Show the four counts for each prevalence, with working.
  • Compute precision and negative predictive value for each prevalence.
  • Compute and compare the three policy costs for each prevalence.
  • State whether the precision in each case is above the break-even precision, and what that implies.

Expected behaviour

At 1% prevalence: TP = 90, FN = 10, FP = 495, TN = 9,405, precision 0.154, NPV 0.999. At 10%: TP = 900, FN = 100, FP = 450, TN = 8,550, precision 0.667, NPV 0.988. Screening plus confirmation costs 167,000 units at 1% and 770,000 at 10%, against 500,000 and 5,000,000 for no screening and 2,000,000 for confirming everybody. The break-even precision is 0.0385.

Hint 1

Start from the number of people with the condition (prevalence times 10,000), then apply sensitivity to them and the false positive rate (1 minus specificity) to everyone else.

Hint 2

Precision is TP divided by everyone flagged, which is TP plus FP, so the flagged count also gives the number of confirmations to pay for.

Hint 3

The cost of screening is the confirmations for everyone flagged plus 5,000 for each case the screen missed.

Show solution

1% prevalence. 100 people have the condition and 9,900 do not. Sensitivity 0.90 gives TP = 90 and FN = 10. A false positive rate of 0.05 on 9,900 people gives FP = 495 and TN = 9,405. Flagged people number 90 + 495 = 585, so precision is 90/585 = 0.154, and NPV is 9,405/(9,405 + 10) = 0.999.

10% prevalence. 1,000 people have the condition. TP = 900, FN = 100, FP = 0.05 x 9,000 = 450 and TN = 8,550. Flagged people number 1,350, precision is 900/1,350 = 0.667 and NPV is 8,550/8,650 = 0.988.

Why they differ. Sensitivity and specificity condition on the true class, so they stay the same, but precision divides the true positives by everyone flagged, and when the condition is rare the 5% of healthy people who are wrongly flagged outnumber the 90% of sick people who are correctly flagged.

PolicyCost at 1%Cost at 10%
No screening100 x 5,000 = 500,0001,000 x 5,000 = 5,000,000
Screen, then confirm flagged people585 x 200 + 10 x 5,000 = 167,0001,350 x 200 + 100 x 5,000 = 770,000
Confirm everybody10,000 x 200 = 2,000,0002,000,000

Break-even. 200 / (200 + 5,000) = 0.0385. Both precisions, 0.154 and 0.667, are well above it, so following up the flagged people pays even at 1% prevalence, where 85% of the flagged people turn out to be healthy. A low precision is acceptable when a false alarm is cheap and a miss is expensive, which is the same logic as the fraud break-even calculation in the Stripe example. The lesson from the FDA IDx-DR figures is the other half: a positive predictive value of 72.7% was reported for a study population with roughly a quarter of participants affected, and it would be lower in a population with fewer cases.

  • Do your four counts add up to 10,000 for each prevalence?
  • Is precision 0.154 at 1% and 0.667 at 10%, and can you say which of sensitivity, specificity and precision changed?
  • Is screening the cheapest policy in both cases, and by what margin?
  • Can you explain why the break-even precision is so much lower than the precision in either case?
