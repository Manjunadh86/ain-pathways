Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a way of answering questions with a language model in which the application first searches a prepared collection of documents for passages relevant to the question, then puts those passages in the prompt so that the model answers from them and not from memory alone. Because the passages are known, each statement in the answer can be traced to a source.

1Overview

Why it matters

A language model knows what was in its training data up to a cutoff date. It has never seen your company's handbook, last week's price list or the ticket a customer opened this morning, and when asked about them it tends to produce a fluent guess. RAG is the standard way to close that gap without retraining anything. The facts stay in documents you control, a search step picks the few passages a question needs, and the model writes the answer from those passages. Updating what the system knows means updating an index, and every answer can point at its source so that a person can check it.

RAG also adds a component that can fail. When a RAG answer is wrong, either the right passage never reached the prompt or the model misused a passage that did, and the two need different fixes. Anyone building a support assistant, an internal search tool or a document question-answering feature has to tell those apart. They also have to recognise when RAG is the wrong tool: when the documents are small enough to paste into the prompt, or when the problem is how the model behaves and not what it knows.

Key terms

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
Answering with a language model after first retrieving relevant passages from an external collection and placing them in the prompt.
Knowledge base
The collection of documents the system is allowed to answer from. It is also called the corpus.
Chunk
A passage cut from a document, typically a few hundred tokens long, that is indexed and retrieved as one unit.
Index
A data structure built ahead of time that makes it fast to find the chunks most similar to a query. It is usually a vector index and sometimes a keyword index.
Retriever
The component that takes a question and returns the top-scoring chunks from the index.
Top-k
The number of chunks the retriever returns for each question.
Grounding
Tying each statement in an answer to a passage supplied in the prompt, so that the answer rests on those passages and not on the model's memory.
Citation
A pointer from a statement in the answer to the passage, and through it the document, that supports the statement.
Parametric knowledge
What a model learned during training and stores in its weights, as opposed to text supplied in the prompt.
Recall at k
The share of test questions for which the passage containing the answer appears among the top k retrieved chunks.

2Explanation

Introduction

In Prompt Engineering you saw that the most reliable way to get a correct answer about your own material is to put that material in the prompt and tell the model what to write when the answer is not there. That works while you can choose the material by hand. A support assistant cannot. It has thousands of help articles, and which three matter depends on the question that has just arrived. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) automates the choosing: a search step finds the relevant passages, and the model answers from them.

OpenAI's guide to optimizing accuracy (opens in a new tab) explains the idea with an exam. A student can answer from what they learned in class, or they can bring the textbook and look the answer up. A model answering from its weights is sitting the closed-book exam. It is fluent and fast, it is sometimes wrong, and it cannot say where an answer came from. RAG turns the question into an open-book exam. The result then depends on two things: whether the right page was found, and whether it was read carefully.

Every RAG system has the same three stages:

  • Index. Ahead of time, split the documents into passages called chunks and store them in a form that can be searched quickly, usually as embedding vectors in a vector database.
  • Retrieve. When a question arrives, search the index and keep the few best-matching chunks.
  • Generate. Put those chunks in the prompt with the question and ask the model to answer from them, citing the chunks it used.

The name comes from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues (opens in a new tab). They paired a text-generating model, whose knowledge sits in its weights (parametric memory), with a searchable index of Wikipedia passages (non-parametric memory). The index held about 21 million chunks of 100 words each. When the authors swapped a 2018 copy of the index for a 2016 copy, the system's answers about world leaders who had changed in between followed the index, with no retraining. That is still the main attraction. What the system knows is a property of the index, and an index is easy to inspect and update.

This lesson walks through each stage, shows how answers are grounded in retrieved passages and how citations are produced and checked, builds a complete pipeline in plain Python, and ends with the decision you will face most often in practice: RAG, a long prompt, or fine-tuning.

How it works

Stage 1: index, before any question arrives

Indexing is a batch job with four steps:

  1. Load and parse. Read each source (PDF, web page, wiki export, ticket) and extract clean text.
  2. Chunk. Split the text into passages small enough to retrieve precisely. Anthropic's description of a standard pipeline uses chunks of no more than a few hundred tokens (opens in a new tab), and Amazon Bedrock's default produces chunks of about 300 tokens that end on sentence boundaries.
  3. Embed. Turn each chunk into a vector with an embedding model (see Embeddings).
  4. Store. Write the vector, the chunk text and its metadata (document title, URL, section, date, access rights) to the index.

Keep the link from every chunk back to its document. Citations, access control and the removal of outdated content all depend on it. The index is a copy of your documents, so it goes stale when they change. Chunking and Document Ingestion covers how to split well and how to keep the copy fresh.

Stage 2: retrieve, once per question

The retriever embeds the question with the same embedding model, asks the index for the nearest chunk vectors and returns the top kkk of them. Vector Databases and Similarity Search explains how that search stays fast.

Vector search is the common choice, not a requirement. Keyword ranking such as BM25 finds exact strings (an error code, a part number) that embeddings can miss, and many production systems combine the two or rerank the results; see Advanced Retrieval. Whatever the method, two properties hold:

  • The retriever always returns kkk chunks. It has no way of saying that nothing relevant exists. A question about something your documents never mention still comes back with kkk passages.
  • The model can only use what was retrieved. If the passage with the answer is not among the kkk, no prompt will recover it.

The second property makes kkk a real decision. A larger kkk raises the chance that the answer is in the prompt, at the price of more tokens and more irrelevant text for the model to read past.

In a Microsoft study of agricultural question answering (opens in a new tab), the passage a question was written from appeared among the retrieved snippets 61.9% of the time at k=1k = 1k=1, 81.5% at k=3k = 3k=3 and 93.6% at k=10k = 10k=10. The authors chose k=3k = 3k=3 to keep the prompt small. Anthropic, testing a different corpus and retriever, found that passing 20 chunks beat 10 or 5, while warning that extra information can distract the model. There is no universal value. Measure recall on your own questions, as the first exercise does.

Stage 3: generate, from the passages

The application now builds a prompt with four parts:

  1. A rule that restricts the model to the sources, for example "answer using only the sources below".
  2. The retrieved chunks, each with an identifier the model can refer to.
  3. A not-found rule giving the exact reply to use when the sources do not contain the answer.
  4. The question, placed last. Google's long-context guidance (opens in a new tab) and the Prompt Engineering lesson both recommend this order.

Where a passage sits in the prompt matters too. The "Lost in the Middle" study (opens in a new tab) found that models did best when the relevant passage was at the start or the end of a long input and clearly worse when it was in the middle. Keep kkk modest and put the highest-scoring chunks first. The worked example prints a complete prompt of this shape.

Grounding and citations

An answer is grounded when each of its statements is supported by a passage that was in the prompt. A citation makes that support visible. It links a statement to the passage and, through the chunk's metadata, to the document. There are three common ways to get citations.

ApproachHow it worksWhat to watch
Prompted markersThe prompt asks for an identifier such as [hb-02] after each sentence, and your code parses the markers. This works with any model.The model can leave a marker out or cite an identifier it was never given, so validate the markers in code.
Model API featureYou pass passages as structured blocks with citations switched on. Claude's API returns the answer as text blocks, each with a citations list holding the source, the title and the exact cited_text.Anthropic's documentation says the pointers are guaranteed to be valid. It also says the feature cannot be combined with structured outputs.
Managed pipelineA service retrieves and generates in one call. Amazon Bedrock's RetrieveAndGenerate returns the text plus a citations array that maps spans of the answer to the retrieved chunks and their locations.You accept the service's retrieval and prompt defaults unless you configure them.

A citation shows where a statement points. It does not show that the passage supports the statement, or that the passage is right. A 2024 study of commercial legal research tools (opens in a new tab) built on RAG found that they still hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time. Systems where errors are costly therefore add a check after generation, as DoorDash does in the examples below, and keep a person in the loop.

Two places a RAG answer can go wrong

OpenAI's accuracy guide splits RAG failures into two areas. Retrieval fails when it supplies the wrong context, so the model cannot answer, or so much irrelevant context that the useful part is drowned out. The model fails when it receives the right context and does the wrong thing with it.

To tell them apart, open the trace and read the retrieved chunks before you read the answer. If the answer is not in the chunks, fix retrieval: the chunking, the query, kkk or a stale index. If it is there and the reply is still wrong, fix generation: the instructions, the order of the passages or the model. Evaluating RAG Systems turns this habit into metrics.

RAG, long context or fine-tuning?

Long context means skipping retrieval and putting the whole knowledge base in every prompt. Anthropic's advice is direct: if the knowledge base is under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages), include all of it, and use prompt caching to cut the cost. Windows have grown since that was written. Current Claude models accept up to 1 million tokens (opens in a new tab), and so do many Gemini models. Two limits remain. First, cost scales with the corpus on every call. A 150,000-token manual plus 300 tokens of instructions and question sends 150,300 input tokens, while RAG with five 400-token chunks sends 5×400+300=2,3005 \times 400 + 300 = 2{,}3005×400+300=2,300, about 65 times fewer. Second, attention is finite. Anthropic's documentation notes that accuracy and recall degrade as the token count grows, and Google's notes lower accuracy when a prompt must surface several separate facts at once.

Fine-tuning changes the model's weights by training it on examples. OpenAI's guide draws the line by the kind of error. When the model lacks knowledge, has out-of-date knowledge or needs proprietary information, the problem is context, and retrieval is the fix. When the model has the facts but produces the wrong format, tone or reasoning steps, the problem is behaviour, and fine-tuning is the fix. The two combine. In the Microsoft study, fine-tuning raised GPT-4's accuracy from 75% to 81%, and adding RAG to the fine-tuned model took it to 86%.

A workable order of decisions is this. Start with a prompt alone. If knowledge is missing and the corpus fits comfortably and rarely changes, paste it in and cache it. If the corpus is too large, changes often, needs per-user access control or needs source links, use RAG. If the remaining errors are about behaviour, consider fine-tuning.

Worked example: A handbook assistant in plain Python that retrieves three passages, builds a grounded prompt and checks the citations

The knowledge base is a six-passage remote-work handbook for an invented company. Two parts are simplified so that the example needs only the Python standard library (3.9 or later). The index uses TF-IDF vectors, which weight each word by how rare it is across the passages, where a production system would use an embedding model. The model is replaced by one scripted reply, so everyone sees the same output. The three stages are unchanged. Save this as mini_rag.py.

python
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

HANDBOOK = {  # chunk id -> text
    "hb-01": "Remote work: Employees may work remotely up to three days per week.",
    "hb-02": "Working abroad: Employees may work from another country for up to 30 days per calendar "
             "year. Ask your manager for approval at least 14 days before you leave.",
    "hb-03": "Internet costs: Home internet costs are reimbursed up to 30 EUR per month.",
    "hb-04": "Business travel: Take the train for journeys under four hours. "
             "Flights need approval from the travel desk.",
    "hb-05": "Security: Connect through the company VPN whenever you work from another country. "
             "Never use public Wi-Fi without the VPN.",
    "hb-06": "Public holidays: Public holidays follow the calendar of the country in your contract.",
}
STOPWORDS = set("a an and are at be by can do for from how i in is it many my need not of on or "
                "per the to up what with you your".split())
TOP_K, MIN_SCORE = 3, 0.15
NOT_FOUND = "I can't find that in the handbook."
REPLY = ("You can work from another country for up to 30 days per calendar year [hb-02]. "
         "Ask your manager for approval at least 14 days before you leave [hb-02]. "
         "Connect through the company VPN while you are away [hb-05].")


def tokens(text):
    return [word for word in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower()) if word not in STOPWORDS]


def build_index(chunks):  # INDEX: one TF-IDF vector per chunk
    counts = {cid: Counter(tokens(text)) for cid, text in chunks.items()}
    doc_freq = Counter(term for count in counts.values() for term in count)
    idf = {term: math.log(len(chunks) / df) for term, df in doc_freq.items()}
    vectors = {cid: {t: tf * idf[t] for t, tf in count.items()} for cid, count in counts.items()}
    return {"idf": idf, "vectors": vectors}


def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(weight * b.get(term, 0.0) for term, weight in a.items())
    norms = math.sqrt(sum(w * w for w in a.values())) * math.sqrt(sum(w * w for w in b.values()))
    return dot / norms if norms else 0.0


def retrieve(index, question, k=TOP_K):  # RETRIEVE: score every chunk, keep the best k
    query = {t: tf * index["idf"].get(t, 0.0) for t, tf in Counter(tokens(question)).items()}
    scored = [(cosine(query, vector), cid) for cid, vector in index["vectors"].items()]
    return sorted(scored, reverse=True)[:k]


def build_prompt(question, hits):  # GENERATE: rules, sources, then the question
    sources = "\n".join(f"[{cid}] {HANDBOOK[cid]}" for _, cid in hits)
    return ("Answer the question using only the sources below.\n"
            "After every sentence, cite the source it came from, like [hb-02].\n"
            f"If the sources do not contain the answer, reply exactly: {NOT_FOUND}\n\n"
            f"Sources:\n{sources}\n\nQuestion: {question}")


def unsupported(reply, hits):  # sentences with no citation, or citing a chunk that was not retrieved
    allowed = {cid for _, cid in hits}
    bad = []
    for sentence in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", reply.strip()):
        cited = set(re.findall(r"\[(hb-\d+)\]", sentence))
        if not cited or not cited <= allowed:
            bad.append(sentence)
    return bad


def answer(index, question, model=lambda prompt: REPLY):  # the lambda stands in for an LLM call
    hits = retrieve(index, question)
    print("Q:", question)
    print("  ", "  ".join(f"{cid} {score:.3f}" for score, cid in hits))
    if hits[0][0] < MIN_SCORE:
        return NOT_FOUND  # nothing relevant was retrieved, so the model is never called
    reply = model(build_prompt(question, hits))
    bad = unsupported(reply, hits)
    return f"REJECTED: {bad}" if bad else reply


if __name__ == "__main__":
    index = build_index(HANDBOOK)
    first = "How many days can I work from another country, and what do I need to arrange?"
    print("A:", answer(index, first))
    print("--- prompt sent to the model ---")
    print(build_prompt(first, retrieve(index, first)))
    print("---")
    print("A:", answer(index, "What is the dental insurance deductible?"))

Run python3 mini_rag.py:

What to notice:

  • Index. A term's weight is its count times ln⁡(N/df)\ln(N/\text{df})ln(N/df), where N=6N = 6N=6 passages and df is the number of passages that contain it. "days" and "another" occur in two passages and weigh ln⁡3=1.099\ln 3 = 1.099ln3=1.099. "work" and "country" occur in three and weigh ln⁡2=0.693\ln 2 = 0.693ln2=0.693.
  • Retrieve. hb-02 wins with a cosine similarity of 0.385. hb-01 is second because it shares "work" and "days", although it describes a different policy. Retrieval returns near misses, and the reply rightly ignores this one.
  • Generate. The prompt has the four parts from Stage 3. unsupported confirms that every sentence cites a chunk that was retrieved. Change [hb-05] to [hb-04] in REPLY and the run prints REJECTED.
  • Nothing relevant. For the dental question every score is 0.000, yet three chunks still come back. The MIN_SCORE check stops the request before any model call.

For a live system, pass model= a function that calls a chat API, and swap build_index and retrieve for an embedding model and a vector database. The other functions stay as they are.

Edge cases and limitations

The retriever returns k chunks even when nothing is relevant
In the worked example the dental-insurance question scores 0.000 against every passage and still gets three chunks back. Without a guard the model receives unrelated text and may answer from memory. Use two guards: a minimum score below which you skip the model, and a not-found rule in the prompt. The threshold alone is weak. "How many weeks of parental leave do I get?" scores 0.277 against the working-abroad passage because both contain the word "leave", so only the prompt rule protects that answer.
The answer exists but is written in different words
"Do I need permission to fly?" scores 0.000 against every passage in the worked example, although the travel passage answers it with the words "approval" and "flights". Microsoft's Azure AI Search documentation describes the same pattern with a question about a PTO policy for remote workers when the documents say time off and telecommute. Retrieval by embeddings, or keyword and vector search combined, closes most of this gap. The symptom is a not-found reply to a question you know the documents cover.
A chunk loses the context that made it meaningful
Anthropic's example is a chunk from a financial filing that says the company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter. Cut out of its document, the sentence names neither the company nor the quarter. A question that names the company may not retrieve it, and if it is retrieved for another company's question the model has no way to notice. Carry the document title, section and date into each chunk's text or metadata, and show them in the prompt next to the chunk.
A valid citation attached to a wrong statement
Anthropic's documentation guarantees that citations produced by its API point at text you supplied. No API can guarantee that the statement follows from that text. The 2024 study of commercial legal research tools measured hallucination rates of 17% to 33% in products that retrieve real sources. Read a sample of cited passages next to the claims they support, and for high-stakes answers add an automated support check before the answer is shown.
Retrieved text is untrusted input
LangChain's RAG documentation warns about indirect prompt injection: a retrieved chunk can contain text that looks like instructions, and because it shares the context window with your own instructions the model may follow it. Anyone who can add a document to the index can therefore try to steer answers. The documentation's advice is to validate outputs before showing them, checking that answers cite the expected documents and that claims match the retrieved material.
The index holds documents the asker is not allowed to see
An index built from every team's files will retrieve a finance document for anyone whose question matches it. Azure AI Search documents this problem and its fix, which is to trim results by the user's permissions inside the search query. Amazon Bedrock's RetrieveAndGenerate request accepts a user context for the same purpose. Apply the filter during retrieval. An instruction telling the model not to reveal restricted text is not access control.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The knowledge base is larger than the model's context window, or sending all of it on every request would cost too much or respond too slowly.
  • The content changes often (prices, policies, tickets, documentation), and an edit has to show up in answers within hours without retraining anything.
  • Users or auditors need to check answers, so each statement has to link to the passage and document it came from.
  • Different users may see different documents, and access has to be enforced on every request by filtering what is retrieved.

Think twice when

  • The whole corpus fits comfortably in the prompt and rarely changes. Paste it in, cache it, and avoid the retrieval failure modes altogether.
  • The model already has the right facts in front of it and the errors are in format, tone or procedure. That is a behaviour problem for prompting or fine-tuning.
  • The question needs every document and not a few passages: a count, a total, or a summary of the whole corpus. Top-k retrieval only ever sees k chunks.
  • Answering takes several dependent lookups, where the second search depends on what the first one found. One retrieval pass will miss it, which is the case agentic RAG is built for.

A simpler alternative. Put the documents directly in the prompt (long context), with prompt caching if your provider offers it. Anthropic suggests this for knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens. It stops being enough when the corpus outgrows the window, when the token bill or the latency per request becomes too high, or when answers get worse because the relevant passage is buried in unrelated text.

Key takeaways

  • RAG has three stages: index the documents ahead of time, retrieve the few best-matching chunks for each question, and generate an answer from those chunks.
  • The model can only use what was retrieved, and the retriever always returns something, so retrieval quality sets the ceiling and every pipeline needs a not-found path.
  • A grounded prompt restricts the model to identified sources, defines the reply for a missing answer and puts the question last.
  • A citation links a statement to a passage. Validate citations, because a citation shows where a statement points and not that the statement is correct.
  • When an answer is wrong, read the retrieved chunks first. If the answer is not in them it is a retrieval failure, and otherwise it is a generation failure.
  • Use long context when the corpus fits and is stable, RAG when it is large, changing or must be cited, and fine-tuning when the problem is behaviour and not knowledge.

Example 1 of 5

Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases: the index-retrieve-generate pipeline as a managed service

Amazon Web ServicesDocumented in official docs

Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases is the managed RAG service of Amazon Web Services. Its user guide explains how the service carries out RAG in two phases, how content is chunked, and how an application queries a knowledge base and receives a generated response. The API reference defines the RetrieveAndGenerate request and response. [9][10][11][12]

The problem

A team wants answers grounded in its own private documents and does not want to build and operate document parsing, chunking, embedding, a vector index and prompt assembly itself. The user guide presents the service as an out-of-the-box RAG solution that takes over the work of building those pipelines. It also points out that a knowledge base removes the need to keep training a model in order to use private data.

Where this topic fits

The service implements all three stages. Before it, you place documents in a data source and choose an embedding model and a vector store. Indexing happens when the data source is ingested. Retrieval and generation happen inside one API call. After it, your application receives the answer text together with citations and decides how to display them.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. During ingestion the service converts each document to text and splits it into chunks. With default chunking a chunk is about 300 tokens and complete sentences are kept together.
  2. An embedding model converts each chunk to a vector, which is written to a vector index while a mapping back to the original document is kept.
  3. At question time the application sends one RetrieveAndGenerate request that carries the question text, the knowledge base id and the model to generate with.
  4. The service converts the question to a vector, queries the vector index for semantically similar chunks and adds those chunks to the prompt as context. The number of results is configurable.
  5. The model generates the answer. The response returns the text in an output field and a citations array that links parts of the text to the retrieved chunks, with each chunk's content, metadata and source location.
  6. A typical application turns each citation into a footnote that opens the source document, which is what the console's own test window does.

Why this approach fits

The three stages are the same for most document question-answering systems, so a service can own them and expose only the choices that differ between customers: where the documents are, which embedding model and vector store to use, how to chunk, how many results to retrieve and which model writes the answer. Citations come almost for free, because the mapping from chunk to source document was stored at indexing time and the service knows exactly which chunks it put in the prompt.

Limitation or tradeoff

Convenience fixes some decisions early. Chunking is chosen when the data source is set up: one of the documentation's remedies for a prompt that exceeds the character limit is to recreate the data source with smaller chunks. The documentation also states that guardrails are applied to the input and to the generated response but not to the references retrieved at runtime, so retrieved text is not screened. The API reference adds that RetrieveAndGenerate cannot be used with managed knowledge bases, which need a different operation, so check which kind you have before designing around this call.

What the sources document

  • To prepare unstructured data, it is converted to text and split into chunks. The chunks are converted to embeddings and written to a vector index while a mapping to the original document is maintained. [9]
  • At runtime an embedding model converts the user's query to a vector, the vector index is queried for chunks that are semantically similar to it, and the user prompt is augmented with the retrieved chunks before it is sent to the model. [9]
  • Default chunking splits content into chunks of approximately 300 tokens and honours sentence boundaries. Fixed-size, hierarchical and semantic chunking are also offered, as is no chunking. [10]
  • RetrieveAndGenerate returns the generated response in an output field and the cited source chunks in a citations array. Each citation has a generatedResponsePart with the text and its span, and retrievedReferences with the chunk content, metadata and location. [11][12]
  • Guardrails are applied only to the input and the generated response, and not to the references retrieved from knowledge bases at runtime. [11]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

DoorDash Dasher support: a RAG chatbot whose replies must pass a grounding check

DoorDashDocumented in an engineering article

DoorDash's engineering post Path to high-quality LLM-based Dasher support automation (September 2024) describes the chatbot that answers support requests from Dashers, the contractors who make deliveries. It retrieves knowledge base articles and is monitored by two further systems that the post calls LLM Guardrail and LLM Judge. [13]

The problem

The earlier automated support followed pre-built resolution paths, so it could resolve only a small subset of issues quickly. A knowledge base of articles existed, but the post lists three limits on its usefulness: the right article was hard to find, finding the useful part of an article took time, and the articles were in English while many Dashers prefer another language.

Once an LLM chatbot answered from those articles, a new problem appeared. Some replies sounded natural but diverged from the retrieved context. The team attributes this to outdated or incorrect information about DoorDash that the model absorbed from public text during training.

Where this topic fits

RAG is the core of the chatbot: summarise the conversation, retrieve articles, generate a reply. This example is mostly about what comes after generation. Every reply is checked for grounding against the retrieved articles before a Dasher sees it, and transcripts are scored afterwards to find where retrieval or generation is failing.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A Dasher describes an issue, often across several messages. The system first condenses the conversation into a summary of the core problem, because the summary is what drives retrieval.
  2. Using the summary, it searches historical data for the top N similar cases that were resolved with knowledge base articles. Each matched case corresponds to an article.
  3. The articles are inserted into a prompt template together with the conversation and the issue summary, and the model generates a reply.
  4. A first guardrail layer compares the reply with segments of the retrieved articles for semantic similarity, using a sliding window. A reply that closely matches an article is unlikely to be invented.
  5. If the first layer flags the reply, a second layer sends the reply, the articles and the conversation history to an evaluator model, which judges whether the reply is grounded and also checks coherence and compliance.
  6. A reply that fails is not shown. Because problematic replies are few, the team finds that defaulting to a human agent keeps quality high while most conversations stay automated.
  7. Separately, an LLM Judge scores transcripts on five aspects, including retrieval correctness and response accuracy, and the findings feed fixes to the knowledge base, retrieval and prompts.

Why this approach fits

Support answers come from policy articles that change, exist in one language and are too many to paste into a prompt, which is the standard case for retrieval. The stakes explain the extra layer. A wrong but confident answer about pay or account status causes real harm, and the model's own memory of public forum posts about DoorDash is exactly the source the team does not want used. Comparing the reply with the retrieved text is a direct test of grounding, and running the cheap comparison first keeps the expensive evaluator for the few replies that need it.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post names latency as the guardrail's drawback: generating, checking and possibly retrying takes time, which is one reason failing replies go to people. The team also tried a more sophisticated guardrail model first and found the added response time and token use too expensive. A similarity check can pass a reply that reuses an article's words while changing its meaning, and can flag a correct paraphrase. The reported reductions are the team's own figures, and the post does not give the rates they started from.

Claude citations: answers returned with the exact supporting passage, as used by CoCounsel and Endex

Where this topic fits

The feature sits in the generate stage. Retrieval remains yours: your pipeline finds the chunks. You pass them to the API as search result blocks, and the response comes back already divided into statements with their supporting passages attached. What follows is display, for example footnotes or links.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Your retriever returns chunks. Each one is wrapped in a search_result block with a source (a URL or an internal identifier), a title and one or more text blocks, and citations are enabled on it.
  2. The blocks are sent in the user message with the question. They can also be returned by a tool that the model calls, which the documentation describes as the route for dynamic RAG applications.
  3. The reply is a list of text blocks. A block that draws on a search result carries a citations list, and a block of connecting words carries none.
  4. Each citation gives the source and title you supplied, the index of the search result, the range of content blocks cited, and cited_text, which is the exact text of those blocks.
  5. The application renders each cited statement with a link built from the source and can show cited_text beside it, so that a reader can compare claim and evidence. This rendering is yours to build.
  6. For whole files, a document block with citations enabled is split into sentences by the API, and citations give character ranges for plain text or page numbers for PDFs.

Why this approach fits

A prompted marker is text the model writes, so it can be malformed, missing or invented. Here the API parses the model's output into structured citations and copies cited_text from the content you supplied, which is why the documentation can guarantee that every pointer is valid. Because the search result block carries your own source and title, the citation arrives ready to render. The quoted text is not billed as output tokens, so asking for evidence does not make answers more expensive to generate.

Citations cannot be combined with structured outputs, and the documentation says such a request returns a 400 error. Dropbox Dash: a lexical index with query-time chunking and reranking to meet a latency budget

DropboxDocumented in an engineering article

Dropbox Dash is Dropbox's universal search and knowledge management product for businesses. The Dropbox engineering post Building Dash: How RAG and AI agents help us meet the needs of businesses (April 2025) explains how the team chose its retrieval system. [14]

The problem

Business content is spread across many applications, comes in many types (emails, documents, meeting notes, task data) and several modalities, and keeps changing. The post frames retrieval design as three tradeoffs: latency against quality, data freshness against scalability, and budget against user experience. Retrieval had to be fast enough to leave time for the rest of the pipeline, so that users do not give up while waiting for a response.

Where this topic fits

This is a decision about the retrieve stage and, through it, about what the index stage has to store. The post says the retrieval system bounds what the model can know at answer time and strongly affects latency. Generation follows, with a model that the team keeps interchangeable.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Content from connected applications is kept reasonably fresh with periodic syncs and, where appropriate, webhooks.
  2. The team weighed the common design, a vector index over pre-chunked content searched semantically, and alternatives such as calling each source's API at question time, against the three tradeoffs.
  3. It chose a traditional information retrieval approach. A lexical search system, with rerankers that use embedding features, finds the candidate documents.
  4. Candidate documents are chunked at query time, so that only the relevant sections are pulled out.
  5. A larger but still efficient embedding model re-sorts the chunks so that the most relevant ones come first.
  6. The top chunks go to the language model, which generates the answer. The team measures quality end to end, because all parts of the RAG system have to work together.

Why this approach fits

The textbook pipeline embeds every chunk ahead of time. For a product that indexes each customer's constantly changing content from many applications, that means chunking and embedding again on every edit. Indexing documents by their words is cheaper to keep fresh, and chunking only the handful of candidate documents at query time moves the expensive work to where it is needed. Reranking with embeddings restores the ability to match on meaning. The example shows that the index stage means any structure that makes retrieval fast and good enough. It does not have to be a vector database.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post itself notes that a lexical index adds latency at serving time for on-the-fly chunking and reranking, and that a tight latency target can force smaller embedding models that reduce retrieval accuracy. A lexical first stage can also miss documents that use different words from the question before any reranker sees them. The team states that RAG answers questions that need information from one or more documents but cannot perform complex multi-step tasks, which is why Dash adds agents.

  • The post lists tradeoffs between retrieval options: latency versus quality, data freshness versus scalability, and budget versus user experience. [14]
  • Dash uses a traditional information retrieval approach: a lexical-based system with rerankers that use embedding features, on-the-fly chunking of documents at query time, and reranking by a larger but still efficient embedding model. [14]
  • The team reports high-quality results in under 2 seconds for over 95% of queries, and says data is kept reasonably fresh with periodic data syncs and webhooks. [14]
  • Retrieval methods and models were evaluated on Natural Questions, MuSiQue and Microsoft Machine Reading Comprehension, with LLM judges for answer correctness and completeness and with source precision, recall and F1. The post states that RAG is incapable of complex multi-step tasks. [14]

Microsoft's agriculture study: measuring what RAG and fine-tuning each add to the same model

RAG vs Fine-tuning: Pipelines, Tradeoffs, and a Case Study on Agriculture (arXiv, January 2024) is a paper whose authors are affiliated with Microsoft. It builds a RAG pipeline and a fine-tuning pipeline over the same agricultural documents and evaluates models with and without each. [15]

The problem

Farming advice depends on place. The paper's opening example shows GPT-4 giving the same answer about when to plant trees and shrubs for Arkansas, Connecticut and Georgia, because it does not bring in location-specific knowledge. That knowledge exists in government and university documents. The authors note that developers add such data to LLM applications in two ways, RAG and fine-tuning, and that the pros and cons of the two are not well understood.

Where this topic fits

RAG appears twice. It generates the answers used to build the question-and-answer dataset, and it is one of the two treatments being compared. Models are tested in up to four conditions: base, base with RAG, fine-tuned, and fine-tuned with RAG.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The team collects agricultural documents. The United States set alone has more than 23,000 PDF files and over 50 million tokens, and the benchmark uses the Washington state subset of 573 files and over 2 million tokens.
  2. Text and document structure are extracted from the PDFs with GROBID, because plain PDF-to-text conversion loses structural markers such as section headers.
  3. Index: text chunks are embedded with sentence transformers and stored in a FAISS index.
  4. Retrieve: a question is embedded and the most similar snippets are fetched. After measuring recall at several values of k, the authors use the top 3.
  5. Generate: the question and the retrieved snippets go to the model in a custom prompt.
  6. Separately, models are fine-tuned on question-and-answer pairs generated from the same documents: Llama2-13B directly, and GPT-4 with low-rank adaptation.
  7. Base and fine-tuned models, with and without RAG, answer 273 human-curated Washington questions, and the answers are scored against a guideline written for each question.

Why this approach fits

Region-specific facts sit in documents, so retrieval can hand the model the right state's guidance at question time, and new guidance needs only re-indexing. Fine-tuning addresses a different gap. In the paper's comparison it gives more precise and more succinct answers and can teach a new skill in the domain. Because the two act on different weaknesses, their gains can add up, which is what the GPT-4 rows of the results show.

Limitation or tradeoff

The gains are not uniform. For Llama-2-chat 13B, adding RAG moved accuracy from 76% to 75%, within the reported margin of error. The fine-tuned Llama2 13B scored 68%, below the 76% of the off-the-shelf chat version, until RAG lifted it to 77%, so fine-tuning is not automatically an improvement. Recall at 3 fell from 81.5% to 75.0% as the index grew from 573 to 3,888 documents. The study covers one domain and models from 2023, and the abstract says GPT-4 was used to evaluate the results.

4Common mistakes

  1. Rewriting the prompt or swapping the model before looking at what was retrieved.

    Why it causes problems
    If the passage with the answer never reached the prompt, no instruction can produce it. Days go into prompt changes that cannot work, and each change risks breaking answers that were fine.
    How to recognise it
    Bug reports that include the question and the answer but not the retrieved chunks, and prompt edits that change the wording of wrong answers without making them right.
    How to fix it
    Log the retrieved chunk ids and scores with every answer. For each failure, check whether the answer is in the chunks, label it as a retrieval or a generation failure, and fix the stage that failed.

  2. Shipping a pipeline that has no not-found path.

    Why it causes problems
    The retriever returns k chunks for every question, including questions your documents never cover. Given unrelated passages and no instruction for that case, the model falls back on its training data and answers with the same confidence as a grounded reply.
    How to recognise it
    Ask five questions that you know are outside the knowledge base. If any of them gets a specific answer, the path is missing.
    How to fix it
    Add a not-found rule with an exact reply to the prompt, skip the model when the best retrieval score is below a threshold tuned on real questions, and keep out-of-scope questions in your test set.

  3. Displaying citations that nothing has checked.

    Why it causes problems
    A footnote makes an answer look verified. With prompted markers the model can cite a chunk it was never given, or one that does not support the sentence, and readers trust the footnote more than the text.
    How to recognise it
    Click through a sample of citations. Look for identifiers that are not in the retrieved set, links that open the wrong document, and cited passages that do not contain the number or name in the claim.
    How to fix it
    Reject or regenerate replies whose markers are missing or unknown, as the worked example does. Prefer citations produced by the API where they are available, and add a support check for answers where a wrong claim is costly.

  4. Building the index once and treating it as finished.

    Why it causes problems
    The index is a copy of your documents. When a policy changes, the old chunk stays retrievable, and the model either repeats the outdated rule or blends the old and new versions into an answer that neither document states.
    How to recognise it
    Answers that were right last quarter and are wrong now, and retrieved sets that contain two versions of the same document.
    How to fix it
    Re-index on every document change, delete the chunks of removed or replaced documents, and store a document date and version in the metadata so that stale chunks can be filtered out or flagged.

  5. Fine-tuning a model to teach it facts that keep changing.

    Why it causes problems
    Every change to the facts needs a new training run, the model cannot point to a source, and nothing forces it to prefer the new fact over what it knew before. OpenAI's guide classifies missing or out-of-date knowledge as a context problem, which retrieval solves.
    How to recognise it
    A plan that retrains whenever content changes, or a fine-tuned model that still gives last month's answer.
    How to fix it
    Keep changing facts in an index, or in the prompt when they are few. Reserve fine-tuning for behaviour that prompting cannot make consistent, and combine it with retrieval when you need both.

  6. Choosing k and the chunk size by feel.

    Why it causes problems
    With too few chunks the answer is often absent from the prompt. With too many, cost, latency and distraction rise, and the relevant passage can end up in the middle of a long prompt, where models use it least well.
    How to recognise it
    No labelled question set exists, and nobody on the team can state the system's recall at its current value of k.
    How to fix it
    Label a few dozen real questions with the passage that answers each, measure recall at several values of k, and pick the smallest k that meets your target. Repeat when the corpus or the retriever changes.

    ("How many days can I work from another country?", "hb-02"),
    ("How often can I work remotely?", "hb-01"),
    ("Does the company pay for my home internet?", "hb-03"),
    ("Is a VPN required when I travel?", "hb-05"),
    ("Should I take the train or fly?", "hb-04"),
    ("Which holidays apply to me?", "hb-06"),

    ("Will I be paid back for my broadband bill?", "hb-03"),

SYNONYMS = {"permission": "approval", "fly": "flights", "broadband": "internet", "bill": "costs"}


def expand(question):
    extra = [SYNONYMS[word] for word in question.lower().strip("?").split() if word in SYNONYMS]
    return question + " " + " ".join(extra)


def rank_of(index, question, gold):
    """1-based rank of the gold chunk among chunks with a score above 0, or None if it is absent."""
    hits = [cid for score, cid in retrieve(index, question, k=len(HANDBOOK)) if score > 0]
    return hits.index(gold) + 1 if gold in hits else None


def recall_at(ranks, k):
    return sum(1 for rank in ranks if rank is not None and rank <= k) / len(ranks)


def report(label, index, rewrite=lambda question: question):
    ranks = [rank_of(index, rewrite(question), gold) for question, gold in EVAL_SET]
    print(label, "ranks:", ranks)
    for k in (1, 2, 3):
        print(f"  recall@{k} = {recall_at(ranks, k):.3f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    index = build_index(HANDBOOK)
    report("baseline", index)
    report("with synonyms", index, rewrite=expand)

RAG is cheaper by $1.68 a day, about $50 a month. To earn that, the team would have to build ingestion, an index and a retrieval test set, and accept retrieval failures such as the word-mismatch misses in the first exercise. With the whole handbook in the prompt, the passage with the answer is always present. The recommendation changes if the load or the material grows tenfold. At 2,000 questions a day the uncached bill is $289.80, and a 1,200-page handbook is 480,000 tokens, long enough that answer quality in such a prompt has to be tested before you rely on it.

Team B: RAG.

  • Corpus size: 30,000×400=12,000,00030{,}000 \times 400 = 12{,}000{,}00030,000×400=12,000,000 tokens, 12 times the window. Long context is not an option at any price.
  • RAG: 2,300 tokens per request and 2,300×5,000=11,500,0002{,}300 \times 5{,}000 = 11{,}500{,}0002,300×5,000=11,500,000 a day, or $34.50.

Daily edits rule out fine-tuning as a way to hold the facts, because each edit would need a training run. With RAG an edit only needs the changed pages to be re-indexed. The link requirement is met by storing each chunk's page URL as metadata and returning it as a citation. The recommendation would change only if the documentation shrank to something that fits the prompt, which is unlikely.

Team C: prompting first, then fine-tuning. The facts are already in the prompt, so no knowledge is missing. The errors are in tone and structure, which OpenAI's guide places on the behaviour axis. Start with explicit style instructions and a few approved replies as examples. If the output is still inconsistent, the 2,000 approved replies are a ready training set for fine-tuning. A document index adds nothing here. One refinement keeps a retrieval step for a different purpose: fetch the approved replies most similar to the current ticket and use them as the examples in the prompt. The recommendation changes if the replies turn out to be factually wrong after all, which would make it a context problem.

Team D: a batch extraction job, not top-k RAG.

  • Corpus size: 4,000×25×400=40,000,0004{,}000 \times 25 \times 400 = 40{,}000{,}0004,000×25×400=40,000,000 tokens.
  • A top-5 retrieval returns chunks from at most 5 contracts, which is 5/4,000=0.125%5 / 4{,}000 = 0.125\%5/4,000=0.125% of them. The model cannot count what it never sees.

Run one request per contract that asks a fixed question (does this contract contain an auto-renewal clause, and where?) and store the answers in a table. Each request reads 25×400+300=10,30025 \times 400 + 300 = 10{,}30025×400+300=10,300 tokens, so the whole pass reads 10,300×4,000=41,200,00010{,}300 \times 4{,}000 = 41{,}200{,}00010,300×4,000=41,200,000 tokens, about $123.60 once. The count is then a database query, and each new contract costs one more request. RAG becomes the right tool for this team when the questions change to ones that a few passages can answer, such as "what is the notice period in the Acme contract?".

Check your answer

  • Did you get 48,300 tokens per request and 28.98 dollars a day for team A without caching, and 3.06 dollars with it?
  • Did you find that team B's corpus is 12 times the window, and that RAG sends 2,300 tokens whatever the corpus size?
  • Did you classify team C as a behaviour problem and avoid proposing a document index for it?
  • Did you see that team D's question cannot be answered from 5 chunks, and cost the one-off pass at about 123.60 dollars?

Chosen for accuracy and usefulness, not search rank. Time figures are estimates.

  • Introducing Contextual Retrieval (opens in a new tab)

    Anthropic

    ArticleIntermediateFreeAbout 25 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A compact primer on the standard pipeline with embeddings and BM25, the rule of thumb for skipping RAG when the knowledge base fits in the prompt, and measurements of how adding context to chunks and reranking reduce retrieval failures.
    When to use it
    Right after this lesson, before you index real documents. It shows what the first round of retrieval problems looks like and what fixes them.

  • Optimizing LLM Accuracy (opens in a new tab)

    OpenAI

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 30 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A mental model that separates context problems from behaviour problems, the two areas where RAG breaks down, and a worked case in which prompting, fine-tuning and RAG are applied in turn and measured.
    When to use it
    When you have a real accuracy problem and need to decide whether to change the prompt, add retrieval or fine-tune.

  • Citations (opens in a new tab)

    Anthropic

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 20 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The exact request and response formats for API-level citations, how documents are chunked for citing, what each citation type returns, and the feature's costs and incompatibilities.
    When to use it
    When you move from prompted citation markers to citations that the API produces and guarantees to be valid pointers.

    DocumentationBeginnerFreeAbout 10 minutes (estimate)

    When to use it
    When you want to see the same three stages offered as a hosted service, or are deciding whether to build the pipeline or buy it.

    When to use it
    When you want to know where the idea and its vocabulary come from. It is not needed for building a first system.
