Regression Metrics

Regression metrics are numbers that summarise how far a model's numeric predictions are from the true values. MAE, MSE, RMSE, R² and MAPE each average the errors in a different way, so each one punishes a different kind of mistake.

1Overview

Why it matters

A regression model outputs numbers: a delivery time, a house price, next week's demand. "It looks close" is not something you can ship. You need one figure to compare two candidate models, to decide whether a model is good enough to launch, and to watch once it is live. The catch is that the choice of figure is itself a decision. Two models can swap places when you switch from MAE to RMSE, as the worked example in this lesson shows, and the one you pick is the one your team will then optimise for months.

Most failures here are quiet. An MAE of 4 minutes is reported with pride although always predicting the average scores 5. One corrupted row makes RMSE jump and a good model is thrown out. MAPE explodes because a few actual values are close to zero. R² is 0.9 while the residuals show that every large prediction is too low. A model is tuned for squared error although the business loses three times as much on a late prediction as on an early one. ML engineers, analysts and anyone who signs off a forecast need to be able to read these numbers, question them and choose the one that matches what an error really costs.

Key terms

Residual
The actual value minus the predicted value for one row. Positive means the model predicted too low.
Absolute error
The size of a residual with its sign removed, so that a miss of 3 above and a miss of 3 below both count as 3.
MAE (mean absolute error)
The average of the absolute errors, in the same units as the target. It answers "how far off is a typical prediction?".
MSE (mean squared error)
The average of the squared residuals, in squared units of the target. Squaring makes large misses count far more than small ones.
RMSE (root mean squared error)
The square root of MSE, which brings the squared-error measure back to the units of the target.
R² (coefficient of determination)
One minus the model's squared error divided by the squared error of always predicting the mean. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the mean, and it can be negative.
MAPE (mean absolute percentage error)
The average of each absolute error divided by its actual value, expressed as a percentage.
Naive baseline
A rule that predicts without looking at the features, such as always predicting the training mean or median. It sets the score a real model has to beat.
Bias (mean error)
The average of the signed residuals. A value far from zero means the model is too low or too high on average.
Outlier
A row whose residual is far larger than the rest, because of a data error or a rare real event.

2Explanation

Introduction

In the linear regression lesson you met the residual: the actual value minus the predicted value for one row. A model that predicts delivery times leaves one residual per delivery, and a test set leaves hundreds or thousands of them. Nobody can compare two models by reading two long lists of residuals, so each list is boiled down to one number. That number is a regression metric.

The boiling-down is where the choices hide. Take three deliveries where a model was off by 1, 1 and 10 minutes.

  • Average the sizes of the misses: (1+1+10)/3=4(1 + 1 + 10) / 3 = 4(1+1+10)/3=4 minutes. This is the mean absolute error (MAE).
  • Square each miss, average the squares, then take the square root: (1+1+100)/3=5.83\sqrt{(1 + 1 + 100) / 3} = 5.83(1+1+100)/3​=5.83 minutes. This is the root mean squared error (RMSE).

Both are honest summaries of the same three predictions. They disagree because squaring lets the 10-minute miss supply 100 of the 102 points, while the plain average lets it supply 10 of 12.

Every metric in this lesson is built the same way: compute a residual for each row, turn it into a penalty, then average the penalties. MAE uses the absolute value. MSE and RMSE use the square. MAPE divides by the actual value to get a percentage. R² rescales the squared error against a model that always predicts the mean. A metric is therefore a statement about which mistakes you mind most, in the way a teacher decides whether one disastrous exam should sink a grade or only pull the average down.

The lesson covers the five standard metrics and what each one rewards, why a score means little until it is set beside a naive baseline, how to look past the single number at the residuals themselves, how outliers move each metric, and how to pick the metric whose penalties resemble the real cost of being wrong. Compute every metric on data the model did not train on, as described in train/test splits and cross-validation.

The penalty also decides which single guess is best. MAPE has no units and does not change if the target is converted from dollars to euros, so it suits targets that span scales, such as prices. It has three traps.

  • Small actual values. A 6-minute miss on a 12-minute delivery is 50%; the same miss on a 58-minute delivery is 10%. At yi=0y_i = 0yi​=0 the term is undefined, and Google Cloud's AutoML evaluation report (opens in a new tab) leaves MAPE out whenever the target column contains a zero.
  • Lopsided penalties. Predicting 150 when the truth is 100 costs 50%; predicting 100 when the truth is 150 costs 33%. With positive targets and predictions, guessing too low can never cost more than 100%, while guessing too high has no ceiling. MAPE therefore favours models that guess low, as the table above shows.
  • It needs a true zero. A percentage of a temperature in °C means nothing.

In scikit-learn 1.9 the functions are mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, root_mean_squared_error, r2_score, mean_absolute_percentage_error and median_absolute_error, each called as metric(y_true, y_pred). The MAPE function returns a fraction (0.106, not 10.6).

Compare against a naive baseline

"MAE = 4 minutes" is neither good nor bad until you know what a rule with no intelligence scores on the same rows. The standard baselines ignore the features: always predict the training mean (the best constant for squared error) or the training median (the best constant for absolute error). scikit-learn ships them as DummyRegressor (opens in a new tab), described there as a simple baseline to compare with real regressors. For data ordered in time the natural baseline is the last observed value, covered in time series forecasting.

State the comparison as a ratio so that it travels:

skill=1−metricmodelmetricbaseline\text{skill} = 1 - \frac{\text{metric}_{\text{model}}}{\text{metric}_{\text{baseline}}}skill=1−metricbaseline​metricmodel​​

A skill of 0.6 means the model removed 60% of the baseline's error. R2R^2R2 is this formula with MSE as the metric and the mean as the baseline. If there is an incumbent, such as last quarter's model or a hand-written rule, score that too: the gap to the incumbent is what a launch decision rests on.

Residual analysis: look under the average

A metric compresses the residuals; the residuals themselves show where the model is wrong. Four checks cover most problems.

  1. Mean residual (bias). A value far from zero means the model is too low or too high on average. Absolute and squared metrics cannot show direction.
  2. Residuals against predictions. A flat, even band is the goal. A slope means the predictions are squeezed or stretched, a curve means a term is missing, and a funnel means errors grow with the prediction. scikit-learn draws this plot with PredictionErrorDisplay.
  3. Residuals by segment. Compute the metric per region, product or hour of day. A fine overall score can hide one group the model serves badly.
  4. The largest residuals, row by row. They are either data errors to fix or the cases the business most needs to hear about.

The NIST handbook (opens in a new tab) calls graphical residual analysis the primary validation tool for most process modeling applications, because a numerical summary focuses on one aspect of the fit and squeezes it into a single number.

Outliers

Squared-error metrics react sharply to a single large residual, and R2R^2R2 moves with them. MAE reacts in proportion. The median absolute error, the middle value of the absolute errors, barely reacts at all. None of these is the correct behaviour in general. If the extreme row is a logging error, a robust metric keeps it from deciding your model choice. If it is a real event that hurts customers, RMSE is doing its job by shouting about it. The same holds in training: Google's ML Crash Course (opens in a new tab) shows a line fitted with MSE moving toward the outliers while one fitted with MAE stays with the bulk of the data. Google Cloud's AutoML exposes the choice as a setting (opens in a new tab): RMSE to capture extreme values accurately, MAE to treat them as outliers with less impact, and RMSLE (RMSE on logarithms) to penalise relative error.

Match the metric to the cost of error

Ask three questions about what a wrong prediction costs.

  • Does the cost grow in proportion to the error, or faster? In proportion: MAE. Faster, so that one large miss is worse than many small ones: RMSE.
  • Is the damage absolute or relative? If 10 units off matters the same everywhere, use MAE or RMSE. If 10% off matters the same everywhere, use a percentage metric and check that the target stays well away from zero.
  • Is too high as bad as too low? If not, no symmetric metric fits. Use the pinball loss, which charges α\alphaα per unit of under-prediction and 1−α1 - \alpha1−α per unit of over-prediction:

Set α=cunder/(cunder+cover)\alpha = c_{\text{under}} / (c_{\text{under}} + c_{\text{over}})α=cunder​/(cunder​+cover​), where cunderc_{\text{under}}cunder​ is the cost of predicting one unit too low and coverc_{\text{over}}cover​ the cost of one unit too high. The scikit-learn guide gives the general recipe: decide which property of the outcome you need (the mean, the median or a quantile), then use the scoring function that matches it for both training and evaluation. Training on squared error and judging on MAE, or the reverse, asks the model for one thing and grades it on another.

Step 2: all three predictors in code

The script uses only the Python standard library.

from math import sqrt
from statistics import mean, median

actual  = [12, 18, 22, 25, 30, 35, 40, 58]   # minutes, held-out deliveries
model_a = [14, 17, 24, 24, 32, 33, 41, 38]
model_b = [18, 23, 26, 27, 29, 32, 35, 49]
baseline = [28] * len(actual)                 # mean delivery time in the training data

def report(name, y, pred):
    res = [yi - pi for yi, pi in zip(y, pred)]             # residual = actual - predicted
    mae = mean(abs(e) for e in res)
    mse = mean(e * e for e in res)
    sst = sum((yi - mean(y)) ** 2 for yi in y)
    r2 = 1 - sum(e * e for e in res) / sst
    mape = mean(abs(e) / abs(yi) for e, yi in zip(res, y))
    medae = median(abs(e) for e in res)
    print(f"{name:9s} MAE={mae:6.3f}  RMSE={sqrt(mse):6.3f}  R2={r2:6.3f}  "
          f"MAPE={100 * mape:5.1f}%  MedAE={medae:4.1f}  bias={mean(res):+.3f}")

for name, pred in [("baseline", baseline), ("model A", model_a), ("model B", model_b)]:

Edge cases and limitations

Actual values at or near zero wreck MAPE
A product that sells 0 or 1 units on some days turns a miss of 2 units into an error of 200% or a division by zero. scikit-learn avoids the division by replacing a zero denominator with a tiny positive number, which returns an astronomically large score instead of an error message, and Google Cloud's AutoML evaluation omits MAPE when the target column contains a zero. If your target can be zero or close to it, use MAE or RMSE, or divide the total absolute error by the total of the actual values so that no single small row is a denominator.
The same model gets a different R² on a different test set
R² divides by the spread of the actual values being scored. Model B in the worked example has an RMSE of 4.96 and an R² of 0.87 on all eight deliveries. Score it on the first seven only and its RMSE improves to 4.07 while its R² drops to 0.80, because removing the 58-minute delivery shrank the spread of the targets more than it shrank the error. Compare R² values only when they were computed on the same rows, and never across datasets or business units.
Two different quantities are both called R²
scikit-learn's r2_score is one minus the squared error divided by the squared error of the mean, and it falls when predictions are biased or squeezed. Some tools report the square of the Pearson correlation between predictions and actual values instead; Google Cloud's AutoML evaluation page defines its r^2 that way, with a range of zero to one. Correlation ignores bias and scale. Model B in the worked example scores 0.87 on the first definition and 0.99 on the second, because its predictions are almost perfectly correlated with the truth and still systematically compressed. Check which definition a tool uses before comparing numbers.
One average over groups of very different size
A retailer that forecasts a product selling 5 units a day and another selling 5,000 gets an overall MAE and RMSE decided almost entirely by the big product, and an overall MAPE decided largely by the small one. Neither average describes both. Report the metric per group, or use an error scaled by each series' own naive-forecast error, such as the mean absolute scaled error described in Forecasting: Principles and Practice, where a value below 1 means the forecast beats the average one-step naive forecast on the training data.
Log-scale metrics are lopsided and need non-negative values
RMSLE compares the logarithm of one plus the prediction with the logarithm of one plus the actual value, so it measures relative error and suits targets that grow exponentially. The scikit-learn guide and Google Cloud's documentation both note that it penalises under-prediction more heavily than over-prediction: for the same miss in units, predicting too low costs more than predicting too high. Google returns the metric only when every label and prediction is non-negative. Do not read an RMSLE as if it were in the target's units.
A metric from a small test set is itself uncertain
In the worked example one delivery out of eight moved model A's RMSE from 1.65 to 7.24 and reversed the ranking of the two models. Real test sets are larger, but rare large errors still make squared-error metrics jumpy. Report the metric as a mean and a spread across cross-validation folds, or resample the test rows and recompute it many times, and treat two models whose ranges overlap as tied.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • MAE: the cost of an error grows in proportion to its size, and you need a figure in the target's own units that non-specialists can check against experience.
  • RMSE: one large miss is worse than several small ones that add up to the same total, or the model is trained on squared error and you want training and evaluation to agree.
  • MAPE or a median percentage error: the target is strictly positive, stays far from zero and spans scales, as prices and electricity load do, and people think of its error as a percentage.
  • R²: you want a unit-free statement of how much better the model is than predicting the mean, for readers comparing models on one fixed test set.
  • Pinball loss or a cost in money: predicting too low and predicting too high have clearly different consequences.

Think twice when

  • MAPE when actual values can be zero or small, or when the target has no true zero, as with temperatures in °C or a profit that can be negative.
  • R² to compare models across datasets, time periods or business units whose targets have a different spread, or as the only evidence that a fit is sound.
  • RMSE as the headline when the test data are known to contain corrupted records you cannot remove, because a few bad rows will set the score.
  • Any single aggregate metric as the launch criterion when the model serves distinct segments; require the metric per segment as well.

A simpler alternative. Report the share of predictions that fall within a tolerance the business already uses, such as within 5 minutes or within 10% of the actual value. It needs no formula to explain and it resists outliers. It stops being enough when you must rank models whose misses mostly lie on the same side of the tolerance, or when the size of the misses outside it matters, because a miss by 11% and a miss by 80% count the same.

Key takeaways

  • Every regression metric is an average of per-row penalties, and the penalty is what differs: the absolute value for MAE, the square for MSE and RMSE, and the share of the actual value for MAPE.
  • RMSE is never below MAE, and a wide gap between them signals a few large errors; one outlier can decide RMSE and R² while barely moving the median absolute error.
  • R² is squared error measured against always predicting the mean, so it depends on the spread of the test targets, can be negative on held-out data and should not be compared across datasets.
  • MAPE breaks when actual values are near zero and charges more for guessing high than for guessing low.
  • A score means little without a naive baseline scored on the same rows, and it still has to be backed by the residuals: their mean, their plot against the predictions and their largest values.
  • Choose the metric from the cost of being wrong (linear or faster-growing, absolute or relative, symmetric or not) and use the same choice for training and for evaluation.

Example 1 of 5

Netflix Prize: RMSE on withheld ratings, read against Cinematch and a movie-average baseline

NetflixDocumented in a paper

The Netflix Prize was an open competition that Netflix launched in October 2006 to improve the accuracy of its Cinematch recommendation system. The cited paper, written by two Netflix employees for the KDD Cup and Workshop 2007, describes the dataset, the scoring rule and progress up to June 2007. [8]

The problem

Netflix wanted outside teams to predict more accurately how many stars a subscriber would give a film. A contest with thousands of entrants needs one number that can be computed automatically for every submission, a reference score that says what "better" means, and a way to stop entrants from tuning their systems to the scoring data.

Where this topic fits

RMSE is the scoring rule of the whole contest and, according to the paper, also how Netflix measured Cinematch internally. Contestants train on the released ratings and submit a predicted rating for every withheld user and film pair. The RMSE on one half of those pairs goes on the public leaderboard and the RMSE on the other half decides the prizes. Every score is read against two references: a naive predictor of movie averages, and the incumbent system.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Netflix withholds more than 3 million of the most recent ratings from the subscribers in the dataset as a qualifying set and releases the rest, with dates, as training data.
  2. A contestant predicts a rating for every withheld pair. The residual for one pair is the star rating the subscriber really gave minus the predicted rating.
  3. The residuals of a fixed, undisclosed half of the qualifying set (the quiz subset) are squared, averaged and square-rooted, and that RMSE is reported back and posted to the leaderboard.
  4. The RMSE on the other half (the test subset) is never shown to contestants and is the figure used to identify winners, so repeated submissions cannot be used to fit the prize data.
  5. Scores are interpreted against two references. Predicting each film's average rating is the naive baseline, and Cinematch, at 0.9514 on the quiz subset, is the incumbent to beat.
  6. The Grand Prize threshold is set as a further 10% reduction relative to Cinematch, a relative improvement over a baseline and not an absolute RMSE picked in isolation.

Why this approach fits

Star ratings are bounded between 1 and 5, so for a prediction inside that range no single residual can exceed 4 and RMSE cannot be hijacked by an extreme outlier the way it can on prices or durations. Within that range the square does useful work: confidently predicting 5 stars for a film the subscriber rates 1 is penalised 16 times as much as being one star off.

Reading the score against baselines is what makes it meaningful. The raw values, roughly 1.05 for movie averages and 0.95 for Cinematch, look close together. Stating progress as a percentage improvement over the incumbent turns them into a target every team can understand, and keeping half of the scoring data hidden protects the result from leaderboard overfitting.

Limitation or tradeoff

RMSE over all withheld ratings weighs every pair equally, including films a subscriber would never be shown. A recommender is used by showing a short ranked list, and a lower average rating error does not guarantee a better top of the list. The whole distance between the naive baseline and the prize target is also small in absolute terms: by our arithmetic a 10% improvement on 0.9514 is under a tenth of a star of RMSE. Whether subscribers notice that is a question the metric cannot answer.

  • The released data contained over 100 million ratings from over 480 thousand subscribers on nearly 18 thousand titles, on a scale of 1 to 5 stars, and over 3 million of the most recent ratings were withheld as the qualifying set. [8]
  • RMSE is computed automatically for a fixed but unknown half of the qualifying set (the quiz subset) and reported to the contestant and the leaderboard; the RMSE for the other half (the test subset) is not reported and is used to identify potential winners. [8]

US National Hurricane Center: mean absolute forecast errors scored beside a naive baseline model

National Hurricane Center (NOAA)Documented in official docs

The US National Hurricane Center (NHC) issues the official forecasts of position and maximum wind for tropical cyclones in the Atlantic and eastern North Pacific. Its Forecast Verification pages describe how those forecasts are scored after each season and which figures are reported as performance measures. [9][10][11][12][13]

The problem

NHC issues a forecast every six hours with projections from 12 to 120 hours ahead. It has to state how accurate those forecasts are, show whether they are improving over decades, and avoid mistaking an easy season for better forecasting. The verification pages stress that annual errors vary significantly from year to year because the storms themselves differ.

Where this topic fits

The metrics are applied at the end of each season. Every forecast is compared with the "best track", NHC's post-storm analysis of where the cyclone was and how strong it was. Errors are averaged by lead time, the same cases are scored for simple baseline models, and annual averages at 48 hours are published as the centre's official performance measures.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. After the season, analysts build the best track: six-hourly estimates of each cyclone's centre and maximum sustained wind from all available data. This plays the role of the actual value.
  2. For every forecast and lead time, a track error is computed as the great-circle distance between the forecast and best-track positions, and an intensity error as the absolute difference in wind speed. Both are absolute errors, so their averages are MAEs in nautical miles and knots.
  3. A forecast is included only if the system was a tropical or subtropical cyclone at both the forecast time and the verifying time, which fixes the population being scored.
  4. A skill-baseline model built on climatology and persistence (CLIPER5 for track, the SHIFOR family for intensity) is verified on exactly the same cases, a homogeneous comparison.
  5. The official error is then judged against the baseline error on that shared sample and expressed as a skill score: the percentage by which the forecast's error is below the baseline's. NHC's verification reports use CLIPER5 as the track baseline and Decay-SHIFOR5 as the intensity baseline.
  6. Because one season is noisy, errors are pooled over five years to describe current accuracy. The circles of the forecast cone are sized so that two-thirds of the official errors in that five-year sample fall inside them.
  7. The annual average 48-hour track and intensity errors for the Atlantic basin are reported as the centre's performance measures.

Why this approach fits

An average error in nautical miles or knots is something an emergency manager can use directly, and an absolute error treats a miss to the east and a miss to the west as equally wrong, which is what matters for a position.

The baseline answers the question a raw MAE cannot: was this a good year for forecasters or an easy year for forecasting? A model that knows only climatology and persistence does well on well-behaved storms and badly on erratic ones, so its error measures how hard the season was. Scoring both on identical cases stops the comparison from being decided by which storms each happened to cover.

Limitation or tradeoff

An absolute intensity error hides direction, so whether forecasts run too strong or too weak has to be read from a separate signed average. Means over a single season rest on few storms: NHC warns that annual average errors have departed from the long-term trend by as much as 50 nautical miles and that statistics for years with few storms may not be representative. A mean absolute error also gives a rare, very large miss no extra weight, although those cases can matter most to the people affected.

Uber DeepETA: an outlier-robust, asymmetric training loss with MAE as the acceptance metric

The post says it became the new production ETA model for global four-wheel ETA prediction, after Uber had used gradient-boosted tree ensembles (XGBoost) for several years. [15]

The problem

One model serves arrival times that are used to calculate fares, estimate pickup times, match riders to drivers and plan deliveries. The post explains that these uses need different kinds of point estimate (a mean for fares, a particular quantile elsewhere), that their data contain different proportions of outliers, and that being late can be worse than being early. The new model also had to beat the incumbent on mean absolute error, overall and within segments of the data.

Where this topic fits

Regression metrics appear in two places. In evaluation, MAE against the incumbent XGBoost model is the stated accuracy requirement, checked across segments. In training, the loss function is chosen so that the model's output is the kind of estimate each use needs. The quantity being predicted is itself a residual: the gap between the routing engine's ETA and what really happened.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The routing engine produces an ETA by summing traversal times for the road segments along the best path between two points.
  2. The machine learning model predicts the residual between that ETA and the observed outcome, and the correction is applied to the routing engine's estimate. Uber calls this ETA post-processing.
  3. Training uses an asymmetric Huber loss. Its delta parameter sets how far the penalty behaves like squared error before turning into absolute error, which limits the pull of outlier trips.
  4. Its omega parameter sets the relative cost of predicting too low against too high, so the output can be shifted toward the side where a miss is cheaper.
  5. The two parameters can be set for the estimate a use needs; a typical choice would be near-symmetric, outlier-controlled settings for a mean-like estimate and a clearly asymmetric omega for a quantile-like one. The post does not say which values Uber uses.
  6. Candidate models are compared with the incumbent on MAE, across all trips and by segment, such as delivery against ride trips and long against short trips.
  7. Differences between segments are handled in the model with bias adjustment layers, which the post reports improve MAE.

Why this approach fits

Trip durations typically have a long right tail, and the post says use cases differ in their share of outliers, so pure squared error would let a few extreme cases steer the model, while pure absolute error would deliver a median when fare calculation needs a mean. A loss with a dial for outlier sensitivity and a dial for asymmetry lets one architecture produce the estimate each product needs without a separate model design for each.

MAE as the acceptance metric is in units of time that product teams can reason about, and requiring it per segment stops an overall gain from hiding a regression on, say, delivery trips.

Limitation or tradeoff

The training loss and the acceptance metric are different functions. A model trained with strong asymmetry is biased on purpose and will look worse on symmetric MAE than a model trained for the median, so each variant has to be judged with a metric that matches its own target, such as pinball loss for a quantile. The loss also adds two hyperparameters that must be chosen per use case. The post gives neither the parameter values nor the MAE figures, so the size of the gain cannot be judged from it.

  • The stated requirements include returning an ETA within a few milliseconds at most and improving mean absolute error significantly over the incumbent XGBoost model, with accuracy measured by MAE across different segments of the data. [15]

CalTRACK: CV(RMSE) and adjusted R² as acceptance rules for building energy baseline models

CalTRACKDocumented in a specification

CalTRACK is a published, versioned set of methods for estimating the energy a building avoided using after an efficiency upgrade, from meter and weather data alone. Version 2.0 of the methods document names energy efficiency procurement, such as pay-for-performance programmes, as its primary use case. [16]

The problem

In these programmes the payment depends on savings that cannot be metered directly. Savings are the difference between what a regression model says the building would have used and what the meter recorded. The model is fitted to the building's usage before the upgrade, with heating and cooling degree days as features. If it fits a building poorly, the "savings" are mostly model error, and the rule for accepting a model has to work across thousands of buildings of very different sizes.

Where this topic fits

Metrics are used at three points. Adjusted R² picks one model per meter from a set of candidates. CV(RMSE), the RMSE divided by mean usage, decides whether that building's model is good enough to count in a portfolio, and it then feeds the formula for the uncertainty of the savings. Bias is reported separately at portfolio level.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. For each meter, candidate regressions of usage per day on heating degree days, cooling degree days or both are fitted, together with an intercept-only model that predicts the same usage every period.
  2. Candidates with a negative coefficient are discarded and, among the rest, the one with the highest adjusted R² is selected. The intercept-only model scores 0 by definition, so it acts as the naive baseline inside the candidate set.
  3. CV(RMSE) is computed for the selected model: the root of the summed squared errors over the number of periods minus the number of explanatory variables, divided by mean usage in the baseline period.
  4. The result is compared with a threshold. The document recommends 100% as the default for portfolio use cases and lets the procurer waive it.
  5. CV(RMSE) enters the fractional savings uncertainty of each site, which is aggregated across the portfolio and compared with a limit chosen by the procurer.
  6. Bias is reported as its own figure, because pooling many buildings shrinks random error and leaves systematic error untouched.

Why this approach fits

Dividing RMSE by the building's mean usage gives a relative error that can be compared between a small home and a large one, without dividing by each period's own value as MAPE does, so one near-zero period (an empty house in August) cannot blow the figure up. Squared error suits the purpose because a few badly predicted periods are exactly what makes a savings estimate unreliable.

Using adjusted R² only to choose among candidates for the same meter is the legitimate use of R²: every candidate is scored on the same data, so the spread of the target is the same for all of them, and the adjustment stops a model from winning merely by having an extra feature.

Limitation or tradeoff

A threshold of 100% is loose: a model passes even when its typical error is as large as the building's average usage. The rule works because random errors partly cancel across a large portfolio, which does nothing for the accuracy of a single building's savings and nothing for bias, as the document itself points out. R² also behaves oddly for buildings whose usage hardly responds to weather: such a meter can have a small CV(RMSE) and an adjusted R² near zero, a good baseline that R² alone would call useless.

4Common mistakes

  1. Reporting a metric with no baseline beside it.

    Why it causes problems
    A figure such as MAE = 4.4 minutes or R² = 0.85 does not say whether the model learned anything. If always predicting the training median already scores 5 minutes, the model has bought a 12% improvement for all its complexity, and a stakeholder who hears "4.4 minutes" will assume far more.
    How to recognise it
    The results table has one row per model and no row for a constant prediction, the previous model or the current manual rule.
    How to fix it
    Add a constant baseline (training mean and training median) and the incumbent to every results table, scored on the same rows, and quote the share of baseline error the model removes.

  2. Treating R² as an accuracy percentage and comparing it across datasets.

    Why it causes problems
    R² depends on how spread out the test targets are. The same RMSE scores high on a wide-ranging test set and low on a narrow one, so a team ends up favouring whichever market or month happened to have more varied targets. A high R² is also taken as proof of a sound fit while the residuals show a tilt.
    How to recognise it
    R² values from different regions, periods or products appear in one ranking, or a report gives R² with no error figure in real units.
    How to fix it
    Report RMSE or MAE in the target's units next to R², compare R² only on identical rows, and check whether the tool computes it from squared error or as a squared correlation.

  3. Using MAPE on a target that can be zero or small.

    Why it causes problems
    A handful of rows with tiny actual values produce errors of several hundred percent and dominate the average, or the metric is undefined. Model selection then rewards whichever model happens to guess low on those rows.
    How to recognise it
    MAPE is far larger than MAE divided by the mean actual value would suggest, it swings between cross-validation folds, or the library returns an enormous number. Sort the rows by percentage error and the top ones all have small actual values.
    How to fix it
    Switch to MAE or RMSE, or to total absolute error divided by total actual value. For many series of different scale use a scaled error such as MASE. Keep MAPE for strictly positive targets well away from zero.

  4. Optimising and reporting a symmetric metric when too high and too low cost different amounts.

    Why it causes problems
    A model that minimises RMSE or MAE aims at the mean or the median. If running out of stock costs three times what leftover stock costs, the cheapest prediction sits well above both, and the model with the best symmetric score can be the most expensive one to run. The second exercise has a forecast that loses on MAE and RMSE and still costs 42% less.
    How to recognise it
    Ask what one unit too high and one unit too low cost. If the answers differ by more than a small factor and the evaluation uses only MAE, RMSE or R², the metric does not match the decision.
    How to fix it
    Compute the cost in money on the test set, or use pinball loss with alpha set to the cost of under-prediction divided by the sum of both costs, and train a quantile model for that alpha.

  5. Accepting an aggregate score without looking at the residuals.

    Why it causes problems
    An average hides direction and location. Model B in the worked example has a bias of 0.1 minutes and the better RMSE, and it is still too high on every short delivery and too low on every long one. In production such a model fails the same customers every time.
    How to recognise it
    Plot residuals against predictions and compute the mean residual per segment. A slope, a curve, a funnel, or a segment whose mean residual is far from zero is a fault the headline metric did not reveal.
    How to fix it
    Make three residual views part of every evaluation: the mean residual, the plot against predictions, and the metric by segment. Read the ten largest residuals row by row before choosing a model.

  6. Computing the metric on a transformed target and reporting it as if it were in real units.

    Why it causes problems
    A model trained on the logarithm of price, or on a standardised target, produces residuals in log units or standard deviations. An RMSE of 0.15 on log price means typical errors of around 15% of the price, not 15 cents, and comparing it with another model's RMSE in dollars is meaningless.
    How to recognise it
    The metric is implausibly small for the units claimed, or two models in one table were scored on different scales.
    How to fix it
    Convert predictions back to the original units before computing any reported metric, and name the scale explicitly when you do report a log-scale metric such as RMSLE.

from math import sqrt
from statistics import mean, median

rows = [  # (flat, furnished, actual rent, predicted rent)
    (1, "no", 900, 950), (2, "no", 1100, 1060), (3, "yes", 1250, 1150), (4, "no", 1300, 1340),
    (5, "yes", 1450, 1330), (6, "no", 1500, 1540), (7, "no", 1650, 1600), (8, "yes", 1800, 1690),
    (9, "no", 2000, 2050), (10, "yes", 4200, 3100),
]
TRAIN_MEAN, TRAIN_MEDIAN = 1500, 1400

def metrics(y, pred):
    res = [a - p for a, p in zip(y, pred)]
    sse = sum(e * e for e in res)
    sst = sum((a - mean(y)) ** 2 for a in y)
    return {
        "MAE": mean(abs(e) for e in res),
        "RMSE": sqrt(sse / len(y)),
        "R2": 1 - sse / sst,
        "MAPE%": 100 * mean(abs(e) / a for e, a in zip(res, y)),
        "MedAE": median(abs(e) for e in res),
        "bias": mean(res),
    }

def show(name, y, pred):
    m = metrics(y, pred)
    print(f"{name:14s}" + "  ".join(f"{k}={v:8.2f}" for k, v in m.items()))

y = [r[2] for r in rows]
model = [r[3] for r in rows]
show("model", y, model)
show("train mean", y, [TRAIN_MEAN] * len(y))
show("train median", y, [TRAIN_MEDIAN] * len(y))

res = [a - p for a, p in zip(y, model)]
sse = sum(e * e for e in res)
worst = max(range(len(res)), key=lambda i: abs(res[i]))
print(f"largest residual: flat {rows[worst][0]}, {res[worst]:+d}, "
      f"{100 * res[worst] ** 2 / sse:.1f}% of squared error")

keep = [i for i in range(len(rows)) if i != worst]
show("model w/o it", [y[i] for i in keep], [model[i] for i in keep])

for flag in ("yes", "no"):
    group = [res[i] for i, r in enumerate(rows) if r[1] == flag]
    print(f"furnished={flag}: residuals {group}, mean {mean(group):+.1f}, median {median(group):+.1f}")

The outlier. RMSE (355) is more than twice MAE (170), the signature of one dominant error. Flat 10 alone supplies 96% of the squared error. Removing it cuts RMSE by 79% and MAE by 61%, and leaves the median absolute error at 50. Whether to remove it is not a statistical question: a $4,200 flat is a real listing, so it stays in the report, flagged.

The residuals by segment. All four furnished flats have positive residuals, and the three ordinary ones are low by $100 to $120. Six of the ten flats are unfurnished with small residuals of mixed sign, which is why the overall median absolute error looks healthy.

The headline. The pricing team describes a cost proportional to the size of the error, the same everywhere, with unusual cases handled by hand. That is MAE. Report MAE = $170 beside the baseline's $515, add the median absolute error ($50) and RMSE ($355) so that readers can see the errors are uneven, and state the furnished bias and the flat 10 miss explicitly. MAPE (6.9%) would be a fair second figure because rents are far from zero, but the team's cost statement is in dollars, not in percentages.

  2. For each forecast, total the loaves short (demand above forecast) and the loaves wasted (forecast above demand), and convert them to dollars for the week. Which forecast is cheaper?
  3. Work out the value of α\alphaα for which pinball loss is proportional to this cost, and say which quantile of demand the bakery should forecast.

Requirements

  • Show the weekly cost of each forecast split into lost sales and waste.
