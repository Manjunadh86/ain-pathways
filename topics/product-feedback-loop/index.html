From Field Work to Product (The Feedback Loop)

The product feedback loop is the routine by which what a forward deployed engineer sees at customers (failures, workarounds and repeated custom work) is written down as evidence, judged to be either one-off or reusable, sent to the team that owns the product in a form it can act on, and answered, so that the field learns what was decided.

1Overview

Why it matters

A forward deployed engineer sees the product used on real data, inside real organisations, before most of the product team does. PostHog's public handbook calls its forward deployed engineering team the fastest feedback loop between real customer usage and the roadmap. That position is only worth something if what is seen travels. Without a routine, one of three things happens: the same workaround is rebuilt at customer after customer, the product team hears about gaps as anecdotes it cannot size, or the field team quietly turns the product into a different product for each customer. Ramp's write-up on its own forward deployed engineers says that ad-hoc customisations pollute the codebase and reduce maintainability over time.

The skill has two halves. One is judgement: telling work that belongs to one customer from a need that several customers share, without generalising too early. The other is writing: turning an observation into a request that a product manager can rank, a designer can scope and an engineer can test. Anyone who sits between customers and a product team needs both: forward deployed engineers, solutions and support engineers, and the product managers who receive their reports. It matters most for AI products, where a gap may be in the model itself and the useful report names a pattern of failures, not one customer's bad day.

Before you start

  • The FDE Engagement LifecycleBeginner

What you will be able to do

  • Record a field observation as evidence that separates what was seen from what you think it means.
  • Group observations by underlying problem and count distinct customers instead of mentions.
  • Decide whether a piece of customer work should stay customer-specific, become a shared recipe, or become a product request.
  • Estimate with a simple payback calculation whether productising a capability repays the hours spent on workarounds.
  • Write a feedback item that is goal oriented, specific and actionable, with evidence, impact, workaround and acceptance criteria.
  • Close the loop by tracking the decision, telling the field and retiring the workaround once the product handles the case.

Key terms

Field observation
A fact seen at a customer, such as something that failed, was measured or was worked around, recorded with the date, the customer and the context.
Evidence log
A shared list of field observations, one entry each, that the whole field team can search and count.
Workaround
Anything done to get past a product gap, such as a script, a manual step or a custom configuration, together with the hours it cost.
One-off work
Work whose need depends on one customer's systems, vocabulary or policy and that other customers are not expected to share.
Reusable capability
A need several customers share, which can be stated without any one customer's terms and built once by the product team.
Distinct customer count
The number of different customers that hit a problem, counted once each however many times they report it.
Customer-specific adapter
Code or configuration that connects the product to one customer's systems and lives outside the product core, so it can be deleted when it is no longer needed.
Feedback item
A written request to a product team that states the customer goal, the evidence, the impact, the workaround and what done would look like.
Payback period
The number of quarters in which hours saved in the field repay the hours the product team spends building the capability.
Closing the loop
Telling the people who reported a problem what was decided, and removing the workaround once the product handles the case.

2Explanation

Introduction

Picture a waiter who hears "too salty" from one table. That is a remark. If the same waiter hears it from six tables over a week, it is a pattern, but the kitchen will only change the recipe if someone writes it down, counts the tables, names the dish and says what was wrong with it. And if the kitchen does change the recipe, the waiter needs to know, so that the apology stops.

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is in the waiter's position, with more at stake. They are embedded with customers, they see the product meet real data and real processes, and they are often the first to notice that something the product should do is missing. The engagement lifecycle ends each project with a summary of what the product team should learn. This lesson is about what happens to that learning: how it is recorded, how you tell customer-specific work from a reusable capability, how you write the request, and how the answer gets back to the field.

Three questions run through everything that follows. Is this a pattern or one customer's quirk? If it is a pattern, does it pay back to build it into the product, or is a shared recipe enough? And is the request written so that someone who was not there can decide what to do with it?

The diagram shows the whole loop. Observations go into an evidence log, are grouped by problem, and then take one of four routes. Only one of those routes is a product request, and getting the other three right is what keeps the field team from flooding the product team with noise or, the opposite failure, from quietly forking the product for each customer.

How it works

The loop has six moves: two produce evidence, three turn it into a decision and a request, and the last brings an answer back.

1. Capture observations while they are fresh

An observation is useful later only if it was written down when it happened and says enough to be counted and compared. Microsoft's engineering playbook for its Industry Solutions Engineering (ISE) teams recommends filing feedback on an ongoing basis instead of batching it up for the end of an engagement (Microsoft ISE (opens in a new tab)), and its guidance asks for what did not work, what was expected and what was tried (guidance (opens in a new tab)). One entry in a shared log is enough to start. Keep what you saw apart from what you think it means:

yaml
- date: 2026-07-09
  customer: B                  # spell it the same way every time
  phase: pilot
  goal: reviewers approve extracted invoices they have already checked
  observed: approval is one click per invoice
  workaround: macro tool that clicks approve for every invoice in a list
  workaround_hours: 8
  effect: slow                 # blocked | slow | annoying
  evidence: ticket-4812, screen recording
  interpretation: may need a bulk approve action

The interpretation line is a guess; the others are facts someone else can check.

2. Count distinct customers, not mentions

Group entries by the underlying problem, not by the words the customer used, then count how many different customers hit it. A customer that raises a problem four times is one customer with a loud voice. Four customers that each raise it once are a pattern.

GitLab's process for customer-facing staff shows the habit. A technical account manager first searches the existing issues, and only if nothing matches creates a new one; the customer's details go into a comment on that issue, so one issue remains the single source of truth about the need (GitLab (opens in a new tab)). The same post keeps the public comment generic (plan tier, number of users) and links to the private CRM record for the rest, which is how you share evidence without sharing a customer's identity.

3. Decide: customer-specific, shared recipe or product change

Not every repeated piece of work should become a product feature, and not every one-off should stay hidden. There are three outcomes, not two, plus a holding state.

OutcomeWhen it fitsWhere it lives
Keep it customer-specificOne customer, and the need depends on their systems, formats or policyAn adapter in the customer's repository
Share a recipeSeveral customers, but a pattern, template or example meets the need, or building it into the product would not pay backA shared store of templates and playbooks
Ask for a product changeThree or more distinct customers, stated in general terms, and it pays backThe product, built and maintained by the product team

The holding state is two distinct customers. You are not at the threshold, but a short heads-up costs little: give the product team the evidence so far and say that a third customer will make it a candidate.

PostHog's FDE handbook states the sequence: solve for this customer first, and generalise when the work gives evidence that something should scale. Where something built for one customer would help others, it is turned into an example, a template or a product improvement (PostHog (opens in a new tab)).

The reason to wait for evidence is that building a general capability early is expensive even when it is wrong. Martin Fowler lists four costs of building a feature before it is needed: build, delay to other work, carrying extra complexity, and repair when it does not match what is learned later (Yagni (opens in a new tab)). Programmers have a related heuristic, the rule of three: two similar pieces of code do not yet justify a shared procedure, and a third makes the shared shape clear (Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)). Applying it to customers is our adaptation, and three is a heuristic, not a law. It guards against generalising from two cases that only look alike.

The opposite error costs just as much. Ramp's post on its forward deployed engineers frames the choice as hacking something together for a customer or building something that hundreds or thousands of customers will use, and says ad-hoc customisations increase entropy and decrease maintainability (Ramp (opens in a new tab)). Red Hat makes the same point about forks: an organisation that diverges from upstream code becomes solely responsible for maintaining its version (Red Hat (opens in a new tab)). Every private change to a product carries that cost, and it grows with each release.

Keep one-off code out of the product core

The practical rule is never to edit the product itself for one customer. Put customer-specific work behind an extension point the product documents, in code that lives in the customer's own repository. When the product later ships the capability, you delete the adapter and nothing else changes.

4. Check that it pays back

The product team has to choose among many requests, so give it numbers. The one only you have is what the workaround costs in hours. A rough payback estimate for a capability that takes the product team EEE hours to build is:

S=D⋅cN⋅hˉ,payback (quarters)=ESS = D \cdot \frac{c}{N} \cdot \bar{h}, \qquad \text{payback (quarters)} = \frac{E}{S}S=D⋅Nc​⋅hˉ,payback (quarters)=SE​

Here DDD is the number of new deployments you expect next quarter, ccc is the number of distinct customers that hit the problem so far, NNN is the number of customers deployed so far, and hˉ\bar{h}hˉ is the mean workaround hours per affected customer. SSS is the hours you expect to save per quarter. The estimate ignores strategic value and the upkeep of the workarounds, which both favour building.

Product teams commonly rank candidates with a score such as RICE: reach times impact times confidence, divided by effort. Intercom's description scores impact from 0.25 to 3, confidence as 100, 80 or 50 percent, and effort in person-months (Intercom (opens in a new tab)). Your evidence feeds two inputs: reach (how many customers and users hit the problem) and confidence (how many distinct customers, and how solid the proof). The product team supplies effort and decides.

5. Write the request so the product team can act on it

Microsoft's playbook describes good feedback as goal oriented (it states what the customer is trying to accomplish), specific (it details the scenario or observation) and actionable (it includes the clarifying information needed to decide) (Microsoft ISE (opens in a new tab)). Shape Up asks for a single specific story that shows why the status quo fails, and says a problem without a solution is unshaped work (Shape Up (opens in a new tab)). Both point to the same template:

text
Title:         what is missing, in product terms (not the customer's name)
Customer goal: what the person is trying to accomplish
Evidence:      N distinct customers, with dates and links (private links for detail)
Scenario:      one specific story that shows why the current behaviour fails
Impact:        blocked or slow, who and how many, and what the workaround costs
Workaround:    what we do now, who maintains it, how it can break
Done means:    two to four observable acceptance criteria
Not in scope:  what you are deliberately not asking for
Urgency:       the date or event that sets the deadline, if there is one
WeakWhy the product team cannot actStronger
Customers want a bulk buttonA solution with no problem and no evidenceReviewers need to approve a checked batch in one action; three customers; 21 workaround hours
The model is bad at tablesNo pattern, no count, nothing to reproduceFails on tables that continue across pages; cases attached and grouped by sub-pattern

For a gap in a model, send the pattern with examples: an interview with OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering describes FDEs sending research a specific task pattern, not the customer's raw problem. Customer data in examples needs the customer's agreement (see Data Privacy and Compliance). An outage or an immediately blocking bug is not feedback; the Microsoft playbook sends those to a support ticket.

6. Close the loop

Write the decision next to the evidence: accepted, scheduled, declined with a reason, or waiting for a trigger such as a third customer. GitLab keeps the tracking issue current with labels and milestones. Microsoft's feedback items are internal and customers cannot track them, so the field has to relay the status (Microsoft ISE FAQ (opens in a new tab)).

When the capability ships, test it against the original scenario, move customers off the workaround, delete the adapter and mark the log entries resolved. Plan for that migration work: OpenAI's guide says the Responses API incorporates improvements based on developer feedback from the Assistants API beta, and that the Assistants API was sunset on 26 August 2026 (OpenAI (opens in a new tab)). Feedback that succeeds changes the product, and people who relied on the old behaviour have to move.

Worked example: Eleven log entries, five customers: which problems go to the product team?

This is a teaching scenario with invented data, built so that every number can be reproduced. A team has deployed an invoice-extraction service with a human review queue at five customers, A to E, and logged every workaround that cost more than an hour. The product team has estimated the build time for two candidate capabilities: 40 hours for a bulk approve action and 30 hours for a confidence-score export. The sales plan expects three new deployments next quarter.

Step 1: fix the rules before looking at results

  • A problem is a candidate when at least 3 distinct customers hit it. Two customers is an early signal. One is customer-specific.
  • A candidate becomes a product request if it pays back within 4 quarters. Otherwise it is shared as a recipe.
  • Payback uses the formula from the explanation, with D=3D = 3D=3 new deployments and N=5N = 5N=5 customers so far.

Step 2: run the log through the rules

The script holds the 11 log entries as CSV text, groups them by problem, counts distinct customers with a set and applies the rules from Step 1.

python
import csv
import io
from collections import defaultdict

LOG = """customer,problem,hours
A,bulk-approve,6
A,bulk-approve,2
B,bulk-approve,8
D,bulk-approve,5
C,export-confidence,3
D,export-confidence,4
E,export-confidence,3
B,rotated-pages,12
C,rotated-pages,10
A,fixed-width-export,14
E,po-field-alias,4
"""
CUSTOMERS_SO_FAR = 5      # N
NEW_DEPLOYMENTS = 3       # D, from the sales plan
MIN_DISTINCT = 3          # customers needed before a problem is a candidate
PAYBACK_LIMIT = 4         # quarters
BUILD_HOURS = {"bulk-approve": 40, "export-confidence": 30}   # product team's estimates

by_problem = defaultdict(list)
for row in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(LOG)):
    by_problem[row["problem"]].append(row)

def assess(problem, entries):
    customers = {e["customer"] for e in entries}
    hours = sum(int(e["hours"]) for e in entries)
    payback = None
    if len(customers) >= MIN_DISTINCT:
        saved = NEW_DEPLOYMENTS * (len(customers) / CUSTOMERS_SO_FAR) * (hours / len(customers))
        payback = BUILD_HOURS[problem] / saved
        decision = "product request" if payback <= PAYBACK_LIMIT else "shared recipe"
    elif len(customers) == 2:
        decision = "early signal"
    else:
        decision = "keep in adapter"
    return problem, len(entries), len(customers), hours, payback, decision

results = sorted((assess(p, e) for p, e in by_problem.items()), key=lambda r: (-r[2], -r[3], r[0]))
print(f"{'problem':<19}{'entries':>7}{'customers':>10}{'hours':>6}{'payback':>9}  decision")
for problem, entries, customers, hours, payback, decision in results:
    shown = "-" if payback is None else f"{payback:.2f}"
    print(f"{problem:<19}{entries:>7}{customers:>10}{hours:>6}{shown:>9}  {decision}")

  • bulk-approve has 4 entries but only 3 customers: customer A logged it twice, 6 hours for a script and 2 more when a product update broke it. The 21 hours over 3 customers average 7.0 hours each. Expected saving next quarter is S=3⋅35⋅7.0=12.6S = 3 \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot 7.0 = 12.6S=3⋅53​⋅7.0=12.6 hours, so payback is 40/12.6=3.1740 / 12.6 = 3.1740/12.6=3.17 quarters. That is inside the limit: send a product request.
  • export-confidence also has 3 customers, but its 10 hours average only 3.3 per customer. Expected saving is S=3⋅35⋅3.33=6.0S = 3 \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot 3.33 = 6.0S=3⋅53​⋅3.33=6.0 hours and payback is 30/6.0=5.030 / 6.0 = 5.030/6.0=5.0 quarters, outside the limit. Step 4: write the request for the winner

text
Title:         Approve many reviewed documents in one action
Customer goal: A reviewer who has checked a batch of extracted invoices approves it at once.
Evidence:      3 distinct customers (A, B, D), 4 log entries, links to each entry.
Scenario:      Each of the three built its own way to avoid one-click-per-invoice approval
               (6, 8 and 5 hours). Customer A's script broke after a product update (2 more hours).
Impact:        21 workaround hours so far. If the next 3 deployments hit it at the same rate
               (3 of 5 customers, 7.0 hours each), about 12.6 hours a quarter.
Workaround:    Per-customer scripts and macros that call the approve action in a loop.
Done means:    1. A reviewer can select a filtered set and approve it in one action.
               2. Every document still records who approved it and when.
               3. The largest batch seen in the field is confirmed with A, B and D and works.
Not in scope:  Approving without review; customer-specific approval rules.
Urgency:       Next deployment, in about a month; no contractual date.

The product team can now rank this, size it and test it. Compare it with the weak version, "customers want a bulk button", which has no goal, no count and no acceptance test.

Edge cases and limitations

One large customer can be strategic without being a pattern
A single customer may be owed a feature by contract or be too important to lose. Count it as one customer when deciding whether the need is reusable, but record the commitment openly. GitLab's priority labels include one for requests promised to a customer, so the obligation is visible instead of being hidden inside a demand count.
Your customers may not represent your users
Embedded engagements tend to involve large, complex customers, so a field log over-represents their needs. Five embedded customers are still five customers. Before calling a need general, check it against support tickets or product usage from customers you have not worked with.
Two cases that look alike can differ where it matters
A capability built from two examples can fit neither, because the part that differs turns out to be the part that matters. The sign is that the two customers disagree about defaults or edge cases. Write a recipe for each and wait for a third case, as the rule-of-three caveat about premature abstraction suggests.
Workarounds break when the product changes
In the worked example, customer A's script broke after a product update and cost two more hours. Workarounds that rely on undocumented behaviour fail without warning, and when a product replaces an interface the old one can disappear, as the Assistants API did. Build adapters on documented extension points, give them a test, and log their upkeep hours.
Customer data does not belong in a feedback item by default
Examples attached to feedback, such as documents, prompts and model outputs, can contain personal or confidential data. Use redacted or synthetic reproductions, or get the customer's written permission. GitLab keeps the public comment generic and puts the detail in a private CRM record for the same reason.
A decline is a valid answer
The product team may decide not to build. Record the reason and the trigger that would reopen the question, such as a third customer or a lower build estimate, and decide whether the workaround becomes a supported recipe in the meantime.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You deploy the same product at several customers, so the same gap can appear more than once and a shared log can show it.
  • Your team has repeated workaround or glue work that took hours at more than one customer.
  • A product team owns the area and can accept a request, so someone is there to receive and answer the feedback.
  • You find a gap in model behaviour that a research or product team could fix once for every customer.

Think twice when

  • The problem is an outage or an immediately blocking bug. Use the support or incident process; Microsoft's playbook sends these to a support ticket.
  • Only one customer has the need and it depends on their systems. Keep it in an adapter instead of asking for a product change.
  • Nobody on the product side owns the area. A request that nobody receives is a complaint, so build the shared recipe and write down the gap.

A simpler alternative. A shared spreadsheet with the columns from this lesson and a monthly thirty-minute review with the product manager, covering the three or four problems with the most distinct customers. Move to written feedback items when a problem reaches its third customer or when the product team asks for more detail than the log holds.

Key takeaways

  • Record field observations as dated, checkable facts with the workaround's cost in hours, and label your interpretation separately from what you saw.
  • Count distinct customers, not mentions; three distinct customers is the rule-of-three style threshold this lesson uses for treating a problem as a pattern.
  • Repeated work has three outcomes, which are a customer-specific adapter, a shared recipe or a product change, and choosing between them is the main judgement in the loop.
  • Never change the product core for one customer; keep customer-specific code in an adapter behind documented extension points so it can be deleted when the product catches up.
  • A feedback item the product team can act on states the customer goal, evidence, impact, workaround and observable acceptance criteria, and leaves out emotion and solution-only demands.
  • The loop is finished only when the decision is recorded, the field knows it, and the workaround is retired once the product handles the case.

Example 1 of 5

PostHog's forward deployed engineers solve for one customer first and generalise on evidence

PostHogDocumented in official docs

PostHog publishes a handbook for its forward deployed engineering team. The page on how the team works sets principles and an improvement loop, the page on working with customers sets the handover conditions and the line between an FDE engagement and support, and the page on working with product engineering says how product gaps are fed back to the teams that own them. [1][2][3]

The problem

A forward deployed engineer is asked to solve one customer's problem, but the same team sees patterns across customers. If it builds only for the customer in front of it, solutions and answers are rebuilt every time. If it generalises from a single case, it risks building something that fits nobody. The handbook sets out when and how work moves from one customer to something shared.

Where this topic fits

This is the classification step of the loop, applied at three moments: during delivery (use the product's own capabilities, solve for this customer first), at handover (generalisable work is moved to a shared store and follow-on work becomes explicit tasks) and in the continuing improvement loop that turns repeated questions into references, playbook entries and standards.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start from the product's own capabilities instead of bespoke engineering; the handbook says the simplest path the product already supports is usually the one the customer can maintain after the engagement.
  2. Solve the problem for this customer first, and note anything that looks likely to apply elsewhere.
  3. When the work produces evidence that something should scale, put it in a reusable form: an example, a template or a product improvement.
  4. At handover, check that a named person on the customer side has approved the deliverable, that follow-on work is written as explicit tasks, that open questions are answered in writing and that anything that generalised has moved to the shared store.
  5. When the work hits a bug or product gap, tell the team that owns that area and file an issue that says what broke, who hit it, what it blocked and how to reproduce it.
  6. Promote recurring knowledge in stages: a customer question becomes a topical reference, a pattern across customers becomes a lesson or playbook entry, and a hard-won rule becomes a standard.
  7. Keep the solution customer-specific where it has to be, while sharing what was learned from it.

Why this approach fits

Waiting for evidence protects against building the wrong general thing, while the three reusable forms mean a good idea does not have to wait in line for the product team. Staging the promotion of knowledge keeps the cost of sharing low enough that people do it. Putting the check at handover ties the decision to a moment every engagement reaches.

Limitation or tradeoff

The pages state principles. They do not say how many customers count as evidence, how product improvements are prioritised or what results the approach has produced, so the thresholds in this lesson are our heuristics and not PostHog's. The shared store is internal and cannot be inspected. A small team close to its product engineers can run this informally; a larger organisation needs the written intake that the next examples show.

What the sources document

  • The handbook says the team should stay close to product engineering because it is the fastest feedback loop between real customer usage and the roadmap, and its page on working with product engineering tells a forward deployed engineer who finds a bug or product gap to tell the owning small team and file an issue stating what broke, who hit it, what it blocked and how to reproduce it. [1][3]
  • Its stated approach is to solve for the current customer first and to generalise when the work gives evidence that something should scale; the solution can stay customer-specific, but the useful findings should not. [1]
  • Where something built for one customer would help others, the principle is to turn it into an example, a template or a product improvement. [1]
  • The improvement loop describes a customer question becoming a topical reference, a cross-customer pattern becoming a lesson or playbook entry, and a hard-won rule becoming a standard. [1]
  • The handover conditions include that follow-on work is captured as explicit tasks and that anything that generalised has been moved into a shared store, which the page calls fde-vault. [2]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

OpenAI's forward deployed engineers send research a specific task pattern, not the customer's raw problem

OpenAIDocumented in an engineering article

The newsletter Engineering Leadership published an interview with Colin Jarvis, described there as OpenAI's Global Head of Forward Deployed Engineering. The free part of the article describes a feedback loop to the product and research teams, using a code-migration project as its example. [4]

The problem

When the product is partly a model, a customer's failing case may point to a weakness in the model and not in the application around it. The article says the engineers do not simply send the exact customer problem back to research. Our reading is that a research team can act more easily on a failure described as a pattern it can reproduce than on a customer's context mixed in with the model behaviour.

Where this topic fits

This is the "write feedback the receiving team can act on" step, with a research or product team as the receiver instead of a platform group. It follows evaluation: the field team has found, in the project's own checks, a kind of task that the model keeps getting wrong.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. During a customer project, find through use and evaluation that the model behind the work keeps making simple mistakes on one kind of task.
  2. Resist forwarding the customer's problem as stated. Reduce it to the kind of task that fails.
  3. State the gap in terms research can use: the types of task the model struggles with, and what additional training data would help.
  4. Send it through the feedback loop that connects the field team to the product and research teams.
  5. Expect the benefit to reach every customer, not only the one whose project exposed the gap, because the model is shared.

Why this approach fits

A model is shared by every customer, so a fix made once helps all of them, which makes pattern-level feedback more valuable than a customer story. A pattern with examples can be reproduced and measured before and after the change, which a narrative cannot.

Limitation or tradeoff

The public part of the article gives one example sentence, not a record of how feedback is submitted, how often or what became of it. The rest of the article is for paying subscribers and was not read. The account is a newsletter's report of an interview, not an OpenAI document, and it does not say how customer cases are shared with research or how customer consent is handled.

What the sources document

  • The article is based on an interview with Colin Jarvis, who is identified as OpenAI's Global Head of Forward Deployed Engineering. [4]
  • It describes a feedback loop with the product and research teams, in which a project found that the model used for a migration kept making some simple mistakes. [4]
  • According to the article, the engineers do not send the exact customer problem back to research. They try to be as specific as possible, for example by naming the types of COBOL-to-Java tasks the model struggles with and asking for more training data on such examples. [4]

Example 3 of 5

Microsoft ISE records customer blockers as structured engineering feedback for Azure product groups

MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

Microsoft's Industry Solutions Engineering (ISE) team works side by side with customers on their technical problems. Its public Engineering Fundamentals Playbook has a section on engineering feedback, made up of an overview, guidance, examples and a FAQ, that explains how engineers record blockers found during customer engagements and pass them to Microsoft product groups. [5][6][7][8][9]

The problem

Engineers inside customer projects meet product limits (a missing feature, an undocumented version, a service that is not available in a region) at the moment they are most visible and most likely to be forgotten. Reported informally, each blocker arrives as a vague complaint from a different person, with no way to compare it with the same blocker on another engagement. The playbook sets out a format and a routine so that product groups receive items they can decide on.

Where this topic fits

This is the writing and routing part of the loop. It runs alongside delivery, not after it: engineers file items as blockers appear, a central feedback team triages and merges similar items, and the merged items go to the engineering teams that own each service. It is separate from support, which handles outages and immediately blocking bugs.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Note the blocker when it happens, while the details are fresh, instead of batching it up for the end of the engagement.
  2. Describe it with the three qualities the playbook asks for: the goal the customer was pursuing, the specific scenario or observation, and the clarifying information that lets someone make a decision.
  3. Classify it with a service, a type (business blocker, technical blocker, documentation or feature request), a stage, the impact, a time frame and a priority from P0 to P3.
  4. Add reproduction prerequisites and steps, and notes on what did not work, what was expected and what was tried.
  5. The feedback team triages the item, asks for clarification when needed, and may combine similar items from several engagements into one aggregate item for the product group.
  6. A monthly report of the highest-priority feedback goes to ISE and Microsoft leadership, and the submitter follows the item until it is addressed.

Why this approach fits

The fixed fields turn a story into something comparable. Type and priority let a triager sort a queue, the stage says whether production is affected, and the workaround shows what the limit costs. Because items follow one format and a central team merges similar ones, a product group sees an item backed by several engagements instead of one engineer's complaint.

Limitation or tradeoff

The FAQ says customers cannot follow the status of these items, so the field has to relay what happens next. The pages explain how items are triaged but not how a product group weighs them against its other work, and the examples record outcomes as followed up until addressed, not as measurements.

Example 5 of 5

Kubernetes Gateway API promotes a capability to the standard only after implementers back it and a trial period passes

KubernetesDocumented in official docs

The Kubernetes documentation describes the Ingress API, where behaviour beyond the standard fields was reached through controller-specific annotations, and its successor, the Gateway API. The Gateway API project's own site documents how a proposed capability is admitted to the standard API: an enhancement proposal, an Experimental release channel and written graduation criteria. [11][12][13][14]

The problem

Users of Ingress needed routing behaviour beyond the standard fields, such as matching on headers or splitting traffic by weight. Controllers offered such behaviour through their own annotations, so a configuration written for one controller could behave differently on another. A project that owns a shared API then has to decide which needs belong in the standard and which should stay specific to one implementation, because a field in the standard channel is meant to stay stable.

Where this topic fits

This is the reusable-capability end of the loop at the scale of an ecosystem. The per-controller annotations play the part of customer-specific workarounds, and the Gateway API project plays the product team deciding what to absorb. Its enhancement process gates that decision with evidence of interest beyond the requester, an experimental period and written criteria, the same shape as waiting for a third customer before asking for a product change.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Users have a need that the standard Ingress fields cannot express, such as header matching or traffic weighting, and each controller satisfies it with its own annotations, which it documents separately.
  2. The Gateway API defines the commonly needed behaviours as standard fields in a new API whose resource kinds are modelled on organisational roles.
  3. A new capability starts as an enhancement proposal. Before it is accepted as implementable, maintainers must agree it is in scope, a community member must act as its shepherd, and at least two implementations must say they are interested in building it.
  4. The change then ships in the Experimental channel, with written criteria for reaching the Standard channel (at least sufficient conformance testing) and a probationary period, six months by default.
  5. If the feature is widely used and working well it graduates to Standard. If the criteria are not met by the end of the probation it moves to Rejected and is removed from the experimental API definitions, and it can return with a new plan that the maintainers accept.
  6. Needs that stay specific to one implementation use the extensibility the API provides, since custom resources can be linked at various layers, so a one-off does not require a change to the standard.
  7. The Kubernetes project freezes the Ingress API, recommends Gateway for new work and says it has no plans to remove Ingress.
  8. A typical migration replaces annotations with the standard fields wherever the standard covers the case.

Why this approach fits

A need that several implementations each satisfied with their own annotation is a sign of a common capability, and a standard field lets every implementation offer it the same way, so configurations become portable. Requiring interest beyond the proposer, an experimental period and written criteria means a capability is promoted on evidence and not on a single request, and it can still be removed if use does not follow. The extension points keep room for needs that really are specific to one implementation, so the standard does not have to absorb every request.

Limitation or tradeoff

The Gateway API pages describe how the project gates its own proposals. They do not say how many users asked for each feature, and the two-implementation test counts software projects that would build a feature, not the end users who need it, so it is a different kind of evidence from a count of customers. Experimental features can be removed, so teams that adopt them accept some churn. The process suits a project with many independent implementers more than a single product team, and the standard covers only what the project agreed to include. Ingress stays generally available, so teams may run both models for some time.

What the sources document

  • The Ingress documentation says controllers frequently use annotations to configure behaviour, tells readers to check their controller's documentation for the annotations it supports, and adds that the various controllers operate slightly differently. [11]
  • The Kubernetes project recommends Gateway instead of Ingress and says the Ingress API has been frozen: it stays generally available with no plans for removal but will receive no further changes. [11]
  • The Gateway API documentation says its resources support common routing use cases such as header-based matching and traffic weighting, which were only possible in Ingress by using custom annotations, and that custom resources can be linked at various layers of the API. [12]
  • The enhancement-process page says that for a proposal to become implementable, maintainers must agree it is in scope, it needs a shepherd, and at least two implementations must be interested in implementing it, which it says ensures community interest outside the proposal's owner. [13]
  • Every new feature starts in the Experimental channel, where the page says it may graduate to Standard or be dropped. A proposal needs written graduation criteria (at minimum sufficient conformance testing) and a probationary period, six months by default, and one that misses its criteria moves to Rejected and is removed from the experimental definitions. [13][14]

4Common mistakes

  1. Counting every mention as separate demand

    Why it causes problems
    One vocal customer that raises a problem four times looks like four customers. The product team builds for a quirk, and a real pattern spread thinly over several customers is ranked lower.
    How to recognise it
    The evidence list in a request contains several entries with the same customer name, or the count changes when you remove duplicate customers.
    How to fix it
    Group by problem and count the set of customers. Show both numbers in the request, mentions and distinct customers, so the reader can see how the demand is spread.

  2. Sending a solution with no problem behind it

    Why it causes problems
    The product team cannot tell whether the proposed button or setting is the right design, cannot test whether it worked, and may build exactly what was asked while the customer's goal stays unmet.
    How to recognise it
    The title is a feature name, and the item has no customer goal, no scenario and no acceptance criteria.
    How to fix it
    Rewrite it with the template - goal, scenario, impact, workaround, done means. Keep your proposed solution if you have one, but label it as a suggestion and put it below the problem.

  3. Changing the shared product for one customer

    Why it causes problems
    A private change to the product core has to be carried through every release, and the cost lands on whoever made it. The next customer then receives a different product, and the codebase collects customer-specific special cases.
    How to recognise it
    Customer names or customer-specific conditions appear in the product's code or configuration, or deployments differ by patch.
    How to fix it
    Move the work into an adapter behind a documented extension point, in the customer's own repository. Ask the product team for the extension point you were missing, and delete the adapter when the capability ships.

  4. Generalising after the first customer

    Why it causes problems
    A general capability designed around one case builds in that case's accidents. The second and third customers need rework, so the build is paid for twice.
    How to recognise it
    The new feature has options that mirror one customer's field names, or a second customer asks for a variant soon after release.
    How to fix it
    Solve it for the customer, keep the work in an adapter and log the hours. At the second customer, write a recipe and send the product team an early signal; ask for the product change at the third distinct customer.

  5. Saving feedback for the end of the engagement

    Why it causes problems
    Exact steps, versions, error messages and the hours the workaround cost are forgotten. The product team receives a vague summary after the chance to reproduce the problem has passed.
    How to recognise it
    Log entries are written weeks after the events and lack dates, versions or hours, or the log is empty until the closing summary.
    How to fix it
    Write the entry on the day the problem occurs. Review the log weekly, and turn entries into feedback items when a problem reaches the threshold you agreed with the product team.

  6. Never closing the loop

    Why it causes problems
    The workaround stays in production after the product handles the case, customers keep paying for its upkeep, and the field keeps reporting a problem that was fixed. The product team then trusts the log less.
    How to recognise it
    Log entries stay open after a release that covers them, adapter repositories are still in use, or nobody in the field can say what happened to a request.
    How to fix it
    Record the decision beside the evidence. When the capability ships, test it against the original scenario, migrate the customers, delete the adapter, mark the entries resolved and tell the customers who reported the problem.

Exercise 1

Turn a field log into decisions with a small script

CodingBeginnerAbout 40 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

A field team keeps its workaround log as CSV. Write a Python script, using only the standard library, that groups the log by problem and decides what to do with each problem. The data is invented for this exercise.

text

Use these settings: 5 customers deployed so far, 2 new deployments expected next quarter, a threshold of 3 distinct customers, and a payback limit of 4 quarters. The product team's build estimates are 24 hours for tool-timeout and 20 hours for slack-alerts. Use the payback formula from the lesson: expected hours saved per quarter are the new deployments, times the affected share of customers, times the mean workaround hours per affected customer. Payback is the build estimate divided by the hours saved.

Requirements

  • Read the CSV text with the csv module and group the rows by problem.
  • For each problem, compute the number of mentions, the number of distinct customers, the total hours, and the first and last date.
  • With 3 or more distinct customers, compute the payback and choose "product request" when it is at most 4 quarters, otherwise "shared recipe". With 2 customers choose "early signal", and with 1 choose "keep in adapter".
  • Sort by distinct customers descending, then total hours descending, then problem name, and print one line per problem.

Expected behaviour

Five lines are printed. tool-timeout and slack-alerts both have 4 mentions but only 3 customers. tool-timeout has a payback of 3.33 quarters and is a product request. slack-alerts has a payback of 6.25 quarters and is a shared recipe. pdf-tables has 2 customers and 16 hours and is an early signal. sso-roles and audit-export are single-customer problems and stay in adapters.

Hint 1

Keep a set of customer names for each problem, so that a customer that appears twice is counted once.

Hint 2

Mean hours per affected customer is the total hours divided by the number of distinct customers, not by the number of mentions.

Hint 3

Only problems with 3 or more customers have a build estimate, so look up the estimate inside that branch and nowhere else.

Show solution
python
import csv
import io
from collections import defaultdict

RAW = """date,customer,problem,hours
2026-07-02,north,tool-timeout,5
2026-07-09,harbor,tool-timeout,4
2026-07-15,north,tool-timeout,3
2026-07-21,lumen,tool-timeout,6
2026-07-23,harbor,pdf-tables,9
2026-08-01,lumen,pdf-tables,7
2026-08-04,quill,sso-roles,12
2026-08-12,north,slack-alerts,2
2026-08-19,quill,slack-alerts,3
2026-08-27,ridge,slack-alerts,2
2026-09-02,ridge,slack-alerts,1
2026-09-05,north,audit-export,8
"""
CUSTOMERS_SO_FAR = 5
NEW_DEPLOYMENTS = 2
MIN_DISTINCT = 3
PAYBACK_LIMIT_QUARTERS = 4
EFFORT_HOURS = {"tool-timeout": 24, "slack-alerts": 20}

groups = defaultdict(list)
for row in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(RAW)):
    groups[row["problem"]].append(row)

def summarise(problem, rows):
    customers = {r["customer"] for r in rows}
    hours = sum(int(r["hours"]) for r in rows)
    dates = sorted(r["date"] for r in rows)
    payback = None
    if len(customers) >= MIN_DISTINCT:
        saved = NEW_DEPLOYMENTS * (len(customers) / CUSTOMERS_SO_FAR) * (hours / len(customers))
        payback = EFFORT_HOURS[problem] / saved
        action = "product request" if payback <= PAYBACK_LIMIT_QUARTERS else "shared recipe"
    elif len(customers) == 2:
        action = "early signal"
    else:
        action = "keep in adapter"
    return {"problem": problem, "mentions": len(rows), "customers": len(customers),
            "hours": hours, "first": dates[0], "last": dates[-1],
            "payback": payback, "action": action}

summaries = sorted(
    (summarise(problem, rows) for problem, rows in groups.items()),
    key=lambda s: (-s["customers"], -s["hours"], s["problem"]),
)
for s in summaries:
    payback = "n/a" if s["payback"] is None else f"{s['payback']:.2f}"
    print(f"{s['problem']:<14} mentions={s['mentions']} customers={s['customers']} "
          f"hours={s['hours']:>2} {s['first']}..{s['last']} payback={payback:<4}  {s['action']}")

pdf-tables     mentions=2 customers=2 hours=16 2026-07-23..2026-08-01 payback=n/a   early signal
sso-roles      mentions=1 customers=1 hours=12 2026-08-04..2026-08-04 payback=n/a   keep in adapter

The grouping is a dictionary from problem to rows. Counting customers with a set is what makes slack-alerts show 3 customers for 4 mentions. The saving for tool-timeout is 2 x (3/5) x (18/3) = 7.2 hours per quarter, so payback is 24 / 7.2 = 3.33 quarters.
