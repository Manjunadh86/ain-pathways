ROI and Measuring AI Impact

Measuring AI impact means comparing a business outcome after an AI feature is introduced with what that outcome would have been without it, starting from a baseline recorded before the change. Return on investment (ROI) then sets the value of that measured change against the full cost of building and running the feature.

1Overview

Why it matters

Every AI engagement reaches the same meeting: someone who controls a budget asks whether the feature is working and whether to pay for more of it. If nobody recorded how the work performed before the feature arrived, the only answers available are opinions and usage counts, and neither survives a finance review. A forward deployed engineer who took a baseline in the first week can answer with a number and say how it was obtained.

Intuition about AI impact is also unreliable, including the intuition of the people using the tool. In one randomised study, experienced developers believed AI tools had sped them up by 20% on tasks that the measurement showed had taken 19% longer (METR (opens in a new tab)). A UK cross-government trial reported 26 minutes saved per person per day from survey answers and could not say how that time was spent (Government Digital Service (opens in a new tab)).

Costs behave differently from ordinary software as well. An AI feature is billed per token, per seat or per outcome, so spend grows with adoption instead of being paid once. Anyone who proposes, builds or renews an AI feature needs to keep three things apart: what was measured, what was estimated from a measurement, and what was assumed.

What you will be able to do

  • Record a baseline for a workflow before an AI feature changes it, and list what the baseline must contain.
  • Link a feature to a business outcome through an outcome metric, a driver metric, guardrail metrics and adoption.
  • Build a cost model with one-off, fixed and per-use costs, and calculate the cost of one successful outcome.
  • Choose a comparison design and say what a randomised test, a staggered rollout and a before-and-after figure can each show.
  • Calculate net benefit, ROI, payback and a switching value, labelling every input as measured, estimated or assumed.
  • Recognise inflated impact claims built on self-reported time, unredeemed savings or users who chose the tool.

Key terms

Baseline
A record of how a workflow performs before a change, covering volume, time per unit, error rate and cost, taken so that later results have something to be compared with.
Counterfactual
What would have happened to the same people or cases if the feature had not been introduced. It cannot be observed, so it is approximated with a comparison group.
Outcome metric
A measure of finished work that the business already cares about, such as issues resolved per agent hour or cost per invoice processed.
Driver metric
A measure the feature changes directly and quickly, such as handling time for one step, which should lead to a change in the outcome metric but is not itself the outcome.
Guardrail metric
A measure that must not get worse while the driver metric improves, such as rework rate, escalations or customer satisfaction.
Difference-in-differences
An estimate of an effect found by taking the change in the group that received a feature and subtracting the change over the same period in a group that did not.
Cost per successful outcome
The cost of one attempt divided by the share of attempts that succeed, plus the cost of dealing with the attempts that fail.
Return on investment (ROI)
Benefit minus cost, divided by cost, over a stated period. An ROI of 73% over twelve months means each dollar spent returned the dollar plus 73 cents.
Switching value
The value an assumption would have to take for an investment to stop being worthwhile, used to show how much the result depends on that assumption.
Optimism bias
The documented tendency of people preparing a business case to underestimate costs and timescales and to overestimate benefits.

2Explanation

Introduction

Suppose a friend says a new route to work saves ten minutes. You would ask two things. Ten minutes compared with what? And did they time it, or does it just feel faster? The first question asks for a baseline, a record of how things performed before the change. The second asks whether the claim is measured or estimated. Those two questions are the core of this lesson.

They apply to every claim about an AI feature. In February 2024 Klarna published first-month results for its AI customer service assistant (Klarna (opens in a new tab)). The release said the assistant had held 2.3 million conversations, two thirds of the company's customer service chats, and that customers got their errands resolved in under 2 minutes where it had previously taken 11. It also said the assistant was "estimated to drive" a $40 million improvement in profit that year. The first statements are counts and timings set against a stated baseline. The last is a projection: a change multiplied by assumptions about volume, cost and the remaining months. Both kinds of number are useful, but they are different kinds of evidence, and a careful report does not blend them into one figure.

Return on investment (ROI) is the gain from an investment minus its cost, divided by its cost. For an AI feature the gain is the change in a business outcome that the feature caused, in money where possible. The cost covers building the feature, running it and checking its output. This lesson follows the order of the work on an engagement, which begins during customer discovery:

  1. Baseline: record how the workflow performs today, before anything changes.
  2. Metrics: pick an outcome the business already tracks, with the driver and guardrail metrics that connect the feature to it.
  3. Cost model: count one-off, fixed and per-use costs, and work out what one successful result costs.
  4. Comparison: introduce the feature in a way that leaves something to compare against.
  5. Return: calculate it, labelling each input as measured, estimated or assumed.

The loop closes because the first answer is provisional. Pilots run on volunteers and on novelty, prices and models change, and a saving counts only once the freed time or money has been put to use.

How it works

1. Take the baseline before anything changes

A baseline records how the workflow performs now: how many units of work arrive (tickets, invoices, claims), how long each takes, how often the result is wrong or redone, and what it costs. Take it from system records where you can, such as timestamps and queue exports, and from timed observation where you cannot. Cover enough time to include the normal cycle of the work, such as a month-end, and split it by case type, because an average over every kind of case hides where the feature will act.

The baseline also has to describe what would happen without your feature. UK Treasury appraisal guidance calls this business as usual: the outcome expected if current arrangements continue, which it notes is not the same as doing nothing (The Green Book (opens in a new tab)). The government's guidance on evaluating AI adds that the business-as-usual process should be documented precisely before the AI is introduced, and that baselines are hard to define where AI enables an activity nobody did before (Evaluation Task Force (opens in a new tab)).

A baseline cannot be recovered afterwards. The UK Department for Business and Trade's evaluation of Microsoft 365 Copilot states that no data was captured from participants before the pilot, so no before-and-after comparison could be made (DBT (opens in a new tab)).

2. Tie the metric to a business outcome

Choose metrics in layers, starting from a number the business already reports.

  • The outcome metric counts finished work: issues resolved per agent hour, invoices posted per clerk hour, share of new accounts that become active.
  • A driver metric is what the feature changes directly, such as handling time for one step. It moves first and is cheap to measure, but it is not the outcome.
  • Guardrail metrics must not get worse: rework rate, escalations, customer satisfaction.
  • Adoption shows whether people use the feature at all. High usage with a flat outcome is a finding, not a success.

The study Generative AI at Work shows the layers in use. Its headline is an outcome, issues resolved per hour, and it reports handling time, chats per hour, resolution rate and a customer satisfaction score beside it, so a reader can see that speed rose while resolution rate and satisfaction barely moved (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (opens in a new tab)).

Time saved is a driver metric. It becomes an outcome only when the freed time is redeemed: more work is completed, overtime or contractor hours fall, or a backlog shrinks. Multiplying minutes saved by salary assumes every freed minute is redeemed, and that assumption is rarely checked.

3. Build the cost model

Costs fall into three groups, and the formula is the same for any feature:

cost over m months=C0+m×(F+V×c)\text{cost over } m \text{ months} = C_0 + m \times (F + V \times c)cost over m months=C0​+m×(F+V×c)

C0C_0C0​ is one-off cost (build, integration, an evaluation set, training people), FFF is fixed monthly cost (maintenance, monitoring, seats), VVV is monthly volume and ccc is the cost of one use. For a feature that calls a model API:

c=calls per unit×input tokens×pin+output tokens×pout1,000,000c = \text{calls per unit} \times \frac{\text{input tokens} \times p_{\text{in}} + \text{output tokens} \times p_{\text{out}}}{1{,}000{,}000}c=calls per unit×1,000,000input tokens×pin​+output tokens×pout​​

with prices ppp quoted per million tokens. On Anthropic's price list every model's output tokens cost five times its input tokens, a cache read costs a tenth of the input price or less, and the Batch API halves both prices (Claude pricing (opens in a new tab)). Other products charge per seat or per outcome: Intercom lists its Fin agent at $0.99 per outcome, which makes the unit of cost the same as the unit of value (Fin pricing (opens in a new tab)).

Then divide by the success rate, because failed attempts are paid for too:

cost per successful outcome=cost per attemptsuccess rate+cost of handling failures\text{cost per successful outcome} = \frac{\text{cost per attempt}}{\text{success rate}} + \text{cost of handling failures}cost per successful outcome=success ratecost per attempt​+cost of handling failures

A draft that a person must check costs the tokens plus the checking time. The FinOps Foundation lists the same unit metrics (cost per inference, cost per token, cost per API call) next to ROI in its guidance for AI spending (FinOps for AI (opens in a new tab)). For ways to lower ccc, see LLM Cost and Latency Optimization.

4. Create a comparison

The effect of a feature is the gap between what happened and the counterfactual: what would have happened to the same group without it (Magenta Book (opens in a new tab)). The counterfactual is never observed, so you approximate it with a comparison group. Designs differ in how well they do that.

The Evaluation Task Force's AI guidance says evaluators should strongly consider experimental designs, and names three practical ways to randomise: who gets access, when they get access, or who is encouraged to use the tool. When you cannot randomise, a staggered rollout still gives a comparison. Take the change in the group that got the feature and subtract the change over the same period in the group that did not. This is difference-in-differences:

effect=(yˉT,after−yˉT,before)−(yˉC,after−yˉC,before)\text{effect} = (\bar{y}_{T,\text{after}} - \bar{y}_{T,\text{before}}) - (\bar{y}_{C,\text{after}} - \bar{y}_{C,\text{before}})effect=(yˉ​T,after​−yˉ​T,before​)−(yˉ​C,after​−yˉ​C,before​)

The subtraction removes anything that changed for everyone, such as a seasonal dip or a new process. It assumes the two groups would otherwise have moved in parallel, so check that they did before the rollout. A/B Testing and Online Experiments covers sample size and significance.

5. Calculate the return and label every input

ROI=benefit−costcost,payback months=C0monthly benefit−monthly running cost\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{benefit} - \text{cost}}{\text{cost}}, \qquad \text{payback months} = \frac{C_0}{\text{monthly benefit} - \text{monthly running cost}}ROI=costbenefit−cost​,payback months=monthly benefit−monthly running costC0​​

Mark each input with one of three labels:

LabelMeaningExample
MeasuredRead from a system or an experiment, with the method statedHandling time fell 1.97 minutes against a control group
EstimatedDerived from a measurement by a stated calculation, or from someone's judgementTwelve minutes to correct one reworked invoice
AssumedChosen without data from this deploymentSixty per cent of freed time is redeemed

A result is only as strong as its weakest input: measured minutes multiplied by an assumed redemption rate is an estimate. The Green Book describes optimism bias as the proven tendency of appraisals to be over-optimistic, and tells appraisers to correct for it by raising cost estimates and lowering benefit estimates. It also asks for switching values: how far a key assumption would have to move before the option stops being worth doing. A switching value turns "we assumed 60%" into "this pays back as long as at least 35% is redeemed", which a sponsor can judge.

Vendor dashboards contain estimates too. Microsoft's Copilot Dashboard reports Copilot assisted hours, which its documentation calls a general estimate computed from usage counts and research-derived multipliers, and Copilot assisted value, which multiplies those hours by an hourly rate that defaults to $72 (Microsoft Learn (opens in a new tab)). That is an estimate multiplied by an assumption, and the documentation labels it as one.

6. Report it, then measure again

Report the effect with its comparison design, the cost in its three groups, and the return with labels and switching values. Then keep measuring after rollout. A pilot overstates the long-run effect when participants volunteered or the tool was new, and understates it while people are still learning. Prices, models and volumes change, so the cost model needs the same review. Tracing tools supply the token and call counts.

Worked example: Invoice coding assistant: from a four-week pilot to a labelled ROI

This is a teaching scenario with invented data. Running the script reproduces every number.

Setting. An accounts-payable team of 12 clerks codes about 9,000 supplier invoices a month. A proposed assistant reads each invoice and suggests the ledger code and cost centre, and the clerk accepts or corrects it. The sponsor wants to know whether to roll it out to everyone.

Metrics. The outcome metric is handling minutes per invoice, from the finance system's open and post timestamps. The guardrail is the rework rate: the share of invoices an approver sends back.

Comparison. Six clerks take part for four weeks. In weeks 1 and 2 (pre) nobody has the assistant. In weeks 3 and 4 (post) clerks A, B and C (group T) use it and clerks D, E and F (group C) do not. A new supplier portal went live for everyone in week 3, which is the kind of change a before-and-after figure would wrongly credit to the assistant.

Costs. Logs show 3,200 input and 250 output tokens per call and 1.08 calls per invoice. The model costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens (Claude Sonnet 5.5 on Anthropic's price list (opens in a new tab), read on 10 October 2026). One-off cost is $25,760: 240 build hours at $100, 20 hours labelling an evaluation set at $40 and 24 hours of training at $40. Maintenance and monitoring cost $1,750 a month.

        for r in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(PILOT))]

def rate(grp, period, field):
    sel = [r for r in rows if r["grp"] == grp and r["period"] == period]
    return sum(r[field] for r in sel) / sum(r["invoices"] for r in sel)

def did(field):
    treated = rate("T", "post", field) - rate("T", "pre", field)
    control = rate("C", "post", field) - rate("C", "pre", field)
    return treated, control, treated - control

Effect. The treated clerks improved by 2.27 minutes, but the control clerks improved by 0.30 minutes with no assistant, so the measured effect is 1.97 minutes. Extra rework takes back 0.06 minutes (0.0051 × 12), leaving 1.91.

Cost. The model bill is $86.51 a month, under 5% of the $1,837 running cost. For a feature that makes one short call per unit of work, people cost far more than tokens.

The return is positive if more than about a third of the hours are redeemed, so the next thing to measure is overtime and agency hours in the three months after rollout.

Edge cases and limitations

The feature enables work that was not done before
If nobody summarised every supplier contract before, there is no baseline handling time to compare with, and a time saving against doing it by hand is fictional because nobody would have done it by hand. Value the new activity by what it changes downstream (disputes avoided, renewals caught) or by what the business would pay for the alternative, and label the figure as estimated. The DBT evaluation went further and counted such novel tasks as added workload, not as savings.
A real benefit that cannot be put in money
Better accessibility, fewer errors reaching customers or lower staff turnover can be genuine and still have no defensible price. The Green Book's approach is to monetise where possible, otherwise quantify, otherwise describe in words. Report such benefits in their own units beside the ROI and do not invent a conversion to make the percentage larger.
Too few units to tell a signal from noise
Six clerks or three teams give an effect whose true range is wide, and one unusual person can move the mean. Show per-person results, extend the pilot until the per-person effects agree in direction, and report a range such as the smallest and largest individual effect. Do not extrapolate a three-person result to three hundred people as a single number.
People refuse to work without the tool
Once a tool is popular, a control group is hard to keep. METR's follow-up study found developers declining tasks they did not want to do without AI, which biased the comparison. Run the comparison early, before habits form, or randomise the timing of access so that everyone gets the tool and the later group serves as the comparison in the meantime.
The cost model goes out of date
Token prices, model versions and tokenizers change. Anthropic's pricing page notes that its newer models use a tokenizer that produces roughly 30% more tokens for the same text, so the same prompt can cost more after an upgrade even at an unchanged price. Agent loops that make several calls per task multiply the per-unit cost. Recalculate the cost per successful outcome from live usage data after every model or prompt change.
The saving moves the work somewhere else
A drafting assistant can cut the author's time and raise the reviewer's, or a chatbot can close chats while phone calls rise. Measure the whole workflow, including the next step and other channels, and treat any metric that covers only the step the feature touches as a driver metric.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • A decision to expand, renew or stop an AI feature is coming, and the person deciding will be asked to justify the spend.
  • The feature acts on a repeated unit of work, such as a ticket, an invoice or a claim, so volume and time per unit can be counted.
  • Running cost scales with usage through tokens, seats or outcomes, so the cost at full adoption differs from the cost of the pilot.
  • The rollout can be staged, which gives a comparison group at almost no extra cost.

Think twice when

  • The change is cheap and easily reversed, so measuring it properly would cost more than being wrong about it.
  • The work is mandatory, such as meeting a regulatory deadline. Compare the cost of the ways to comply instead of calculating a return.
  • There is no repeated unit and no stable process yet. Run a short exploratory prototype first and measure once the workflow has settled.

A simpler alternative. A one-page before-and-after note: one outcome metric measured for two weeks before and two weeks after, the monthly running cost, and a sentence naming anything else that changed in between. This is enough when the effect is large, the process is stable and the spend is small. It stops being enough when the decision is expensive, the work is seasonal, or the people who used the feature chose to.

Key takeaways

  • A baseline has to be recorded before the feature arrives, because it cannot be reconstructed afterwards and every later claim depends on it.
  • An impact claim should be stated in an outcome the business already tracks. Time saved is a driver metric until the freed time is shown to be redeemed.
  • The cost of an AI feature is one-off cost plus fixed monthly cost plus cost per use times volume, and the figure to compare is the cost of one successful outcome.
  • The effect of a feature is the change against a comparison group. A before-and-after figure or a survey answer can differ from it in size and even in direction.
  • Every input to an ROI should be labelled as measured, estimated or assumed, and the result is no stronger than its weakest input.
  • A switching value tells the sponsor how wrong an assumption can be before the investment stops paying, which is more useful than a single ROI percentage.

Example 1 of 5

Generative AI at Work: a staggered rollout studied across 5,172 support agents, in issues resolved per hour

Documented in a paper

Generative AI at Work, by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond, studies an AI assistant introduced in the customer support operation of a Fortune 500 software company. The arXiv record and the full text of version 2 (November 2024) describe the data, the rollout and the estimates. [1][2]

The problem

The firm gave support agents a tool that suggests replies and links to internal documentation in real time, and agents were free to ignore it. The question was whether access changed how much work agents completed and at what quality. Agents differ widely in skill and tenure, the mix of problems shifts from month to month, and the tool reached different agents at different times, so a simple before-and-after average would mix the tool's effect with everything else that changed.

Where this topic fits

This is the comparison step of this lesson carried out on company records. The baseline is each agent's own history before access. The outcome metric is resolutions per hour, with handling time, chats per hour, resolution rate and a customer satisfaction score reported beside it. The comparison group comes from the staggered rollout: at any date some agents had the tool and others did not yet.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Define the outcome as issues resolved per hour, a unit of finished work that the support operation already tracks, instead of counting how often the tool was used.
  2. Record the companion metrics that show whether extra resolutions came from speed or from quality, namely average handling time, chats per hour, resolution rate and net promoter score.
  3. Use the rollout order as the comparison. The paper says the timing of access was driven by onboarding bottlenecks and the firm's budget for the tool, so agents gained access in different months.
  4. Estimate the effect with a difference-in-differences model that has agent and time fixed effects, so each agent is compared with their own past and with agents who did not yet have access in the same period.
  5. Split the estimate by skill and tenure, which shows that the average hides a large gain for the least experienced agents and little change for the most experienced.
  6. State what the design cannot answer. The authors say the paper covers medium-run effects inside one firm and is not designed to speak to wages or overall employment.

Why this approach fits

A support operation produces the same unit of work thousands of times a day and logs every one, which is the easiest case for measurement: the outcome is countable, the baseline already sits in the system, and a rollout that could not reach everyone at once supplies a comparison group at no extra cost. Using resolutions per hour instead of handling time alone guards against a tool that shortens chats by leaving more issues unresolved.

Limitation or tradeoff

The rollout was not randomised, so the estimate depends on the assumption that agents who received access earlier would otherwise have followed the same trend as the rest. It is one firm and one tool. The result is also in units of productivity, not money: the paper does not convert resolutions per hour into revenue or cost, and the authors say they have no data on individual pay. A team reusing this design still has to build the cost model and decide what an extra resolution is worth.

What the sources document

  • The paper studies the staggered introduction of a generative AI conversational assistant using data from 5,172 customer support agents, and reports that access increased productivity, measured as issues resolved per hour, by 15% on average. [1]
  • In the full text, access to AI recommendations raises resolutions per hour by 0.30 chats, or 15.2%, against a pre-treatment mean of 1.97, and average handle time falls by 3.7 minutes from a baseline mean of 43 minutes. [2]
  • The paper attributes the variation in when agents got access to bottlenecks in onboarding caused by limited training resources and to the firm's overall budget for AI assistance. The average share of active workers with access grew from about 5% in October 2020 to about 70% in January 2021. [2]
  • The abstract reports that less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved both speed and quality, while the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw small gains in speed and small declines in quality. [1]
  • The authors state that the findings capture medium-run impacts within a single firm and that the paper is not designed to shed light on the aggregate employment or wage effects of generative AI tools. [2]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

METR's developer study: forecasts and self-estimates checked against a randomised measurement

METRDocumented in a paper

METR ran a randomised controlled trial of AI coding tools with experienced open-source developers in early 2025 and published it as a paper and a blog post. In February 2026 it published an update on a larger follow-up study and on problems it had found with the design. [4][3][5]

The problem

Claims about AI coding tools rested mostly on benchmark scores and on what developers said about their own speed. METR wanted to know how much faster, or slower, experienced developers actually were when working on real issues in repositories they knew well, and how that compared with what the developers and outside experts expected.

Where this topic fits

The study separates three numbers that are usually merged: a forecast made before the work, a self-estimate made after it, and a measurement from a randomised comparison. It is the clearest published case of this lesson's labels pointing in different directions for the same people and the same tasks.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Recruit developers who contribute to large open-source projects and have them supply real issues from repositories they already work in.
  2. Before the work, collect each developer's forecast of how much AI will change completion time, and collect forecasts from outside experts in economics and machine learning.
  3. Assign each issue at random to one of two conditions, AI tools allowed or AI tools not allowed, so that task difficulty is balanced between the conditions.
  4. Record how long each issue takes. The blog post says developers reported their implementation time and recorded their screens while working.
  5. After the study, ask the developers how much they think AI changed their speed, then set forecast, self-estimate and measurement side by side.
  6. Publish the limits of the result. METR lists what the study does not show, and its 2026 update explains that developers who avoid working without AI now bias the comparison.

Why this approach fits

Randomising at the level of the task removes the usual objection that people pick AI for the tasks it suits. Collecting forecasts and self-estimates inside the same study turns the question "how good is our intuition?" into a number. For anyone assembling a business case, it shows why a survey answer cannot stand in for a timing.

Limitation or tradeoff

Sixteen developers working in mature repositories they know well is a narrow setting, and METR says the result should not be read as a statement about most developers, other kinds of work or later tools. The follow-up exposes a second weakness that matters in field work: once people depend on a tool, many decline the control condition or hold back the tasks they want AI for, so the comparison stops being fair. METR's own conclusion was that the follow-up data is only very weak evidence about the size of any speed-up.

What the sources document

  • The study was a randomised controlled trial in which 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 tasks, each randomly assigned to allow or disallow AI tools. Developers mainly used Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet. [4][3]
  • Before the tasks, developers forecast that AI would reduce completion time by 24%. After the study they estimated it had reduced completion time by 20%. The measured result was that allowing AI increased completion time by 19%. [4]
  • Experts in economics had predicted that AI would shorten completion time by 39%, and experts in machine learning by 38%. [4]
  • METR states that the study does not provide evidence that AI systems fail to speed up many or most software developers, or evidence about domains other than software development. [3]
  • The February 2026 update reports data from 57 developers, 143 repositories and more than 800 tasks, with an estimated speedup of -18% (confidence interval -38% to +9%) for returning developers and -4% (-15% to +9%) for newly recruited ones. It says 30% to 50% of developers were choosing not to submit some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI. [5]

UK government Microsoft 365 Copilot trials: self-reported time savings and what an adjustment did to them

UK Department for Business and TradeDocumented in a case study

Two UK government reports cover trials of Microsoft 365 Copilot in late 2024. One is the Government Digital Service's cross-government findings report of June 2025. The other is the Department for Business and Trade's evaluation of its own 1,000-licence pilot, published in August 2025 and summarised on its Digital Trade blog. [6][7][8]

The problem

Departments had to decide whether to keep paying for Copilot licences after a three-month trial. General office work has no single unit of output like a resolved ticket, so both evaluations relied largely on what users reported. The question for this lesson is how far such reports can carry a value-for-money decision.

Where this topic fits

Both reports start on the self-report row of the evidence table, and the DBT report then moves partway up it. It adjusts the self-reported figures for work that produced nothing, checks them against a small set of timed tasks done by users and non-users, and keeps the finding "users report saving time" apart from the finding "productivity improved".

How it is applied, step by step

  1. GDS surveyed users near the end of the trial, asking them to choose a range for the average time saved each day. Each answer was converted to the middle of its range, with the top band counted as 60 minutes, and averaged.
  2. DBT ran a one-week diary study. For each task, respondents recorded the time taken with Copilot and their estimate of the time it would have taken without it.
  3. DBT also asked whether the output was used and whether the task was one the person would normally do. Where the output went unused, or the task was new work done only because Copilot was available, the time spent was counted as a negative saving.
  4. DBT reviewed outliers before averaging and removed entries where the diary showed that several tasks had been merged or that minutes had been entered as hours.
  5. DBT ran timed task sessions with licence holders and with colleagues who had no licence, and had the outputs scored blind for accuracy and quality.
  6. Both reports state their limits. GDS says it could not identify how the saved time was spent. DBT says it found no robust evidence that time savings led to improved productivity, and that a value-for-money assessment was still being carried out.

Why this approach fits

Where there is no countable unit of output and no baseline, self-report is often the only data available, and the DBT method shows how to make it less misleading at low cost: ask two extra questions per task and subtract the work that led nowhere. The timed sessions then act as a spot check on the direction of the diary figures.

Limitation or tradeoff

The adjusted figures are still the users' own estimates, from a diary that 32% of licence holders completed, and about 70% of the licences went to volunteers. The timed sessions were very small, and DBT says their results should be treated with caution. Neither report includes cost, so neither states a return. The GDS figure also cannot be turned into money as it stands: 26 minutes a day is a self-reported driver metric, and the report says the use of that time was not identified.

What the sources document

  • The GDS report covers a cross-government trial that ran from 30 September to 31 December 2024 with 20,000 government employees. It reports an average saving of 26 minutes a day and says the time savings are self-reported. [6]
  • GDS asked participants to estimate their average daily time saved in ranges from none or less than 5 minutes to more than an hour, and calculated the figure from the middle point of each range, with the largest savings estimated at 60 minutes. It states that it was not possible to identify how the time saved was spent. [6]
  • DBT had 1,000 licences, about 70% allocated to volunteers and about 30% to randomly selected staff. Its diary study had a response rate of 32%. [7]
  • In DBT's table of mean hours saved per task, summarising research falls from 1.6 hours unadjusted to 0.8 after adjusting for unused outputs and novel tasks, and data analysis falls from 2.0 to 0.6. Scheduling and generating images end below zero, at -0.6 and -0.5 hours. [7]
  • In DBT's observed sessions, Copilot users summarised reports faster and with higher scores than non-users but completed Excel data analysis more slowly and with lower scores. The report states that it did not find robust evidence that time savings are leading to improved productivity, and that no data was captured from participants before the pilot. [7]

Intercom Fin: an AI agent priced per outcome, with a written definition of what counts

IntercomDocumented in official docs

Fin is Intercom's AI agent for customer service. Intercom's help centre article on Fin outcomes and the Fin pricing page define the unit the product is billed in and the rules for when that unit is counted. [10][11]

The problem

A buyer of an AI support agent pays for it from the support budget and wants the cost to track the work it takes off the team. Per-seat and per-token prices do not do that: a seat costs the same whether or not the agent resolves anything, and tokens are spent on failed conversations as well as successful ones. Pricing per outcome aligns cost with value only if "outcome" is defined tightly enough that both sides count it the same way.

Where this topic fits

This is the cost model with the unit already chosen. The cost per attempt and the success rate are folded into one published price per successful outcome, so the buyer's remaining work is the other side of the comparison: what a human-handled conversation costs today, what share of conversations the agent resolves, and whether the resolutions are real.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Define the billable unit as an outcome, counted when the agent delivers a defined result in a conversation, and publish a price for it.
  2. Define the main outcome, a resolution, in two forms. A confirmed resolution is one where the customer says the answer helped. An assumed resolution is one where the customer leaves after the agent's last answer without asking for more help.
  3. Exclude the cases that are not value, such as the agent only replying to a greeting or the customer asking to speak to a person.
  4. Charge at most one outcome per conversation, however many questions or actions it contains.
  5. Reverse the charge if the customer comes back to the same conversation needing more help.
  6. Report the resolution rate, the share of conversations the agent resolves without human involvement, as the headline performance figure.

Why this approach fits

For the buyer, a price per resolution makes the unit cost known in advance and comparable with the cost of a human-handled conversation, which is a comparison a support leader already makes. It also moves the cost of failed attempts to the vendor. The written definition matters as much as the price, because it tells the buyer exactly which events they are paying for and which they should audit.

Limitation or tradeoff

An assumed resolution is an inference from silence. A customer who gives up, or who phones instead of replying, leaves the chat without asking for more help and is counted the same way as one whose problem was solved; the documented deduction applies when the customer returns to the same conversation. A buyer should therefore track the split between confirmed and assumed resolutions and watch repeat contacts on other channels. The saving is not automatic either: fewer human-handled conversations lower cost only if staffing or overtime actually changes. The prices and definitions are the vendor's and can change; these were read on the access date.

What the sources document

  • Intercom's Fin pricing page lists a price of $0.99 per outcome with a minimum of 50 outcomes per month, and the help article lists qualification outcomes at $9.99. [11][10]
  • The help article defines a resolution as an outcome counted when, following Fin's last answer, the customer either confirms the answer was satisfactory (a confirmed resolution) or exits the conversation without requesting further assistance (an assumed resolution). [10]
  • The article states that a customer is charged for only one outcome per conversation, even if Fin takes multiple actions. [10]
  • If a conversation is considered resolved and the customer later returns to the same conversation seeking further assistance, the article says that resolution is deducted and not charged. A reply to a greeting alone is not counted as a resolution. [10]
  • The article describes Fin's resolution rate as the percentage of conversations that Fin resolves without human involvement. [10]

4Common mistakes

  1. Starting to measure only after the feature has launched.

    Why it causes problems
    Without a record of how the work performed before, any later figure has nothing to be compared with, and the business case falls back on opinion. The DBT evaluation could not make a before-and-after comparison for exactly this reason.
    How to recognise it
    The project plan has a launch date and a review date but no task for collecting pre-launch data, and nobody can state last month's handling time or error rate.
    How to fix it
    In the first week, export or time at least two weeks of the outcome and guardrail metrics, split by case type, and store the result with the date and the method used.

  2. Multiplying self-reported minutes saved by a salary and calling the product a saving.

    Why it causes problems
    The figure stacks a guess (the minutes) on an assumption (that all freed time turns into value). In METR's trial the developers' own estimates were wrong in direction, and the GDS trial could not say how the saved time was spent.
    How to recognise it
    The headline is in hours or currency per year, the source is a survey, and no payroll, overtime, backlog or throughput figure has changed.
    How to fix it
    Time the work from system records against a comparison group, name the place where the saving should appear, such as overtime hours or cases closed per week, and apply a stated redeemed share until that figure moves.

  3. Crediting the feature with the whole before-and-after change.

    Why it causes problems
    Seasonal volume, a new process or simple practice can move the metric in the same weeks. In the worked example the control group improved by 0.30 minutes with no assistant, so the raw change overstated the effect by about 15%.
    How to recognise it
    The report compares one group with its own past and does not mention anything else that changed during the period.
    How to fix it
    Stage the rollout so that some comparable people or cases do not have the feature yet, and subtract their change from the treated group's change.

  4. Counting only the model bill as the cost of the feature.

    Why it causes problems
    Build time, maintenance, monitoring, training and human review are usually larger than token spend for a low-volume feature, so the return is inflated many times over. In the worked example tokens were under 5% of the monthly running cost.
    How to recognise it
    The cost line is a single number taken from the provider's invoice, with no hours of anyone's time in it.
    How to fix it
    List one-off, fixed monthly and per-use costs separately, include the time people spend checking output, and divide by the success rate to get the cost of one successful outcome.

  5. Reporting usage or satisfaction as if it were impact.

    Why it causes problems
    Counts of prompts, active users and positive survey scores show that people try or like the feature. They do not show that the outcome changed. DBT found 72% of respondents satisfied with Copilot and still found no robust evidence of improved productivity.
    How to recognise it
    Every chart in the report is about adoption or sentiment, and none shows the outcome metric against a baseline.
    How to fix it
    Keep adoption as its own layer and show the outcome metric and the guardrails beside it. Treat high adoption with a flat outcome as a result that needs explaining.

  6. Blending measured results and projections into one headline number.

    Why it causes problems
    The reader cannot tell which part would survive an audit, and when the projection misses, the measured part loses credibility along with it.
    How to recognise it
    A single annual figure appears with no method beside it, or phrases such as estimated to and could save sit in the same list as counts taken from a system.
    How to fix it
    Present three columns, measured, estimated and assumed, and give the switching value for each assumption so the reader can see how much rests on it.

        for r in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(DATA))]

    sel = [r for r in rows if r["team"] == team and (r["week"] >= ROLLOUT_WEEK) == post]
    return sum(r[field] for r in sel) / sum(r["tickets"] for r in sel)


def did(field):
    treated = rate("North", True, field) - rate("North", False, field)

C. Recalculation

  • Slide: 4.5 × 120 × $210 × 52 = $5,896,800.
  • Hours if all time were redeemed, after halving and using 46 weeks: 4.5 × 0.5 × 120 × 46 = 12,420 hours, worth 12,420 × $210 = $2,608,200.
  • With 30% redeemed: $2,608,200 × 0.3 = $782,460.
  • First-year cost: 120 × $90 × 12 + $60,000 = $189,600.
  • ROI: (782,460 − 189,600) / 189,600 = 3.13, or about 313%.
  • Switching value of the redeemed share: 189,600 / 2,608,200 = 0.073, about 7%.

The adjusted return is still positive, and the slide's own figures would give about 3,010%. But the calculation still rests on the self-reported 4.5 hours, and adjusting an estimate does not turn it into a measurement. The switching value says the case survives a low redeemed share, so the input worth measuring next is the hours themselves.

D. Headline

Measured: 14 volunteer lawyers reviewed NDAs 38% faster than in their own previous month, with no comparison group. Estimated from a survey: about 4.5 hours a week saved per user. If half of that holds across 120 lawyers and 30% of it is redeemed, the first-year value is about $0.78 million against about $0.19 million of cost, and a staged rollout with timings is needed to confirm it.

Check your answer

  • Did you label claim 3 as assumed and not as measured, even though it contains precise-looking numbers?
  • Does your recalculated value come to 782,460 dollars and your first-year cost to 189,600 dollars?
  • Did you notice that in a firm billing by the hour, saved time is not automatically value?
  • Does your rewritten headline say how the 38% was obtained and from how many people?
