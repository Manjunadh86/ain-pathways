Bias, Variance and Overfitting

Underfitting is when a model is too simple to capture the pattern in its data, and overfitting is when it follows the training data so closely that it also learns the noise. Bias is the error that remains however much data you add, because the model's assumptions are wrong; variance is the error that comes from the model changing whenever the training sample changes.

1Overview

Why it matters

Every supervised model is trained on one sample and used on another. The most common reason a model that looked good in a notebook disappoints in use is that it fitted the sample instead of the pattern. The opposite failure is quieter: a model too rigid to learn what the data plainly show, which no amount of extra data will fix. Telling the two apart decides what you do next. Collecting more data, adding features, growing the model, penalising it and stopping training earlier are all reasonable steps, but each one helps with only one of the two problems and can make the other worse.

Anyone who chooses a model's size or signs off a training report needs this: an analyst picking a polynomial degree or a tree depth, an engineer setting max_depth in a boosting library or the number of epochs for a neural network, a reviewer deciding whether a validation score can be trusted. The same ideas explain why regularization, tree ensembles and early stopping exist. Each one gives up a little bias to remove a larger amount of variance.

  • Choose a remedy that matches the diagnosis, and name one that would be wasted effort.

Key terms

Generalization
How well a model performs on new data drawn from the same source as its training data, as opposed to the rows it was fitted on.
Generalization gap
Validation error minus training error for the same model and metric. A large gap means the model learned things that hold only in the training sample.
Underfitting
A model too simple, too heavily penalised or too briefly trained to capture the pattern, so its error is high on training and validation data alike.
Overfitting
A model that fits its training data, noise included, so closely that its error on new data is much higher than its training error.
Bias
The gap between the truth and the average of the models you would get by training on many different samples. It comes from assumptions the model cannot escape.
Variance
How much the fitted model, and so its predictions, change from one training sample to another of the same size.
Irreducible error (noise)
The spread of the target around its true pattern. No model can predict it, so it sets a floor under the test error.
Capacity
How flexible a model family is, set by choices such as polynomial degree, tree depth, number of features, training steps or penalty strength.
Validation curve
Training and validation error plotted against one capacity setting, used to pick the setting with the lowest validation error.
Learning curve
Training and validation error plotted against the number of training rows, used to see whether more data would help.

2Explanation

Introduction

In train/test splits and cross-validation you learned to judge a model on rows it has not seen. In linear regression you saw why: as polynomial terms were added, training error kept falling while held-out error fell, bottomed out and rose again. This lesson explains that shape, names the two ways a model can miss, and shows how to tell which one you have.

Picture two students revising from the same past exam papers. The first learns one rule of thumb and applies it to every question. She does poorly on the past papers and just as poorly in the exam: she has underfitted. The second memorises every past answer, misprints in the answer key included. He gets full marks on the past papers and does poorly in the exam, where the questions are new and the misprints do not repeat: he has overfitted. The student you want learned the method.

Here is the same story in data. Eleven noisy readings were taken from a smooth curve and three polynomials were fitted to them by least squares. Each is scored twice with mean squared error (MSE, see regression metrics): on the eleven readings it was fitted to, and on ten fresh ones.

ModelTraining MSEValidation MSEWhat happened
Degree 1 (line)0.2840.310Misses the bend. Poor on both sets: underfitting.
Degree 3 (cubic)0.0310.103Follows the bend and ignores the wobble.
Degree 90.00030.320Threads almost every reading. Poor on new ones: overfitting.

Validation error minus training error is the generalization gap. The line has almost no gap and a large error. Degree 9 has a tiny training error and a large gap. Two words say where those errors come from. Bias is error caused by the model's assumptions, here a straight line imposed on a curve; a million more readings would not remove it. Variance is error caused by the model reacting to its particular sample, here a degree-9 curve chasing noise; draw eleven new readings and it would swing somewhere else.

The rest of the lesson makes these ideas measurable: a diagnosis from two numbers, the bias-variance decomposition, how capacity moves a model between the two failures, and how a learning curve shows whether more data would help.

How it works

Two numbers give the diagnosis

Fit the model, then compute one metric on the training set and on a validation set (or by cross-validation). scikit-learn's guide to validation curves (opens in a new tab) states the rule in terms of scores: if the training and validation scores are both low, the estimator is underfitting; if the training score is high and the validation score is low, it is overfitting. In iterative training the same pattern unfolds over time. Google's Machine Learning Crash Course (opens in a new tab) describes it on a loss curve: training loss keeps falling or levels off while validation loss starts to rise.

"High" and "low" need reference points. Compare both numbers with a naive baseline such as predicting the training mean, with the lowest error you believe is achievable, and with each other.

No threshold makes a gap officially too large. Tools pick conventions: the built-in Overfit rule in Amazon SageMaker Debugger (opens in a new tab), a service AWS no longer opens to new customers, fires by default when the gap between mean validation loss and mean training loss exceeds 10 % of the mean training loss. Treat such a number as an alarm to investigate, and judge the model by whether its validation error is good enough for the job.

Bias, variance and noise

Suppose the data follow y=f(x)+εy = f(x) + \varepsilony=f(x)+ε, where fff is the true pattern and ε\varepsilonε is noise with mean 0 and variance σ2\sigma^2σ2. A training set is a random sample, so the fitted model f^\hat{f}f^​ is random too: another sample would give another f^\hat{f}f^​. Fix one input xxx and average the squared error over every training set you might have drawn and over the noise in the new observation:

  • Bias compares the average model with the truth. It is what stays wrong after the sample-to-sample wobble has been averaged away.
  • Variance measures how far individual fitted models scatter around that average model.
  • Noise is the spread of yyy around f(x)f(x)f(x). No model can remove it, which is why it is called irreducible error.

The three terms add with no cross terms for two reasons: deviations from the average model average to zero, and the noise in a new observation has mean zero and is independent of the training set. These lecture notes (opens in a new tab) give the derivation. Two consequences follow. The decomposition describes a procedure (an algorithm, its settings and a sample size), not one fitted model. And bias can only be measured when you know fff, and variance only when you can redraw training sets, so both are estimated by simulation, as in the worked example, while real projects rely on training and validation error.

Capacity moves a model along the tradeoff

Capacity is how flexible a model family is: the degree of a polynomial, the depth of a tree, the number of features, the number of boosting rounds or training epochs, a small kkk in k-nearest neighbours, a weak penalty. More capacity lets the average model get closer to the truth, so bias falls. It also gives each fitted model more freedom to follow its own sample, so variance rises. The XGBoost tuning notes (opens in a new tab) say this of tree depth: a more complicated model fits the training data better and is less biased, but needs more data.

For the eleven-reading problem both quantities can be computed exactly. A least-squares prediction is a weighted sum of the training targets, f^(x)=∑ihi(x) yi\hat{f}(x) = \sum_i h_i(x)\,y_if^​(x)=∑i​hi​(x)yi​, so the average model is the fit to the noise-free values f(xi)f(x_i)f(xi​), and the variance at xxx is σ2∑ihi(x)2\sigma^2 \sum_i h_i(x)^2σ2∑i​hi​(x)2.

From degree 1 to degree 3, bias² drops from 0.193 to 0.004 while variance rises only from 0.015 to 0.028: a good trade. Past degree 3 almost no bias is left to remove (bias² is 0.0003 at degree 4), and variance climbs to 0.197 at degree 9. Expected test error is therefore U-shaped with its minimum at degree 3. Expected training error only falls, and reaches zero at degree 10, where the polynomial has as many coefficients as there are readings. That is why training error can never choose capacity. The tool for that choice is a validation curve: training and validation error plotted against one capacity setting.

Data size and the learning curve

More data reduces variance and leaves bias alone. Least squares makes this exact. With ppp coefficients and nnn training rows, the variance of the fitted values, averaged over the training inputs, is σ2p/n\sigma^2 p/nσ2p/n, and

E[training MSE]=bias2+σ2(1−pn),E[MSE on new targets, same inputs]=bias2+σ2(1+pn)\mathbb{E}[\text{training MSE}] = \text{bias}^2 + \sigma^2\Big(1 - \frac{p}{n}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{MSE on new targets, same inputs}] = \text{bias}^2 + \sigma^2\Big(1 + \frac{p}{n}\Big)E[training MSE]=bias2+σ2(1−np​),E[MSE on new targets, same inputs]=bias2+σ2(1+np​)

Here bias² is averaged over the training inputs. The gap between the two is 2σ2p/n2\sigma^2 p/n2σ2p/n. What matters is the ratio of parameters to rows: double the rows and the gap halves, double the parameters and it doubles. A learning curve plots training and validation error against the number of training rows and shows this directly.

The line's two curves sit together from the start, at about three times the noise floor: more rows would be wasted on it. The degree-9 model begins with a wide gap that closes as rows arrive, and by 20 rows its test error is less than half the line's. The scikit-learn guide reads its own two examples the same way: where both curves converge to a low score, more data will probably not help much; where the training score is much greater than the validation score, more samples will most likely improve generalization.

So size the model to the data. Rule 21 of Google's Rules of Machine Learning (opens in a new tab) says the number of feature weights a linear model can learn is roughly proportional to the amount of data, with examples running from about a dozen features for 1,000 labelled examples to about ten million features for a billion.

scikit-learn computes both curves by cross-validation:

python
from sklearn.model_selection import learning_curve, validation_curve

train_scores, val_scores = validation_curve(
    model, X, y, param_name="max_depth", param_range=[1, 2, 4, 8], cv=5)
sizes, train_scores, val_scores = learning_curve(
    model, X, y, train_sizes=[0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 1.0], cv=5)

Each score array has one row per setting or size and one column per fold, so average across columns before plotting. Exercise 1 runs both on real data.

Matching the fix to the diagnosis

  • High bias (underfitting). Add capacity: more informative features, a higher degree, deeper trees, more training steps, a weaker penalty. More rows will not help.
  • High variance (overfitting). Get more rows or augment the ones you have, cut capacity, add a penalty on complexity (regularization, which Google's crash course sums up (opens in a new tab) as minimising loss plus complexity), stop training when validation error turns upward, or average many models. Averaging attacks variance directly: in scikit-learn's bagging example (opens in a new tab) a single decision tree has an error of 0.0255 (0.0003 bias², 0.0152 variance, 0.0098 noise) and a bagged ensemble of such trees has 0.0196 (0.0004, 0.0092, 0.0098).
  • Both errors low and close together. You are near the noise floor. Only better features or cleaner labels can lower it.

Worked example: Eleven noisy readings of a known curve, fitted at three capacities

The data are synthetic, because bias and variance can only be measured when the true curve is known. The truth is f(x)=cos⁡(1.5πx)f(x) = \cos(1.5\pi x)f(x)=cos(1.5πx) for xxx between 0 and 1, the curve scikit-learn uses in its underfitting and overfitting example (opens in a new tab). Each reading is f(x)f(x)f(x) plus normal noise with standard deviation σ=0.3\sigma = 0.3σ=0.3, so the noise floor is σ2=0.09\sigma^2 = 0.09σ2=0.09. The readings were drawn once with np.random.default_rng(7) (eleven training noise values, then ten validation values) and rounded to two decimals; the arrays in Step 1 are pasted in, so the code runs without repeating that draw. The printed outputs are from NumPy 2.5.3.

Step 1: one training set, three models

python
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import Polynomial

def f(x):                                  # the true curve
    return np.cos(1.5 * np.pi * x)

x_train = np.arange(11) / 10               # 0.0, 0.1, ..., 1.0
y_train = np.array([1.00, 0.98, 0.51, -0.11, -0.45, -1.00, -0.93, -0.59, -0.96, -0.64, 0.15])
x_val = np.arange(10) / 10 + 0.05          # 0.05, 0.15, ..., 0.95
y_val = np.array([1.08, 0.79, 0.10, -0.09, -0.31, -1.26, -1.13, -1.49, -1.04, -0.79])

for degree in [1, 3, 9]:
    model = Polynomial.fit(x_train, y_train, degree)       # least-squares polynomial
    train_mse = np.mean((model(x_train) - y_train) ** 2)
    val_mse = np.mean((model(x_val) - y_val) ** 2)
    print(f"degree {degree}: train MSE {train_mse:.4f}, validation MSE {val_mse:.4f}")
text
degree 1: train MSE 0.2840, validation MSE 0.3098
degree 3: train MSE 0.0314, validation MSE 0.1030
degree 9: train MSE 0.0003, validation MSE 0.3202

Degree 1 is poor on both sets, at more than three times the noise floor: underfitting. Degree 9 is almost perfect on the readings it saw and over a thousand times worse on the ones it did not: overfitting. Degree 3 has a gap too, but its validation error is close to the 0.09 floor. For reference, predicting the mean of y_train everywhere scores 0.511 and 0.750. Training error alone would have chosen degree 9.

The coefficients tell the same story. model.convert().coef for the cubic is 1.14, −3.10, −4.60 and 6.67. For degree 9 the coefficients run from −267,685 to +262,094: huge terms that cancel one another to thread the curve through the noise. Penalising large coefficients is the idea behind regularization.

Step 2: redraw the training set 2,000 times

One training set cannot separate bias from variance. Because the truth is known, new training sets can be drawn at the same eleven inputs and each model refitted on every one. Run this after the Step 1 code.

python
x_test = np.linspace(0, 1, 101)
sigma, n_sets = 0.3, 2000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

for degree in [1, 3, 9]:
    preds = np.empty((n_sets, x_test.size))
    for s in range(n_sets):
        y = f(x_train) + rng.normal(0, sigma, x_train.size)    # a fresh training set
        preds[s] = Polynomial.fit(x_train, y, degree)(x_test)
    bias2 = np.mean((preds.mean(axis=0) - f(x_test)) ** 2)     # average model vs truth
    variance = np.mean(preds.var(axis=0))                      # scatter around the average model
    print(f"degree {degree}: bias^2 {bias2:.3f} + variance {variance:.3f} + noise {sigma**2:.3f}"
          f" = expected test MSE {bias2 + variance + sigma**2:.3f}")
text
degree 1: bias^2 0.193 + variance 0.015 + noise 0.090 = expected test MSE 0.298
degree 3: bias^2 0.004 + variance 0.028 + noise 0.090 = expected test MSE 0.122
degree 9: bias^2 0.000 + variance 0.196 + noise 0.090 = expected test MSE 0.287

preds.mean(axis=0) is the average model and preds.var(axis=0) is the scatter around it; both are then averaged over 101 test inputs. The exact least-squares values are 0.193, 0.004 and 0.000 for bias² and 0.015, 0.028 and 0.197 for variance, so 2,000 redraws are enough.

The line and the degree-9 polynomial have nearly the same expected error, 0.298 and 0.287, for opposite reasons. About two-thirds of the line's error is bias; about two-thirds of the polynomial's is variance. The validation errors in Step 1 (0.310, 0.103, 0.320) were single noisy samples of these expectations.

Edge cases and limitations

Very large models can improve again after they fit the training data exactly
The U-shaped curve describes models that are too small to fit their training set exactly. Belkin and co-authors describe a double descent curve that extends it: they present evidence, across a wide spectrum of models and datasets, that increasing capacity beyond the point where a model interpolates the training data can improve test performance again. This is one reason deep networks are routinely trained to zero training error. The practical rule is unchanged: choose capacity by held-out error, and do not assume that the first minimum you find is the last.
The additive formula holds for squared error only
The identity error = bias squared + variance + noise is derived for squared error. For classification accuracy or log loss there is no single agreed additive split, although the words bias and variance are still used for the same two symptoms. Diagnose classifiers with training and validation metrics and with learning curves, which work for any metric, and do not expect three numbers that sum to the error.
A small validation set makes the diagnosis itself noisy
In a simulation that redraws the worked example's 11 training and 10 validation readings 2,000 times (noise from `np.random.default_rng(0)`, training noise first and then validation noise in each round), the degree-3 validation MSE has a median of 0.11 and 90 % of its values fall between 0.05 and 0.22. Degree 9 beats degree 3 on the validation readings in about 6 % of the rounds and the straight line beats it in about 2 %, although degree 3 has the lowest expected error. With few validation rows a single split can reverse the ranking of two models. Use k-fold cross-validation, look at the spread across folds, and treat differences smaller than that spread as ties, choosing the simpler model.
Models whose capacity grows with the data do not close the gap
The promise that more rows shrink the gap assumes capacity stays fixed. A fully grown decision tree or a 1-nearest-neighbour model memorises every training row however many there are, so its training error stays at zero and, on noisy data, its validation error stays high. In Exercise 1 an unlimited-depth tree has a training RMSE of 0.0 and a validation RMSE between 77.7 and 82.9 at every size from 35 to 353 rows. Limit the capacity (depth, leaf size or k) before asking for more data.
A gap caused by different data is not overfitting
Training and validation errors are comparable only when both sets come from the same distribution. Google's crash course lists the conditions: examples that are independent and identically distributed, a dataset that does not change significantly over time, and partitions with the same distribution. If validation rows come from a later period, another region or another labelling process, a gap can appear that no capacity setting will close; if rows leak between the sets, a real gap is hidden. Google's Rules of Machine Learning recommends measuring performance on data gathered after the training period (Rule 33) and says that a large drop between holdout and next-day performance may indicate time-sensitive features (Rule 37). Check the split first.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You have a training score and a validation score and must decide what to change next, whether capacity, penalty, features, training time or data.
  • You are choosing one capacity setting, such as a polynomial degree, a tree depth, a number of boosting rounds or k, and can afford a validation curve over it.
  • Someone proposes collecting or labelling more data and you need evidence from a learning curve that it would lower validation error.
  • Validation error is acceptable and you need to know how far it is from the noise floor before spending more effort on tuning.

Think twice when

  • Validation and production data differ in time, source or labelling. Fix the split or the pipeline first, because no capacity change repairs a distribution shift.
  • The metric is not squared error and you want three numbers that add up. Use training and validation metrics and learning curves as the diagnosis instead of a decomposition.
  • The model is a very large neural network trained past zero training error. One U-shaped curve is not a reliable guide there, so compare complete training runs on held-out data.

A simpler alternative. Compute three numbers: the validation error of a naive baseline (the training mean or the majority class) and your model's training and validation errors. A model that barely beats the baseline is underfitting; a training error far below the validation error is overfitting. That is enough to choose a direction. It stops being enough when you must pick an exact setting or justify the cost of more data, which is when validation curves and learning curves are worth their compute.

Key takeaways

  • Underfitting shows as high error on both training and validation data; overfitting shows as low training error with a much higher validation error.
  • Expected squared error on new data is bias squared plus variance plus noise, where bias is the error of the average model, variance is the scatter of fitted models around it, and noise is a floor no model can beat.
  • Raising capacity lowers bias and raises variance, so test error is U-shaped in capacity while training error only falls, and capacity must be chosen on held-out data.
  • More training rows shrink variance and the gap between training and validation error, but they do nothing for bias.
  • A learning curve whose two lines have met at a poor value calls for a more flexible model or better features; a wide remaining gap calls for more data or less capacity.
  • Check that the validation data resemble the data the model will meet, because a gap caused by shifted data is not overfitting and no capacity setting will close it.

Example 1 of 5

XGBoost: tree depth, sampling and step size as bias-variance controls

XGBoostDocumented in official docs

XGBoost is an open-source library for gradient-boosted decision trees. Its official documentation includes notes on parameter tuning that present the main parameters as a bias-variance tradeoff, a parameter reference that says which way each setting moves the model, and a Python introduction that documents early stopping. [12][13][14]

The problem

A boosted ensemble adds trees one after another, each correcting the errors left by the trees before it. Nothing in that process stops by itself: deeper trees and more rounds keep lowering training error. The tuning notes name the symptom users then meet, high training accuracy with low test accuracy, and the task is to choose settings whose capacity matches the data.

Where this topic fits

The concept lives in the training configuration. Depth, minimum child weight and gamma set how complex each tree may be. Row and column subsampling and the step size control how strongly each tree follows the particular sample. The number of rounds sets how long capacity keeps growing. Validation error, measured after every round, is the signal used to set all of them.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Train with an evaluation list that holds the training set and a validation set, so that both errors are reported after every boosting round.
  2. If training error is good and validation error is poor, limit complexity directly: lower max_depth, or raise min_child_weight or gamma, each of which the reference describes as making the algorithm more conservative.
  3. Add randomness so that each tree depends less on the exact sample: set subsample and colsample_bytree below 1.
  4. Reduce the step size eta so that each tree contributes less, and raise the number of rounds to compensate, as the notes advise.
  5. If both errors are poor, move the same settings the other way. The notes say that a more complicated model, for example a deeper one, is less biased.
  6. Use early stopping on the validation set to choose the number of rounds, and predict with the best iteration, because the model returned by training is the one from the last round.

Why this approach fits

Boosting makes capacity easy to add and hard to notice: every extra round and every extra level of depth lowers bias a little and raises variance a little. The library answers with a small set of parameters that map onto the levers in this lesson. Depth and split thresholds cap capacity, subsampling and a small step size reduce the variance of each addition, and early stopping reads the validation curve over training time to pick the stopping point. Describing each parameter by whether it makes the model more conservative tells a user which way to turn it once training and validation error have given the diagnosis.

Limitation or tradeoff

The parameters interact. A smaller eta needs more rounds, and max_depth and min_child_weight both limit the same splits, so tuning one at a time can mislead; the notes point to hyperparameter optimisation frameworks for a joint search. Early stopping also spends the validation set: the stopping round is chosen on it, so the validation score at that round is optimistic and a separate test set is still needed. The notes describe themselves as guidance, since tuning depends on the scenario, and they give no settings that are right for every dataset.

What the sources document

  • The tuning notes say that letting the model get more complicated, for example with more depth, gives it a better ability to fit the training data and results in a less biased model, that such a model requires more data to fit, and that most parameters in XGBoost are about the bias-variance tradeoff. [12]
  • For overfitting, described as high training accuracy with low test accuracy, the notes give two approaches: directly controlling model complexity, with parameters including max_depth, min_child_weight and gamma, and adding randomness with subsample and colsample_bytree. They also suggest reducing the step size eta and increasing num_round when doing so. [12]
  • The parameter reference gives max_depth a default of 6 and says that increasing it makes the model more complex and more likely to overfit. It gives eta a default of 0.3 and describes it as step size shrinkage used to prevent overfitting, and says larger values of gamma and min_child_weight make the algorithm more conservative. [13]
  • Early stopping requires at least one evaluation set in evals and uses the last one if there are several. xgboost.train() returns a model from the last iteration, not the best one, and the best round is available as best_iteration and can be passed to predict through iteration_range. [14]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

AlexNet on ImageNet: 60 million parameters against 1.2 million images

University of TorontoDocumented in a paper

"ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks" by Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton describes a convolutional network, a variant of which won the ILSVRC-2012 image classification competition. Its section 4, "Reducing Overfitting", documents how the authors kept a very high-capacity model from overfitting its training set. [15]

The problem

Recognising 1,000 object categories needs a model with enough capacity to represent them, and the authors report that depth seems to be important: removing any one convolutional layer made results worse. A network that large has far more parameters than there are labelled images, and the paper states that its size made overfitting a significant problem even with 1.2 million training examples. A smaller network would have overfitted less at the cost of accuracy.

Where this topic fits

Capacity is deliberately kept high, to keep bias low, and the variance that follows is attacked by the two other routes from this lesson. Data augmentation raises the effective number of training rows. Dropout, which the paper presents as an efficient form of combining many models, averages variance away. Both act during training and leave the architecture unchanged.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Choose a deep, wide network of five convolutional and three fully connected layers with 60 million parameters, because the authors found that depth seemed to be important for accuracy.
  2. Recognise the imbalance between parameters and labelled images, which the authors say cannot be learned without considerable overfitting.
  3. Enlarge the training set with label-preserving transformations: random 224 × 224 crops of each 256 × 256 image and their mirror images, generated on the fly so that nothing extra is stored.
  4. Add a second augmentation that shifts the intensity of the red, green and blue channels along their principal components, so the model cannot rely on exact colour or brightness.
  5. Apply dropout to the first two fully connected layers: on every training pass each hidden unit is zeroed with probability 0.5, so no unit can depend on particular other units being present.
  6. At test time use every unit with its output halved, and average the predictions over ten crops of each image.

Why this approach fits

Cutting capacity would have swapped variance for bias, and the authors report that even one missing convolutional layer cost accuracy. Holding capacity fixed and reducing variance was the better trade. Augmentation suits images because shifting or mirroring a photograph leaves its label unchanged, so new training rows cost almost nothing. Dropout suits large fully connected layers, where most of the parameters sit, and gives much of the benefit of averaging many networks for roughly twice the training time of one.

Limitation or tradeoff

Augmented images are not new information. The paper itself calls them highly interdependent, and they can only teach the invariances built into the transformations: mirroring is harmless for photographs of objects and wrong for text or digits. Dropout roughly doubled the number of iterations needed to converge. For the crop augmentation and for dropout the paper says only that the network overfits substantially without them and gives no measured difference in test error, so the size of each contribution cannot be read from it.

What the sources document

  • The network has 60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons in five convolutional and three fully connected layers. It was trained on the 1.2 million images and 1000 classes of ILSVRC-2010, where it reached top-1 and top-5 test error rates of 37.5 % and 17.0 %. [15]
  • The authors write that the size of the network made overfitting a significant problem even with 1.2 million labelled training examples, and that the 10 bits of constraint each training example imposes are insufficient to learn so many parameters without considerable overfitting. [15]
  • Extracting random 224 × 224 patches and their horizontal reflections from the 256 × 256 images increases the size of the training set by a factor of 2048, with highly interdependent examples. Without this scheme the network suffers from substantial overfitting, which the authors say would have forced them to use much smaller networks. [15]
  • Dropout sets the output of each hidden neuron to zero with probability 0.5 and is used in the first two fully connected layers. Without it the network exhibits substantial overfitting, and it roughly doubles the number of iterations required to converge. The colour augmentation reduces the top-1 error rate by over 1 %. [15]
  • Removing any convolutional layer, each of which contains no more than 1 % of the model's parameters, resulted in inferior performance. [15]

Example 3 of 5

Google Flu Trends: 50 million candidate queries fitted to 1,152 data points

GoogleDocumented in a case study

Google Flu Trends (GFT) estimated influenza activity in the United States from search queries. Ginsberg and colleagues at Google and the CDC described how it was built in Nature in 2009. Lazer, Kennedy, King and Vespignani analysed its later errors in Science in 2014. [16][17]

The problem

Official figures for influenza-like illness (ILI) from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) appeared with a lag of one to two weeks. GFT aimed to estimate the current week from search behaviour instead. The modelling problem was to find, among the 50 million most common search queries, those whose weekly frequency tracks ILI, with only a few years of weekly data to learn from.

Where this topic fits

The capacity in this system is not in the final model, which has one explanatory variable and two coefficients. It is in the search that built that variable. Picking the best of 50 million candidates against 128 weekly points per region is an extremely flexible procedure, and some candidates will match by coincidence. The builders controlled this with cross-validation and a held-out season. The later analysis shows what that validation could not catch.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Compute weekly query fractions for the 50 million most common queries in each region, and take the CDC's weekly ILI percentage in nine regions as the target.
  2. Score every query alone: fit a one-variable model per region with 4-fold cross-validation and average the correlations on the held-out weeks.
  3. Rank the queries by score, then choose how many of the top ones to sum into a single feature by out-of-sample fit. The best number was 45.
  4. Fit the final two-coefficient model on all 1,152 training points and check it on 42 held-out weeks per region, where the estimates correlated strongly with CDC data.
  5. Lazer and co-authors later called the initial version part flu detector and part winter detector: seasonal terms unrelated to flu, such as high school basketball, correlated with the CDC data, and the developers report weeding such terms out. The original model missed the 2009 pandemic, which arrived outside the usual season, and the version updated that year was high for 100 of 108 weeks from August 2011.
  6. Lazer and co-authors compared GFT with a simple baseline built from lagged CDC data, the check this lesson recommends, and with a model that combined the two. The combined model had the lowest error of the three.

Why this approach fits

The case shows that overfitting can come from the search for features and not only from the number of parameters in the final model. A two-coefficient model looks too simple to overfit, yet the procedure that chose its input examined 50 million alternatives. When counting capacity, count everything that was tried.

It also shows the limit of validation. The held-out weeks came from the same kind of seasons as the training weeks, so a query that merely tracks winter passed the check. Validation data protect only against the failures they contain.

Limitation or tradeoff

Lazer and co-authors discuss two issues behind GFT's errors: big data hubris, where they raise overfitting, and algorithm dynamics, the changes that Google's engineers and users made to the search service after the model was built, which altered the data-generating process. The second is a shift in the data, not a capacity problem, and no validation on past seasons would have revealed it. The authors add that GFT never documented the 45 search terms, so outside analysts could not separate the causes precisely.

What the sources document

  • Ginsberg et al. computed weekly time series for 50 million of the most common US search queries and tested each one separately as the single explanatory variable for the CDC's ILI percentage in each of nine regions, fitting 450 million models in total. [16]
  • The number of top-scoring queries was chosen for the best fit on out-of-sample data, and summing the top 45 queries gave that fit. Model performance drops steeply after adding query 81, "oscar nominations", and other queries in the top 100 that were left out covered topics such as high school basketball, which tend to coincide with the influenza season. [16]
  • The final model was regressed over 1152 points, the 128 training weeks from each of nine regions, and validated on 42 further weeks per region that were excluded from all earlier steps, where its estimates had a mean correlation of 0.97 with CDC-observed ILI percentages. [16]
  • Lazer et al. describe the method as finding the best matches among 50 million search terms to fit 1152 data points, say the odds of finding terms that match the flu but are structurally unrelated were quite high, and say that having to weed out seasonal terms such as high school basketball should have warned that the big data were overfitting. They call the initial version part flu detector, part winter detector, and report that it missed the 2009 A-H1N1 pandemic. [17]
  • Lazer et al. report that GFT was high in 100 out of 108 weeks starting with August 2011, and that mean absolute error in their out-of-sample period, with each error measured relative to the CDC estimate, was 0.486 for GFT, 0.311 for a model using lagged CDC data and 0.232 for a model combining GFT with lagged CDC data. [17]

Example 4 of 5

Microsoft Research: learning curves out to a billion words before building new algorithms

Microsoft ResearchDocumented in a paper

Banko and Brill's ACL 2001 paper "Scaling to Very Very Large Corpora for Natural Language Disambiguation" reports learning-curve experiments done at Microsoft Research on confusion set disambiguation, the task of choosing between easily confused words such as "then" and "than" from the surrounding text. [18]

The problem

The work was partly motivated by the wish for a better grammar checker. With a fixed amount of time, the authors had to decide where effort would pay most: modifying standard learning algorithms, exploring new techniques, building more sophisticated features, or simply training an existing method on much more data. Published comparisons were made on corpora of a million words or fewer, so they could not answer the question.

Where this topic fits

A learning curve is the tool for this decision. Before changing any model, hold everything fixed except the amount of training data, measure accuracy on one test set at each size, and see whether the curve has flattened. The curves come first and the decision about algorithms second.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Fix a test set of one million words of Wall Street Journal text and keep all Wall Street Journal text out of the training corpus.
  2. Assemble a training corpus of one billion words. Labels cost nothing for this task, because the correct word is visible in any reasonably well-edited text.
  3. Train each of four learners (winnow, perceptron, naive Bayes and a simple memory-based learner) on growing cut-offs of the corpus, from the first million words up to the full billion.
  4. At each size, average test accuracy over ten confusion sets and plot it against corpus size on a logarithmic axis.
  5. Read the curves. Every learner is still improving at a billion words, and none is close to flat at the corpus sizes the field normally used.
  6. Draw the conclusion for the project: weigh spending on data against spending on algorithms, and account for the cost that larger training sets bring, which here was a much larger learned model.

Why this approach fits

A learning curve turns "would more data help?" from an opinion into a measurement, and it does so before any engineering time is spent on new models. In this lesson's terms, curves that are still rising say the learners were limited by variance, by how little data they had seen, more than by their capacity. Running four learners side by side also showed that the answer did not depend on the choice of algorithm, which is the information a team needs when choosing where to invest.

Limitation or tradeoff

The result depends on labels being free. The authors themselves note that very few problems have annotated data of this size at no cost, and they go on to study active learning and unsupervised methods for that reason. A log-linear curve also means each further step in accuracy needs about ten times more data, so the curve says more data helps without saying it is worth the price. And a learning curve is a measurement up to the largest size tried; extending it beyond that is a guess.

What the sources document

  • The authors say the work was partially motivated by the desire to develop an improved grammar checker, and that before exploring more expensive options such as modifying learning algorithms or using more sophisticated features they first tested what happened if an existing method was trained with much more data. [18]
  • They collected a 1-billion-word training corpus, three orders of magnitude larger than the largest previously used for this problem, tested on 1 million words of Wall Street Journal text with no Wall Street Journal data in training, and trained each learner at several cut-off points from the first one million words up to all one billion. [18]
  • Learning curves are shown for winnow, perceptron, naive Bayes and a very simple memory-based learner, each point being the average over ten confusion sets. The curves appear to be log-linear even out to one billion words, and none of the learners is close to asymptoting at the training corpus size commonly employed by the field. [18]
  • The authors conclude that the results suggest reconsidering the trade-off between spending time and money on algorithm development and spending it on corpus development, and they note that the gains do not come for free because the size of the learned representations grows with the training data. [18]

Example 5 of 5

Keras EarlyStopping: ending training when the validation metric stops improving

KerasDocumented in official docs

Keras is a deep learning API, and its EarlyStopping callback is documented on keras.io. TensorFlow's official tutorial "Overfit and underfit" uses the callback while training networks of four sizes on the Higgs dataset. [19][20]

The problem

A neural network trained by gradient descent fits more of its training set with every epoch (one pass over the training data). The TensorFlow tutorial describes the usual result: accuracy on validation data peaks after a number of epochs and then stagnates or starts to fall. Training for a fixed, generous number of epochs therefore returns an overfitted model, and the right number is not known in advance.

Where this topic fits

The number of epochs is a capacity setting, and EarlyStopping chooses it from a validation curve that is drawn while training runs. The callback sits inside the fit loop. After each epoch it compares the monitored validation metric with the best value seen so far and decides whether training continues.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Hold out validation data and pass it to fit, so that a validation metric such as val_loss is computed at the end of every epoch.
  2. Create the callback with the metric to monitor, a patience (how many epochs without improvement to tolerate) and, if wanted, a min_delta (the smallest change that counts as an improvement).
  3. After each epoch the callback compares the metric with its best value so far. An improvement resets its counter, and any other result adds one to it.
  4. When the counter reaches the patience, training stops, however many epochs were requested.
  5. Set restore_best_weights so that the model keeps the weights from its best epoch instead of the weights from the last, slightly overfitted one.
  6. Report final performance on a test set that played no part in the stopping decision.

Why this approach fits

Choosing the number of epochs by trial would mean one full training run per candidate. Early stopping gets the whole validation curve over training time from a single run, because the model after epoch 20 is on the way to the model after epoch 50. It needs no change to the network and works for any model trained step by step, which is why boosting libraries offer the same mechanism over rounds. The patience setting acknowledges that validation loss is itself noisy and should not end training on one bad epoch.

Limitation or tradeoff

The defaults are easy to misuse. With patience=0 training ends at the first epoch that fails to improve, which can be a random fluctuation, and with restore_best_weights=False the model keeps the weights of the last epoch, not the best one. The stopping epoch is chosen on the validation set, so the validation score at that epoch is optimistic. Early stopping also limits training time only. A network far too large for its data overfits within a few epochs, which is what the tutorial reports for its larger models, so stopping early is used together with a smaller network, weight penalties or dropout and does not replace them.

What the sources document

  • The Keras documentation says EarlyStopping stops training when a monitored metric has stopped improving, that the check is made at the end of every epoch, and that the monitored quantity defaults to val_loss. [19]
  • patience is the number of epochs with no improvement after which training will be stopped and defaults to 0; min_delta is the minimum change in the monitored quantity that qualifies as an improvement and defaults to 0. [19]
  • restore_best_weights defaults to False, in which case the weights obtained at the last step of training are used; when it is True the weights from the epoch with the best value of the monitored quantity are restored. start_from_epoch sets a number of warm-up epochs before monitoring begins. [19]
  • In the documented example, with monitor set to loss and patience set to 3, a fit call that asks for 10 epochs runs only 4. [19]
  • The TensorFlow tutorial uses the Higgs dataset of 11,000,000 examples with 28 features, takes 1,000 examples for validation and 10,000 for training, trains with EarlyStopping(monitor='val_binary_crossentropy', patience=200), and reports that typically only its Tiny model avoids overfitting altogether while each larger model overfits more quickly. [20]

4Common mistakes

  1. Choosing the model size, polynomial degree or number of training rounds by the training score.

    Why it causes problems
    Training error never rises when capacity is added, so this rule always picks the most flexible model on offer. In the worked example it picks degree 9, whose training MSE is 0.0003 and whose validation MSE of 0.320 is worse than the straight line's.
    How to recognise it
    The reported score was computed on rows that were used for fitting, or the chosen setting is the most flexible one that was tried. A nearly perfect training score on noisy data is itself a warning.
    How to fix it
    Plot a validation curve: training and cross-validated error against the capacity setting. Take the setting with the lowest validation error, or the simplest setting whose validation error is within the fold-to-fold spread of the lowest.

  2. Collecting or labelling more data for a model that is underfitting.

    Why it causes problems
    More rows reduce variance, and an underfitted model has little variance to remove. In the worked example the straight line's expected test MSE moves only from 0.298 with 11 rows to 0.277 with 161, while the cubic's is already 0.122 with 11. The labelling budget buys almost nothing.
    How to recognise it
    The learning curve's training and validation lines have already met, and both sit well above the error you need. Training error is about as poor as validation error.
    How to fix it
    Lower bias instead: add informative features, raise capacity or weaken the penalty, then plot the learning curve again. Ask for more data only when a clear gap remains at the largest training size.

  3. Tuning against one validation set again and again, then quoting its best score as the expected performance.

    Why it causes problems
    Every comparison fits a little of the validation set's noise, so the best score found is biased upward; the scikit-learn guide warns that a validation score used for tuning is no longer a good estimate of generalization. Google Flu Trends shows the scale of the problem: it scored 50 million candidate queries by cross-validation, so its best cross-validated scores were bound to be optimistic, and its authors kept 42 weeks out of every earlier step for a final check. That check (a mean correlation of 0.97) came from a regular winter season and could not warn of the nonseasonal 2009 pandemic the system later missed.
    How to recognise it
    The number of configurations tried is large compared with the number of validation rows, the same split has been reused for weeks, or the first run on fresh data scores clearly worse than validation did.
    How to fix it
    Set aside a test set and use it once, after every choice has been made. With little data use nested cross-validation. Report how many configurations were tried alongside the score.

  4. Calling a model healthy because its training and validation errors are close together.

    Why it causes problems
    A small gap rules out overfitting and nothing else. An underfitted model also has a small gap: the straight line in the worked example scores 0.284 and 0.310, close together and both more than three times the noise floor. The model ships with error that a better model would have removed.
    How to recognise it
    The report contains no baseline. Compare with predicting the mean or the majority class, and with one more flexible model. If the flexible model's validation error is much lower, the first was underfitting.
    How to fix it
    Report three numbers together every time: a naive baseline, training error and validation error. Then try one step up in capacity and check whether validation error falls.

  5. Keeping the model from the last epoch or boosting round instead of the best one.

    Why it causes problems
    Validation error usually passes its minimum before training ends, so the final model is more overfitted than the best one seen. xgboost.train returns the model from the last iteration, and Keras EarlyStopping keeps the last weights unless restore_best_weights is set.
    How to recognise it
    The validation metric in the training log was lower at an earlier epoch or round than at the end, or the saved model's validation score does not match the best value in the log.
    How to fix it
    Monitor a validation metric during training, stop with a patience of several epochs or rounds, and select the best iteration: restore_best_weights=True in Keras, or best_iteration with iteration_range in XGBoost.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score, learning_curve, validation_curve
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True)
cv = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
rmse = "neg_root_mean_squared_error"

# 1. Reference points
for name, model in [("predict the mean", DummyRegressor()), ("linear regression", LinearRegression())]:
    print(f"{name}: validation RMSE {-cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring=rmse).mean():.1f}")

# 2. Validation curve over tree depth
depths = np.arange(1, 11)
train_scores, val_scores = validation_curve(
    DecisionTreeRegressor(random_state=0), X, y,
    param_name="max_depth", param_range=depths, cv=cv, scoring=rmse)
print("depth  train RMSE  validation RMSE")
for depth, tr, va in zip(depths, -train_scores.mean(axis=1), -val_scores.mean(axis=1)):
    print(f"{depth:5d}  {tr:10.1f}  {va:15.1f}")

# 3. Learning curves for a shallow tree and an unlimited one
for depth in [2, None]:
    sizes, train_scores, val_scores = learning_curve(
        DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=depth, random_state=0), X, y,
        train_sizes=[0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0], cv=cv, scoring=rmse,
        shuffle=True, random_state=0)
    print(f"max_depth={depth}")
    for n, tr, va in zip(sizes, np.abs(train_scores).mean(axis=1), np.abs(val_scores).mean(axis=1)):
        print(f"  n={n:3d}  train RMSE {tr:5.1f}  validation RMSE {va:5.1f}")

Show solution

A: underfitting. The gap is 1,500, about 2.5 % of the training error, so there is no overfitting to speak of. Both errors are high: the model removes only about a fifth of the baseline error of 80,000. The flat learning curve says the two lines met long ago. First action: lower bias with more informative features (location, interactions between size and area) or a more flexible model. Wasted: buying more rows, or adding a penalty, which would raise bias further.

B: overfitting. The gap is 18.8 percentage points, with training error almost zero. Doubling the data cut validation error from 27 % to 19 %, so the learning curve is still falling: the error is mostly variance. First action: more images or augmentation, together with dropout or a weight penalty. Wasted: a larger network or more epochs, which add capacity where it is already in excess.

C: overtraining. At round 2,000 the gap is 0.405. Validation loss was lowest at round 180 (0.412) and has risen by 0.043 since, while training loss kept falling. The model that training returned is worse than one it passed through earlier. First action: early stopping on the validation set and prediction from the best round; after that, a smaller step size or shallower trees. Wasted: more rounds.

D: near the noise floor. The gap is 0.3 percentage points, and the validation error of 4.4 % is about the rate at which human reviewers disagree with each other. If the labels themselves are uncertain for about 4 % of claims, little of the remaining error is the model's to remove. First action: stop tuning, confirm once on the test set, and if lower error is needed, improve the labels by having disputed claims reviewed again. Wasted: a bigger model or more rows labelled the same way.

E: not a fitting problem. The gap is 0.01 AUC, so the model generalises well to data like its training data. The drop to 0.74 comes from rows three months later: customers or features have changed, or a feature that was informative in spring is not available or not stable in autumn. The random split could not show this, because its validation rows come from the same months as the training rows. First action: validate on a later period than the training period, find the features whose relationship to churn changed, and plan to retrain on recent data. Wasted: a stronger penalty or a smaller model tuned on the random split.

The pattern across the five: the two error columns never gave the answer alone. Each diagnosis needed a reference point, which was a baseline in A, a second training size in B, an earlier round in C, a noise level in D and later data in E.

Check your answer

  • Did you label exactly one report as underfitting and say why more rows would not help it?
  • Did you notice that the best model in report C is the one from round 180, not the one training returned?
  • Did you resist proposing regularisation for report E and look at the split instead?
  • Did every diagnosis cite a number from the last column?
