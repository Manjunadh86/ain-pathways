k-Means Clustering

k-means groups unlabelled data points into k clusters. It starts from k centroids, assigns every point to its nearest centroid, moves each centroid to the mean of the points it received, and repeats until no assignment changes, which steadily lowers the total squared distance from points to their centroids.

1Overview

Why it matters

k-means is the usual first answer to "what groups are in this table?" when the rows are numeric and nobody has labelled them: customer or station profiles, regions with similar attributes, a short palette for an image, the coarse cells of a vector-search index. It is cheap, it scales to very large tables, and its result is easy to hand over, because each of the k centroids is an average member of its group.

The algorithm always returns k groups, whether or not the data contain any. What goes wrong is seldom the computation. Features on different scales quietly decide the result, k is picked by habit, one unlucky start ends in a poor local optimum, or crescent and ring shapes are cut by straight boundaries and the output is reported as discovered structure. Analysts, ML engineers and anyone who builds on cluster labels need to know which of these assumptions their data break.

  • Choose k by combining an inertia curve (the elbow), the silhouette score and the purpose the clusters will serve.
  • Standardise features before clustering and explain what goes wrong when you do not.
  • Recognise data shapes on which k-means fails (non-spherical clusters, uneven spread, outliers) and name the next method to try.

Key terms

Cluster
A group of points that the algorithm treats as similar. In k-means every point belongs to exactly one cluster.
Centroid
The mean of all points in a cluster, computed coordinate by coordinate. It usually is not one of the data points.
Inertia
The sum, over all points, of the squared distance to the nearest centroid. Also called the within-cluster sum of squares. k-means tries to make it small.
Assignment step
The half of each iteration in which every point is given to its nearest centroid.
Update step
The half of each iteration in which every centroid moves to the mean of the points assigned to it.
k-means++
A way to choose the starting centroids so that they tend to be far apart, by picking each new one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen.
Local optimum
A clustering that the assign-and-update loop cannot improve by itself, although a better clustering exists elsewhere.
Elbow method
Plotting inertia against k and choosing the k after which the curve flattens.
Silhouette coefficient
A score between -1 and 1 that compares how close a point is to its own cluster with how close it is to the nearest other cluster. Higher means better separated.

2Explanation

Introduction

Imagine a box of 200 photographs with no captions, and a request to sort them into five piles. You would probably pick five photos as rough examples of the piles, put every other photo with the example it most resembles, and then step back. Now each pile has contents, so which photo is the most typical one? Take that as the new example and sort again. After a few rounds the piles stop changing. That is k-means.

In data terms each photo is a row of numbers, a point. The number of piles is k, and the typical member of a pile is its centroid, the mean of its points. A cluster is the set of points whose nearest centroid is the same. Clustering is unsupervised: there are no labels to learn from, so the only information available is how close points are to each other. "Close" means ordinary (Euclidean) distance, which is why the columns you feed in, and their scales, decide what the algorithm finds. The preparation covered in feature engineering and scaling is therefore part of k-means, not an optional extra.

The loop has two alternating steps. From k starting centroids, the assignment step gives every point to its nearest centroid, and the update step moves every centroid to the mean of the points it received. Repeat until no point changes cluster. This is Lloyd's algorithm, which is what libraries usually mean by "k-means".

Each step lowers, or at worst keeps, one number: the total squared distance from every point to its own centroid, called the inertia. The loop therefore always ends, but it ends at a local optimum, a state it cannot improve by itself that may not be the best clustering available. Much of practical k-means follows from that fact: where to start, how many restarts to run, and how to choose k, which the algorithm cannot decide for you.

The rest of the lesson traces the algorithm on nine imaginary shop customers, compares starting points and values of k with exact numbers, shows five documented uses and then the data shapes on which k-means misleads. Curved, nested or noisy groups call for the methods in hierarchical and density-based clustering.

How it works

The objective: inertia

Given points x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_nx1​,…,xn​ and k centroids μ1,…,μk\mu_1, \dots, \mu_kμ1​,…,μk​, k-means looks for the centroids that minimise

J=∑i=1nmin⁡j∥xi−μj∥2J = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \min_{j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2J=i=1∑n​jmin​∥xi​−μj​∥2

Each point contributes its squared distance to the nearest centroid. Equivalently, split the points into clusters C1,…,CkC_1, \dots, C_kC1​,…,Ck​ and let each μj\mu_jμj​ be the mean of CjC_jCj​; then JJJ is the total squared distance of the points to their own cluster's mean. scikit-learn calls JJJ the inertia, or within-cluster sum of squares (opens in a new tab), and the k-means++ paper (opens in a new tab) calls it the potential. The paper also notes that finding the exact minimum is NP-hard even for two clusters, meaning no known algorithm is guaranteed to find it quickly, which is why practice relies on a local search.

Lloyd's algorithm

  1. Initialise. Choose k starting centroids, most simply k of the data points.
  2. Assign. Give every point to the nearest centroid (smallest squared distance, ties to the lower index).
  3. Update. Replace each centroid with the mean of the points assigned to it. If a cluster received no points, implementations keep or re-seed its centroid.
  4. Stop when no point changes cluster. scikit-learn stops when the centroids move by less than a relative tolerance (tol, default 0.0001) or after max_iter iterations (default 300).

Neither step 2 nor step 3 can raise JJJ. With the centroids fixed, the nearest centroid is by definition the cheapest choice for each point. With the assignments fixed, the mean is the best centre for a group, because for any point zzz and any set SSS with mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ

For the group (1,2),(2,1),(2,3)(1,2), (2,1), (2,3)(1,2),(2,1),(2,3) and z=(1,2)z = (1,2)z=(1,2) the left side is 4.0; on the right, 2.667 for the mean (1.667,2)(1.667, 2)(1.667,2) plus 3×0.4443 \times 0.4443×0.444. Moving the centre from a data point to the mean saves exactly the extra term. Since JJJ never rises and there are finitely many ways to split nnn points, the loop must stop. It stops at a local optimum, and scikit-learn's guide says plainly that the result depends strongly on the initial centroids.

In practice you call a library. In scikit-learn 1.9.1 the defaults are n_clusters=8, init='k-means++', n_init='auto', max_iter=300, tol=0.0001; the KMeans reference (opens in a new tab) gives the average cost as O(knT)O(knT)O(knT) for nnn samples and TTT iterations.

Initialisation: where the loop starts decides where it ends

Choosing k data points uniformly at random can put two starting centroids inside one group and none in another, and Lloyd's loop will not repair that. k-means++ spreads the seeds out. The first seed is a uniformly random point. Every later seed is drawn with probability proportional to the squared distance D(x)2D(x)^2D(x)2 from the point to its nearest seed so far:

P(x)=D(x)2∑x′D(x′)2P(x) = \frac{D(x)^2}{\sum_{x'} D(x')^2}P(x)=∑x′​D(x′)2D(x)2​

Points already chosen have D=0D = 0D=0, so they cannot be picked again, and far-away points are the most likely. The paper proves that this seeding alone gives an expected inertia of at most 8(ln⁡k+2)8(\ln k + 2)8(lnk+2) times the optimum, a guarantee plain random seeding does not have, and reports on its test datasets lower inertia and faster runs; on two real datasets the inertia was 20 to 1,000 times smaller. scikit-learn's default is a "greedy" variant that tries several candidates at each draw. It runs once by default with k-means++ and ten times with random. Restarting and keeping the run with the lowest inertia is the second line of defence. The worked example puts exact numbers on both.

Choosing k

k is an input. The algorithm will happily split any data into any number of clusters, and JJJ falls as k grows until it reaches zero at k=nk = nk=n, so the lowest inertia is never the answer. Three aids help:

  • Elbow. Run k-means for k=1,2,3,…k = 1, 2, 3, \dotsk=1,2,3,… and plot JJJ. Google's clustering course suggests the k at which the slope first changes drastically. The method fails when the data are not clearly clustered: Yellowbrick (opens in a new tab) warns that the curve may be smooth, with no visible elbow.
  • Silhouette. For a point let aaa be its mean distance to the other points of its own cluster and bbb its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. Then s=(b−a)/max⁡(a,b)s = (b - a) / \max(a, b)s=(b−a)/max(a,b), from −1-1−1 to 111. Near 1 means well inside its cluster, near 0 means on a border, negative means probably in the wrong cluster. The mean over all points is the score, defined only for 2≤k≤n−12 \le k \le n - 12≤k≤n−1. scikit-learn's own silhouette example finds the average highest at k = 2 (0.705) with k = 4 second (0.651); the page calls the choice between them ambivalent and points to the similar cluster sizes at k = 4, so the score informs a decision and does not make it.
  • Other indices and the purpose. ArcGIS Pro scans k from 2 to 30 with the Calinski-Harabasz pseudo F-statistic (a ratio of between-cluster to within-cluster dispersion, higher is better), and BigQuery ML's hyperparameter tuning scores candidate values of k with the Davies-Bouldin index (average similarity between clusters, lower is better) by default. Google's course also advises checking whether downstream performance improves, and DeepCluster (below) picked its k that way.

Prepare the features

Euclidean distance adds squared differences across columns, so a column measured in dollars outweighs one measured in years. Standardise each column (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) before clustering. ArcGIS Pro does this by default and BigQuery ML's STANDARDIZE_FEATURES defaults to true. When there are very many dimensions, Euclidean distances become less informative, and scikit-learn suggests reducing them first, for example with PCA.

What k-means assumes, and where it fails

Minimising squared distance to a mean favours compact, roughly round clusters of similar spread. scikit-learn's guide says inertia assumes clusters are convex and isotropic and responds poorly to elongated clusters or irregular shapes, and its assumptions example shows the same weakness for clusters of unequal variance. Two interlocking crescents are the clearest case:

k-means draws one straight boundary between its two centroids, and that boundary slices through both crescents. The edge cases below give the numbers. A Gaussian mixture model, which fits one bell-shaped cloud with its own spread to each cluster, copes with elliptical clusters, and the next lesson covers methods for arbitrary shapes. For very large tables, MiniBatchKMeans updates the centroids from small random batches. It converges faster than KMeans at some cost in quality, a difference scikit-learn says can be quite small in practice.

Step 5: run it

python
points = [(1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 3), (6, 8), (7, 9), (8, 8), (9, 2), (10, 3), (11, 1)]

def sq_dist(a, b):
    return sum((u - v) ** 2 for u, v in zip(a, b))

def mean_point(group):
    return tuple(sum(coords) / len(group) for coords in zip(*group))

def lloyd(points, centers):
    """Lloyd's algorithm. Returns (labels, centers, inertia)."""
    labels = None
    while True:
        # Assignment step: index of the nearest centre for every point
        new_labels = [min(range(len(centers)), key=lambda j: sq_dist(p, centers[j])) for p in points]
        if new_labels == labels:          # no point changed cluster: converged
            break
        labels = new_labels
        # Update step: every centre moves to the mean of its points
        groups = [[p for p, lab in zip(points, labels) if lab == j] for j in range(len(centers))]
        centers = [mean_point(g) if g else c for g, c in zip(groups, centers)]
    inertia = sum(sq_dist(p, centers[lab]) for p, lab in zip(points, labels))
    return labels, centers, inertia

for name, seeds in [("good start (P1, P4, P7)", [points[0], points[3], points[6]]),
                    ("bad start  (P4, P8, P9)", [points[3], points[7], points[8]])]:
    labels, centers, inertia = lloyd(points, seeds)
    print(f"{name}: inertia = {inertia:.3f}")
    print("  labels :", labels)
    print("  centres:", [tuple(round(c, 3) for c in ctr) for ctr in centers])

Edge cases and limitations

Features measured on different scales
Eight customers as (income in dollars, age): (35000, 24), (41000, 58), (48000, 27), (56000, 62), (63000, 25), (71000, 60), (78000, 29), (86000, 64). On the raw values the first customer is 6,000.1 away from the 58-year-old earning 41,000 and 13,000.0 away from the 27-year-old earning 48,000. The 34-year age gap adds only 1,156 to a squared distance of about 36 million, so k = 2 splits low incomes from high incomes and ignores age. After standardising, the same two distances are 1.98 and 0.79 and the split follows age instead.
Crescents, rings and other non-convex shapes
Place 12 points on each of two interlocking crescents, at (cos t, sin t) and (1 - cos t, 0.5 - sin t) for 12 evenly spaced values of t from 0 to pi. Started from each of the 276 pairs of the 24 points, k-means with k = 2 ends in one of four clusterings, and the best, reached from 76 of the pairs, has inertia 9.79 with 6 of the 24 points on the wrong side. The true crescents score a worse inertia, 15.94, and a worse mean silhouette (0.24 against 0.46). The scores reward the compact cut. When the shape is the point, use another method.
Clusters of different spread or size
scikit-learn's assumptions example says k-means is equivalent to taking the maximum likelihood estimator for a mixture of k Gaussians with the same variances but possibly different means. With one shared spread, a broad cluster next to a tight one is separated by a boundary equidistant from the two centroids, not at the natural gap. The example builds blobs with standard deviations 1.0, 2.5 and 0.5 and shows this, and for blob sizes of 500, 100 and 10 it recommends more restarts (n_init=10) to avoid a poor local optimum. A `GaussianMixture` model, which allows a separate variance per cluster, copes better.
Empty clusters and collapsing centroids
If no point is nearest to a centroid, the mean of its cluster is undefined. Libraries add rules for this. The Faiss documentation mentions heuristics that stop centroids from collapsing, and DeepCluster re-seeds an empty cluster from a randomly chosen non-empty one with a small random perturbation. In your own implementation keep the old centroid at least, and count the points per cluster after fitting to spot a cluster that stays at size 0 or 1.
A few extreme points drag the means
A centroid is an average, so one distant point moves it. Add a customer at (30, 2) to the group (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 3) and its centroid moves from (1.667, 2) to (8.75, 2), outside the range of the original three points. Outliers can claim a centroid of their own, or distort neighbouring clusters. Look at the points farthest from their centroid after fitting. ArcGIS Pro offers k-medoids, which is more robust to noise and outliers but slower, and density-based methods can label noise explicitly.
A smooth inertia curve, or scores that disagree
On data without clear groups the inertia curve is smooth and has no elbow, as the Yellowbrick documentation warns. Silhouette and the elbow can also point to different k. In scikit-learn's example k = 2 has the highest average silhouette (0.705) while k = 4 scores 0.651, and the page calls the choice between 2 and 4 ambivalent, noting that at k = 4 the clusters are of similar size. Take such a disagreement as a sign that the answer depends on what the clusters are for, and check how stable the clusters are across seeds.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The columns are numeric, can be put on comparable scales, and you expect compact groups of similar spread, such as customer behaviour profiles or station usage profiles.
  • You have many rows and need a method whose cost grows about linearly with them. scikit-learn gives O(knT) on average, and a mini-batch variant exists for larger tables.
  • You want a small, reusable summary of the data, k centroids that can label new points with one nearest-centroid lookup, as in vector indexes, colour palettes and quantization.
  • You want a fast baseline clustering to compare with density-based or mixture models before choosing a more complex method.

Think twice when

  • Clusters are elongated, nested or curved (crescents, rings, long chains) or differ greatly in spread. Use a Gaussian mixture, hierarchical or density-based clustering.
  • Many points are outliers or noise that should not belong to any cluster. k-means forces every point into one. Use DBSCAN or HDBSCAN, or k-medoids.
  • The data are mostly categorical or text with no useful numeric embedding, so a mean has no meaning.
  • Distances in the raw feature space say little, for example hundreds of sparse dimensions that cannot be reduced first.

A simpler alternative. Group by an existing business rule or by quantile bins of one or two columns, for example spend terciles, and compare group averages. If such rules already separate the groups you care about, k-means adds nothing. It earns its place when no single column separates them and there are more columns than you can inspect by eye.

Key takeaways

  • k-means alternates between assigning each point to its nearest centroid and moving each centroid to the mean of its points, and neither step can raise the inertia, so it always stops.
  • It stops at a local optimum that depends on the starting centroids, so use k-means++ seeding, run several restarts and keep the run with the lowest inertia.
  • k is an input and not a result, because inertia falls as k grows; combine the elbow, the silhouette score and the purpose of the clusters, and expect judgement.
  • Distances decide everything, so standardise features before clustering and reduce very high-dimensional data first.
  • k-means assumes compact, roughly round clusters of similar spread; with crescents, rings, outliers or uneven variance it still returns k clusters, and they can be wrong.
  • The centroids are a cheap summary of a dataset, which is why k-means also works inside other systems such as vector indexes, palette extraction and self-supervised training.

Example 1 of 5

BigQuery ML: segmenting London bike-hire stations with a k-means model

Google Cloud (BigQuery ML)Documented in official docs

BigQuery ML trains k-means models with SQL inside the data warehouse. Google's tutorial clusters London bicycle-hire stations, and the CREATE MODEL reference for k-means lists the options and their defaults. [9][10]

The problem

Trip records sit in a table with one row per trip, but the operator's questions are about kinds of stations: which are busy and central, which are quiet, which see long rentals. Nobody has labelled stations with a type, and the people asking work in SQL. The tutorial's goal is descriptive: find groups of similar stations so that decisions, such as where to trial a new type of lock or which stations to stock with racing bikes, can be aimed at a group.

Where this topic fits

k-means is the modelling step after SQL feature engineering. A query turns trip rows into one feature row per station and day type; CREATE MODEL with model_type = 'kmeans' fits the centroids; the centroids are then read as station profiles, and ML.PREDICT assigns stations to clusters.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Aggregate the trip rows into one row per station and weekday or weekend, with the average rental duration, the number of trips and the distance from the city centre computed with geography functions.
  2. Leave out the station name and the weekday or weekend flag with EXCEPT, as the tutorial does, so that only the three numeric columns enter the clustering.
  3. Set num_clusters to 4 in the OPTIONS clause. The tutorial does not say why four; left unset, BigQuery ML defaults to log10 of the number of training rows, within the allowed range of 2 to 100.
  4. Let BigQuery ML apply its documented defaults, which include standardising numeric features, random centroid initialisation, at most 20 passes over the data and early stopping when a pass improves the loss by less than 1 percent.
  5. Read the four centroids on the model's Evaluation tab, where the tutorial describes them as two kinds of quiet city station, a busy station near the centre, and a suburban station with longer trips.
  6. Run ML.PREDICT on chosen stations to see which cluster they fall in, then pick stations for the lock trial or the racing bikes.

Why this approach fits

The data already live in the warehouse, the question is descriptive and unlabelled, and k-means needs only distances to k centroids, so it can be offered as one SQL statement with sensible defaults such as standardisation while the analyst keeps control of k. A centroid is also an easy hand-over to a business reader: each of the four is a short profile such as "busy and close to the centre".

Limitation or tradeoff

The tutorial gives no reason for k = 4, and the model will return four clusters whether or not stations form four natural groups. The documented default initialisation is random, so retraining can give different centroids; the reference says to choose CUSTOM initialisation to reuse the same centroids. It also notes that changing the order of columns in the SELECT can change the final centroids. The readings of the centroids ("less busy city station") are the analyst's interpretation of averages.

What the sources document

  • It describes the four centroids as a less busy city station with shorter rentals, a less busy city station with longer rentals, a busy station close to the centre, and a suburban station with longer trips, and suggests using them to choose where to test a new lock or stock racing bikes. [9]
  • The reference says KMEANS models are for data segmentation such as identifying customer segments, that training needs no labels, and that NUM_CLUSTERS accepts 2 to 100 and defaults to log10 of the number of training examples. [10]

ArcGIS Pro Multivariate Clustering: grouping map features by attributes

Esri (ArcGIS Pro)Documented in official docs

Esri's ArcGIS Pro includes the Multivariate Clustering geoprocessing tool, which uses k-means by default. The tool reference and the "How Multivariate Clustering works" page document its seeding, scaling, stopping rule and choice of k, and the reference includes a code sample. [11][12]

The problem

A GIS analyst has points, lines or polygons with several numeric attributes and wants groups of features that resemble each other, with no labelled examples and often no idea how many groups there are. The reference's own sample groups a district layer (District_Vandalism) on three attribute fields, named TOTPOP, VACANT_CY and UNEMP, into five clusters. The clusters are about attribute values, not about where the features sit on the map.

Where this topic fits

The tool wraps the whole workflow of this lesson around the algorithm: it scales the fields, seeds with k-means++, runs assign and update for at most 100 iterations, and, when the analyst leaves the number of clusters empty, repeats the run for every k from 2 to 30 and reports the best k by a pseudo F-statistic.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The analyst picks numeric analysis fields. Fields with no variation are dropped, and a categorical field has to be converted to numeric dummy variables first.
  2. Unless scaling is switched off, each field is scaled to unit variance so that fields with large values do not dominate the distances.
  3. With the default Optimized seed locations, the first seed is random and later seeds are drawn with a weighting that favours features far from the existing seeds, which the how-it-works page calls k-means++.
  4. Each feature is assigned to its closest seed in attribute space, a mean centre is computed for every cluster, and features are reassigned to the closest centre until membership stabilizes or 100 iterations pass.
  5. If the number of clusters is blank, the tool evaluates 2 through 30 clusters. For each k it solves ten times and keeps the highest pseudo F-statistic, because random seeds make single runs differ.
  6. The output has a CLUSTER_ID for every feature and an IS_SEED flag, plus messages and charts that describe each cluster, which the analyst maps and interprets.

Why this approach fits

Analysts in this setting have several numeric attributes on very different scales, no labels, and a need for groups they can colour on a map and describe in a sentence. k-means gives each feature one cluster ID and each cluster a mean profile, and it stays fast on large layers, which is why the reference recommends it for large datasets. The pseudo F-statistic gives a documented, repeatable way to pick k when no domain rule does, and ten solves per k with k-means++ seeding limit the run-to-run variation.

Limitation or tradeoff

The documentation is open about the weaknesses. The algorithm is a greedy heuristic that converges to a local minimum and not always the global one. With random seeds the result can differ between runs, the numbering of the clusters can change from one run to the next, and borderline features can switch cluster. The clusters are nonspatial, so nearby features need not share a cluster; for contiguous regions Esri points to a separate spatially constrained tool. Its best-practice advice is to start with a single analysis field and add more only when needed.

What the sources document

  • The tool finds natural clusters of features based solely on feature attribute values, accepts points, lines or polygons, and writes a CLUSTER_ID field plus an IS_SEED field marking the features used as starting points. [11]
  • K means is the default method; K medoids is more robust to noise and outliers, K means is generally faster, and K means is recommended for large datasets. [11]
  • With optimized seed locations the first seed is selected randomly and later seeds are weighted to be far from the existing ones in data space, a part of the algorithm the how-it-works page calls k-means++. Features are then assigned to the closest seed, mean centres are computed and features reassigned until membership stabilizes, up to a maximum of 100 iterations. [12][11]
  • When the number of clusters is left empty the tool computes a Calinski-Harabasz pseudo F-statistic for 2 through 30 clusters and, for each number, solves 10 times and uses the highest of the 10 values. The largest values mark the best-performing solutions. [11][12]

Example 3 of 5

Faiss: k-means centroids as the cells of a vector-search index

Meta (Faiss library)Documented in official docs

Faiss is an open-source library for similarity search over dense vectors. Its wiki pages on index types, on choosing an index and on k-means document how inverted-file (IVF) indexes use k-means to split the vectors into cells, and how many centroids and training vectors to use. [13][14][15]

The problem

Comparing a query with every stored vector is exact but its cost grows with the size of the collection. The Faiss documentation describes partitioning techniques such as k-means as a way to speed up the search at the cost of losing the guarantee of finding the exact nearest neighbour, because only a fraction of the vectors is compared with each query.

Where this topic fits

k-means runs once, when the index is trained. Its k centroids define nlist cells; each database vector is stored in the inverted list of its nearest centroid. At query time the same nearest-centroid computation, applied to the query, decides which nprobe lists to scan. The related lesson on vector databases covers the retrieval side.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Collect training vectors. The guidelines say k-means needs between 30 and 256 training vectors per centroid, and more is better.
  2. Choose nlist, the number of centroids k. Below one million vectors the guidelines recommend 4 to 16 times the square root of the collection size.
  3. Call train. Faiss runs k-means on the training vectors and adds the resulting centroids to the coarse quantizer, which is typically a flat index.
  4. Call add. Each database vector is assigned to its nearest centroid and appended to that centroid's inverted list.
  5. At query time the query is compared with the centroids, and only the vectors in the nprobe closest cells are compared with it.
  6. Raise nprobe to scan more cells for better recall, or lower it for speed. The wiki describes nprobe as a query-time setting for exactly this trade-off.

Why this approach fits

An IVF index needs a partition that follows where the vectors actually lie, with cells of workable size, and a cheap rule for finding the cell of a new vector or a query. k-means provides both: its centroids concentrate where the data are, and "nearest centroid" costs one distance per centroid. The expensive clustering is paid once at build time, and every later vector or query needs only the lookup. The centroids are a summary of the data, not labels, which suits a partition that only has to be useful and not meaningful.

Limitation or tradeoff

The wiki states the failure case: the cell holding a query's true nearest neighbour is not among the probed cells, so the neighbour is missed. The fraction of data scanned is only approximately nprobe over nlist because cells differ in size. Training is the expensive step on very large collections: from one million vectors upward the guidelines switch to an HNSW-based cluster assignment, and from ten million they warn that training is going to be slow and suggest training on a GPU or clustering in two levels.

What the sources document

  • The index-types page describes IndexIVF indexes as cell-probe methods that use a partitioning technique such as k-means. The feature space is cut into nlist cells, each database vector goes to the inverted list of its nearest centroid, and at query time nprobe lists are selected and searched. [13]
  • The speed-up costs the guarantee of finding the exact nearest neighbour, and the failure case is that the cell holding the query's nearest neighbour is not among the selected cells. [13]
  • As a rule of thumb the number of centroids should balance the assignment cost of nlist times d for a plain k-means against the cost of scanning inverted lists, which gives nlist of the form C times the square root of n. [13]
  • The guidelines recommend K between 4 and 16 times the square root of N below one million vectors, say this just clusters the vectors with k-means, and say that between 30 times K and 256 times K vectors are needed for training. [14]
  • The k-means page says k-means is at the foundation of quantization operations and IVF indexes in Faiss, that the algorithm starts from an initial (random) assignment, and that extra heuristics stop centroids from collapsing. [15]

Material Color Utilities: k-means to pick a theme colour from an image

Material Foundation (Material Color Utilities)Documented in an open-source project

Material Color Utilities is Material's open-source colour library for Material You, and its README ties it to Material 3 dynamic colour. The README lists a quantize component that turns an image into N colours, and its TypeScript source shows that component is a k-means variant seeded by a Wu quantizer, followed by a scoring step that picks the theme colour. [16][17][18][19]

The problem

A photo or wallpaper contains thousands of distinct pixel colours, but a theme needs one source colour to derive a whole scheme from. The library first has to boil the pixels down to a short list of representative colours, and the code comments say the algorithm was also tuned for speed.

Where this topic fits

k-means is the colour-reduction stage between pixels and theme. Each pixel is a point in the Lab colour space, up to 128 centroids form the palette, and the palette goes on to a scoring step that ranks the colours for theming.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The image bytes are read and pixels that are not fully opaque are skipped; each remaining pixel is stored as an ARGB colour.
  2. A Wu quantizer reduces the pixels to at most the requested number of colours (128 in this call). These become the starting centroids, in place of random ones.
  3. Pixels are converted to Lab colour space, and identical pixels are collapsed into one point with a count, so the update step uses weighted means.
  4. Each iteration assigns points to the nearest cluster, skipping distance checks that a triangle-inequality rule shows cannot change the answer, then recomputes each cluster as a weighted mean.
  5. The loop ends when no point moves, or after at most 10 iterations; a point moves only if it gets closer by more than a small threshold.
  6. The result maps each palette colour to the number of pixels it stands for. Score.score ranks that palette for suitability for theming and the top colour becomes the source colour.

Why this approach fits

The representative colours of an image are what a codebook is: a few centres that keep the squared colour error small, and k-means is the classic way to compute them. The Lab space is designed so that distances roughly follow perceived colour difference, which makes squared Euclidean distance a sensible measure there. Starting from the Wu result replaces the random start that this lesson warns about, and collapsing repeated pixels shrinks the work, since photographs repeat many exact colours.

Limitation or tradeoff

The loop is capped at 10 iterations, so the output approximates the k-means optimum rather than proving it. A centroid is a mean, so a palette colour can be one that no single pixel has. Frequent colours are not always good theme colours, which is why the library adds a separate ranking step instead of using cluster sizes alone. The count of 128 colours is a design choice in this function, not a property of the method. The README says some consumers, such as Material Design Components, need none of the quantization, scoring or image-extraction components, and the pages read here do not show which products call this function.

  • The README lists a quantize component described as turning an image into N colours, composed of Celebi, which runs Wu and then WSMeans, and a score component that ranks colours for suitability for theming. It names Material Design Components and Android System UI as consumers that integrate subsets of the library. [16]
  • The QuantizerCelebi source comment says it improves on a standard K-Means by setting the initial state to the output of a Wu quantizer instead of random centroids, and credits M. Emre Celebi's 2011 paper on improving the performance of K-Means for color quantization. [17]
  • The QuantizerWsmeans comment says it speeds up K-Means by deduplicating identical pixels and using a triangle-inequality rule to reduce comparisons. The code sets MAX_ITERATIONS to 10 and MIN_MOVEMENT_DISTANCE to 3.0, and works on Lab colour coordinates. [18]
  • The function that finds a source colour from image bytes skips pixels with alpha below 255, calls QuantizerCelebi.quantize with 128 colours, ranks the result with Score.score and returns the first ranked colour. [19]

DeepCluster: k-means cluster ids as training labels for a vision network

Facebook AI Research (Meta)Documented in a paper

Caron, Bojanowski, Joulin and Douze of Facebook AI Research (ECCV 2018) train a convolutional network without human labels by alternating between k-means on the network's own features and training the network to predict the resulting cluster assignments. [20]

The problem

Labelled image collections are expensive, so the authors want useful visual features learned from images alone, on collections as large as ImageNet and YFCC100M. That puts two demands on the clustering step: it must scale to over a million feature vectors, and it has to be repeated throughout training because the features keep changing.

Where this topic fits

k-means is the label generator inside a training loop. Every epoch the current network turns each image into a feature vector, k-means groups the vectors, and the group index of each image is used as its class label for ordinary supervised training. The centroids themselves are thrown away.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Pass every training image through the current convolutional network to get one feature vector per image.
  2. Reduce the features with PCA to 256 dimensions, whiten them and L2-normalise them, as the paper's implementation details describe.
  3. Run k-means on the prepared vectors. The paper's best ImageNet setting uses k = 10,000, chosen by performance on a downstream Pascal VOC classification task.
  4. If a cluster ends up empty during k-means, copy the centroid of a randomly chosen non-empty cluster with a small random perturbation and split that cluster's points between the two.
  5. Use each image's cluster index as its pseudo-label and train the network to predict it, sampling images uniformly over the pseudo-labels so that a few big clusters do not dominate.
  6. Return to the first step; for ImageNet the clustering is redone every epoch.

Why this approach fits

The loop needs a function that turns any set of feature vectors into discrete labels without a fixed label set, cheaply enough to repeat every epoch. k-means does that with a single parameter and handles a million vectors, and a cluster index is exactly what a classification loss expects. The authors note that preliminary results with other clustering algorithms indicate the choice of method is not crucial.

Limitation or tradeoff

Alternating a classifier with its own labels is prone to degenerate solutions, which is why the paper needs the empty-cluster rule and uniform sampling over pseudo-labels. The assignments never fully settle: the paper reports that the agreement between consecutive epochs' clusterings stops short of a stable partition, so a significant fraction of images is reassigned each epoch. The method is expensive, since training takes 12 days on a Pascal P100 GPU for AlexNet. And k is not obvious: the best value, 10,000, is ten times the 1,000 classes of ImageNet, so k was tuned on a downstream task.

What the sources document

  • DeepCluster iteratively groups features with the standard clustering algorithm k-means and uses the resulting assignments as supervision to update the network weights, and is applied to ImageNet and YFCC100M. [20]
  • The cluster assignments are used as pseudo-labels and the centroid matrix is not used. The authors write that preliminary results with other clustering algorithms indicate this choice is not crucial. [20]
  • Empty clusters are handled by giving the empty cluster the perturbed centroid of a randomly selected non-empty cluster and splitting that cluster's points. Sampling images uniformly over pseudo-labels is equivalent to weighting each input's loss by the inverse of its cluster's size. [20]
  • For clustering, features are PCA-reduced to 256 dimensions, whitened and L2-normalised, and the k-means implementation of Johnson et al. is used. The best result on the Pascal VOC validation task is obtained with k = 10,000, although ImageNet has 1,000 classes. [20]
  • Training for 500 epochs takes 12 days on a Pascal P100 GPU for AlexNet, and the authors report that the normalised mutual information between the clusterings of consecutive epochs saturates below 0.8, so a significant fraction of images is regularly reassigned. [20]

4Common mistakes

  1. Clustering raw columns that are measured on very different scales.

    Why it causes problems
    Squared Euclidean distance is dominated by the column with the largest numbers, so the clusters mostly reflect that one column and the others have almost no say. The output looks tidy and answers a different question from the one you asked.
    How to recognise it
    The centroids differ in one column and are nearly identical in the rest, or the clusters look like slices of a single variable. In the lesson's income and age example, a 34-year age gap adds only 1,156 to a squared distance of about 36 million, so income alone decides the split.
    How to fix it
    Standardise each column before fitting, fit the scaler on the training data only, and apply the same scaler to new points. Read the centroids back in original units afterwards. Choose a different scaling only for a reason you can state.

  2. Choosing k because it has the lowest inertia, or from an elbow plot alone.

    Why it causes problems
    Inertia never rises as k grows and reaches zero at k equal to the number of points, so it always favours more clusters. An elbow can be missing or ambiguous, and the silhouette score may peak somewhere else.
    How to recognise it
    An inertia curve that keeps falling smoothly, or criteria that disagree. In the nine-customer example the inertia runs 196.4, 83.0, 9.3, 6.3 and 4.7, and only the first two drops carry information.
    How to fix it
    Plot inertia and silhouette across a range of k, look at cluster sizes and centroid profiles, and decide with the purpose of the clusters in mind. Check that the clusters you keep are stable across seeds and across resamples of the data.

  3. Trusting a single run from random starting centroids.

    Why it causes problems
    Lloyd's algorithm ends at a local optimum set by its start. A poor start can leave two real groups merged and another group split, with no signal from the algorithm that anything is wrong.
    How to recognise it
    Different random seeds give different centroids or noticeably different inertia. In the worked example 12 of the 84 possible starts finish above 9.333, one of them at 111.833, about twelve times worse.
    How to fix it
    Use k-means++ seeding and several restarts, and keep the run with the lowest inertia. Fix the random seed so a result can be reproduced, and compare the inertia of runs before choosing one.

  4. Treating cluster numbers and borderline assignments as stable facts.

    Why it causes problems
    Cluster numbers are arbitrary labels, and with random initialisation the same data can come back with different numbering and different assignments for points near a boundary. A rule written for "cluster 2" silently changes meaning after the next retrain.
    How to recognise it
    Re-run with another seed and match clusters by centroid. ArcGIS Pro's documentation warns that the cluster number given to a set of features can change from one run to the next and that some features can switch clusters.
    How to fix it
    Name clusters after their centroid profile, keep the fitted centroids and assign new points to the nearest stored centroid instead of refitting, and measure how many points change cluster across seeds before using the labels in a decision.

  5. Using the silhouette score or the inertia as proof that the clusters are real.

    Why it causes problems
    Both measures reward compact, round clusters, so they can score a wrong grouping above a right one. A good number then gets quoted as evidence for structure that is not there.
    How to recognise it
    Plot the clusters. For the two interlocking crescents in this lesson, the k-means grouping has a mean silhouette of 0.46 and the true crescents 0.24, although 6 of the 24 points sit on the wrong side.
    How to fix it
    Use the scores to compare values of k on the same scaled data, never across different scalings, and confirm the clusters in a plot or in domain terms. If the groups are not compact, choose a method that does not assume it.

python
import itertools
import random
from collections import Counter

points = [(1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 3), (6, 8), (7, 9), (8, 8), (9, 2), (10, 3), (11, 1)]
BEST = 9.333          # the lowest inertia reachable with k = 3

def sq_dist(a, b):
    return sum((u - v) ** 2 for u, v in zip(a, b))

def mean_point(group):
    return tuple(sum(c) / len(group) for c in zip(*group))

def lloyd(points, centers):
    """Return the inertia that Lloyd's algorithm reaches from the given centres."""
    labels = None
    while True:
        new_labels = [min(range(len(centers)), key=lambda j: sq_dist(p, centers[j])) for p in points]
        if new_labels == labels:
            break
        labels = new_labels
        groups = [[p for p, lab in zip(points, labels) if lab == j] for j in range(len(centers))]
        centers = [mean_point(g) if g else c for g, c in zip(groups, centers)]
    return round(sum(sq_dist(p, centers[lab]) for p, lab in zip(points, labels)), 3)

def run_from(indices):
    # Sorting fixes how exact ties are broken, so results do not depend on draw order.
    return lloyd(points, [points[i] for i in sorted(indices)])

def kmeans_pp_indices(k, rng):
    chosen = [rng.randrange(len(points))]                   # first centre: uniform
    while len(chosen) < k:
        d2 = [min(sq_dist(p, points[c]) for c in chosen) for p in points]
        r = rng.random() * sum(d2)                          # next centre: probability proportional to D(x)^2
        running = 0.0
        for i, w in enumerate(d2):
            running += w
            if w > 0 and running >= r:
                chosen.append(i)
                break
    return chosen

outcomes = Counter(run_from(trio) for trio in itertools.combinations(range(len(points)), 3))
print("all 84 starts:", dict(sorted(outcomes.items())))

TRIALS = 5000
rng = random.Random(0)
uniform = sum(run_from(rng.sample(range(len(points)), 3)) == BEST for _ in range(TRIALS)) / TRIALS
plusplus = sum(run_from(kmeans_pp_indices(3, rng)) == BEST for _ in range(TRIALS)) / TRIALS
print(f"uniform starts : {uniform:.3f} reach inertia {BEST}")
print(f"k-means++ starts: {plusplus:.3f} reach inertia {BEST}")

def standardise(rows):
    cols = list(zip(*rows))
    means = [sum(c) / len(c) for c in cols]
    stds = [math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in c) / len(c)) for c, m in zip(cols, means)]

    labels = None
    while True:
        new = [min(range(len(centers)), key=lambda j: math.dist(p, centers[j])) for p in points]
        if new == labels:
            break
        labels = new
        groups = [[p for p, lab in zip(points, labels) if lab == j] for j in range(len(centers))]
        centers = [mean_point(g) if g else c for g, c in zip(groups, centers)]
    return labels, sum(math.dist(p, centers[lab]) ** 2 for p, lab in zip(points, labels))

def best_of_all_starts(points, k):
    runs = [lloyd(points, [points[i] for i in idx]) for idx in itertools.combinations(range(len(points)), k)]
    return min(runs, key=lambda run: run[1])

def mean_silhouette(points, labels):
    scores = []
    for i, p in enumerate(points):
        mean_to = {}
        for lab in set(labels):
            others = [q for j, q in enumerate(points) if labels[j] == lab and j != i]
            mean_to[lab] = sum(math.dist(p, q) for q in others) / len(others)
        a = mean_to.pop(labels[i])
        b = min(mean_to.values())
        scores.append((b - a) / max(a, b))
    return sum(scores) / len(scores)

scaled = standardise(customers)
for name, data in [("raw units", customers), ("standardised", scaled)]:
    for k in (2, 3):
        labels, inertia = best_of_all_starts(data, k)
        print(f"{name:12s} k={k}: labels={labels}  inertia={inertia:,.3f}  silhouette={mean_silhouette(data, labels):.3f}")
print("distance 1-2 raw:", round(math.dist(customers[0], customers[1]), 1), " 1-3 raw:", round(math.dist(customers[0], customers[2]), 1))
print("distance 1-2 std:", round(math.dist(scaled[0], scaled[1]), 2), " 1-3 std:", round(math.dist(scaled[0], scaled[2]), 2))

Part 4. In raw units a difference of 6,000 dollars is 6,000 distance units and a difference of 34 years is 34, so income decides everything: the raw split is the four lower incomes against the four higher ones, and every cluster mixes young and old customers. After standardising, one standard deviation of income and one of age count the same, the ages fall into two tight groups, and the split follows age (labels alternate between the young and the old customers).

Part 5. The two silhouettes are computed in different spaces. In raw units the space is one dimension for all practical purposes, an income line on which the split looks clean, so the score is high; the age differences it ignores cost nothing there. The standardised space includes age, where income varies freely inside each age group. A score is only comparable between clusterings of the same scaled data, for example k = 2 against k = 3 on the standardised values. Choose the scaling from what the columns mean, then compare k.

  • Are the raw distances from customer 1 about 6000.1 and 13000.0, and the standardised ones about 1.98 and 0.79?
