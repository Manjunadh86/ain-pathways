Stakeholder Management: Maps, Status Updates and Escalation

Stakeholder management is the work of finding everyone who can affect or is affected by an engagement, deciding how each of them should be involved, and keeping them informed with status updates, escalations, plain-language explanations of technical tradeoffs and clear answers to requests for more scope.

1Overview

Why it matters

A forward deployed engineer can build the right thing and still fail because of people the plan never mentioned: the security reviewer who first hears about the project in week five, the team lead whose staff never saw the tool before launch, the sponsor who learns from a colleague that the date has slipped. None of these is a coding problem, and each of them can stop a project that works.

Four things are expensive to get wrong. Who has to say yes before you can go live. Whether the people funding and using the work hear about progress and problems early enough to act. Whether a disagreement reaches someone with the authority to settle it. And whether a new request is weighed against the plan or quietly absorbed until the date slips. In a customer engagement you have no authority over anyone you depend on, so you cannot order these things into place. You have to map the people, keep them informed in a form they can act on, and ask for decisions at the right moment. Forward deployed engineers, solutions engineers, consultants and internal platform teams all need this.

Before you start

  • The FDE Engagement LifecycleBeginner

What you will be able to do

  • Build a stakeholder map that records each person's role, power, impact and current and needed stance, and mark the approvers who can block go-live.
  • Turn the map into a communications matrix that says who hears what, how, how often and from whom.
  • Write a status update that gives an overall status, progress against plan, risks with owners and specific asks with dates, and promises the next update.
  • Decide when a blocker or disagreement needs escalating, and write the escalation with the decision needed, the options, the cost of delay and a date.
  • Explain a technical tradeoff to a non-engineer in units they care about, with two or three options and a recommendation.
  • Answer a scope request by sizing it, showing its effect on the date and offering swap, extend or defer options instead of a silent yes or a flat no.

Key terms

Stakeholder
Any person or group who can affect an engagement or is affected by it, including people who never attend a meeting.
Sponsor
The stakeholder who funds or champions the work and has the authority to change its priority, scope or date.
Blocker
A stakeholder whose approval, access or cooperation you need and who can stop or delay the work, such as a security reviewer or a system owner.
Power and impact grid
A four-sector table that places each stakeholder by how much power they have over the outcome and how much the change affects them, which suggests how closely to engage them.
Stance
Whether a stakeholder is in favour, neutral or opposed today, recorded next to the stance the project needs from them.
Communications matrix
A table with stakeholders down one side and topics across the other, where each cell says how that communication will happen.
Status update
A short, regular message that gives overall status, progress against plan, risks and the decisions needed, so that its readers can act.
Escalation
Taking a blocker, risk or disagreement to a person with the authority to resolve it, with options and a date, because you cannot resolve it yourself in time.
Appetite
The amount of time a piece of work is allowed to take, set as a time budget before the design starts instead of estimated afterwards, and used as a fixed box while the scope is allowed to vary.
Error budget
The amount of unreliability a service is allowed in a period, equal to 100% minus its availability target, used as a shared number between product and operations teams.

2Explanation

Introduction

Think about having a kitchen renovated. The person paying sets the budget. The people who cook in it every day know what is wrong with the old kitchen. The inspector who signs off the gas work and the neighbour who may object to the extension never pick up a tool, yet either can stop the job. A builder who talks only to the person paying finds out about the rest late, and at the worst moment.

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect an engagement or is affected by it. A sponsor is the stakeholder who funds or champions the work and can change its priority. A blocker is a stakeholder whose approval or cooperation you need and who can stop or delay you. An engineer who builds inside a customer's organisation (see The FDE Engagement Lifecycle) usually depends on blockers they have no authority over: security, legal, the owner of a system whose data you need, the team lead whose staff must change how they work.

Stakeholder management is four skills used together, and this lesson covers them in order:

  1. Map the people: who sponsors, who uses, who can block, and where each one stands.
  2. Update them with regular status messages that let people decide something, not just feel informed.
  3. Escalate when a blocker or disagreement is beyond your authority, early and with options.
  4. Negotiate: explain technical tradeoffs in plain terms, and answer scope requests with a trade instead of a silent yes or a flat no.

The first skill builds on the interviews in Customer Discovery and Requirements, where you meet most of these people for the first time. The last connects to Technical Scoping and Sequencing, which gives you the plan that scope requests are weighed against. None of this needs special software. It needs a one-page map, a weekly message, and the habit of saying what you need and by when.

How it works

1. Map who matters

Start with a list and check it for gaps. The NHS Institute for Innovation and Improvement's handbook suggests brainstorming with well-networked people, then checking the list against nine groups, among them commissioners (who pay), customers, collaborators, consumers (whom your customers serve) and champions (NHS handbook, section 3.1 (opens in a new tab)). In a customer engagement these roles most often matter:

WhoWhat they controlHow they can hurt the engagement
SponsorBudget and priorityLoses interest when results are unclear
Day-to-day ownerAccess to people and dataBecomes a bottleneck, or your best advocate
End usersWhether the tool is usedWork around it quietly
Approvers (security, legal, procurement)Permission to go liveReviews take weeks and start only when asked
System and data ownersAPIs, exports, credentialsCannot be hurried
InfluencersWhat others hear about the workSpread doubt before you have met them

The grouping is ours, not the handbook's. Two questions in the first meeting fill most of it: "Who else has to say yes before this can go live?" and "Who will use this on a Monday morning?"

Next, rate each person. The handbook places people in a four-sector table by power (how much they can influence the outcome) and impact (how much the change affects them). Each sector has an approach: manage, satisfy, inform or monitor.

Approvers sit in satisfy: a security reviewer is barely affected by your tool but can delay go-live by weeks. Users sit in inform: little power, the most to gain or lose, so the handbook suggests helping such groups gain influence by organising them or consulting them actively.

Then record stance: in favour, neutral or opposed today, and where you need each person to be. The handbook calls this a readiness-for-change matrix and marks the movement needed. Its interview questions include what information a person wants, how they want to receive it and who influences them, and it says that talking to them directly is often best.

Finish with a communications matrix: people down one side, topics across, each cell saying how that communication will happen. The handbook recommends sending it to participants with politically sensitive items removed, because conversations about who was left out are shorter before the fact than complaints after it. Keep the map itself private, and revisit it at each phase of the engagement, because roles and positions change.

2. Send status updates people can act on

A status update exists so that someone can decide something. Microsoft's guidance for Dynamics 365 implementations says a project status report should answer questions such as: are we on track, what are the main risks or issues, and what do we need from the steering group. It adds that the report should use plain language, show the evidence behind data and estimates, compare actual progress with plan, explain deviations with causes and consequences, and propose actions (Microsoft Learn (opens in a new tab)).

Four habits make it work:

  • Lead with the conclusion. Put "at risk" in the first line, not after three paragraphs of activity.
  • Report unknowns, not only percentages. Shape Up notes that to-do lists grow as people discover work. Its hill chart marks each piece of work uphill (still working out the approach) or downhill (only execution left). Managers compare snapshots, and a dot that stays put signals trouble without anyone admitting they are stuck (Shape Up, chapter 13 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Promise the next update. PagerDuty's guide to incident updates says to tell stakeholders the longest they will wait for the next one and, when nothing has changed, to add something useful instead of repeating a message. It warns that too-frequent updates can undermine confidence (PagerDuty (opens in a new tab)). The rule works at a weekly pace too.
  • Match the channel to the person. GOV.UK's service manual wants senior responsible officers to join show-and-tells so they can decide quickly, and asks for light-touch monitoring (governance principles (opens in a new tab)). Extra reporting should be added only if it is really necessary (reporting (opens in a new tab)).

When a problem is live, PagerDuty's customer-liaison guidance has customer messages approved by the incident commander before posting, asks the writer to describe impact rather than technical detail, and says not to estimate resolution times (PagerDuty (opens in a new tab)). The same logic suits a customer-facing escalation: say what you know and what you do not, and promise only dates you can keep.

3. Escalate early, with options

To escalate is to take a blocker or decision to someone with the authority to resolve it, because you cannot resolve it in the time available. It is not a complaint and not a failure. GOV.UK's guidance says a service owner and team should escalate only when they need to, and that teams should know where their decision-making boundaries lie and who is accountable for helping when a decision falls outside them. Escalate when:

  • a blocker is outside your authority and its deadline is close (approval, access, a vendor reply);
  • two parties disagree and neither can settle it;
  • the plan no longer fits the date and the sponsor owns the tradeoff;
  • customer data, safety or compliance is at risk, whatever the date.

Agree the route in advance. The example error budget policy in Google's SRE Workbook (opens in a new tab) states beforehand that a disagreement about the budget calculation, or the actions it requires, goes to the CTO. A written route turns "going over someone's head" into "following the agreed process". Write the message so the reader can decide in one reading: the decision needed, the date and the cost of missing it, two or three options with consequences, your recommendation, and what you have tried. Tell your counterpart you are taking it up, and when. Waiting is also a decision: the Columbia investigation (see the examples) found engineers whose concerns stayed in local channels and never reached the managers deciding.

4. Explain tradeoffs in the listener's units

A technical tradeoff is a choice where improving one property costs another: faster delivery against more testing, higher availability against higher cost. Non-engineers decide these well when options are stated as effects they can weigh (days, money, risk, what users see) and badly when given a tour of the mechanism.

  1. Name the decision and who owns it. "Choose between two dates" beats "we have an architecture question".
  2. Translate to effects. An availability target such as 99.9% becomes allowed downtime: allowed downtime=(1−target)×period\text{allowed downtime} = (1 - \text{target}) \times \text{period}allowed downtime=(1−target)×period. Over a 30-day month (43,200 minutes), 99.9% allows 43.2 minutes and 99.99% allows 4.32.
  1. Offer two to four options and recommend one. Microsoft's ISE playbook describes trade studies: agree requirements with stakeholders, turn them into criteria, shortlist two to four options, evaluate them and record the decision (ISE playbook (opens in a new tab)). A lightweight version is a table of options against criteria with a one-sentence recommendation.
  2. Share one number when two groups disagree. Google's SRE book describes the error budget: product management sets a service level objective (SLO), a neutral monitor measures uptime, and the gap is the budget; releases continue while budget remains. The argument becomes about a number agreed in advance (SRE book, chapter 3 (opens in a new tab)).
  3. Use exact words. The Columbia investigation reproduced an analysis of a briefing slide that used "significant" five times with very different meanings. Use a number and a unit.

5. Answer scope pressure with a trade

Scope grows without anyone misbehaving. Shape Up says scope creep is not the fault of bad clients, managers or programmers, because a project's details only appear once you are inside it (chapter 14 (opens in a new tab)). The question is what a request displaces.

  1. Fix the box. Shape Up fixes the time (the appetite) and lets scope vary, so every idea meets the question "is there time for this?". Must-haves define done; nice-to-haves are built only if time is left. Cutting scope to fit the box is what the book calls scope hammering.
  2. Size the request and show its effect on the date, as in the worked example.
  3. Offer a trade, not a refusal: swap (something of equal size leaves), extend (the date moves) or defer (next phase, with an estimate). Recommend one and ask for a decision by a date.
  4. Check who owns the money. PostHog's handbook says a deliverable that doubles in scope is a new quote, not an informal extension (PostHog (opens in a new tab)).
  5. Write it down in the next status update, so plan, date and scope change together.

Sizing is covered in Technical Scoping and Sequencing. The boundary with support after launch is in Production Handoff and Support.

Worked example: Claims-triage pilot: a stakeholder map, a week-3 status update and a scope request

This is a teaching scenario with invented people and numbers. A forward deployed engineer has a six-week appetite (30 working days, one engineer, starting Monday 2026-10-19) to build a claims-triage assistant for an insurer. The plan holds 24 person-days of must-have work, so 6 days are contingency. It is Friday 2026-11-06, the end of week 3 (working day 15).

Step 1: classify the map

Each person gets a power score and an impact score from 1 to 5, a stance (-1 opposed, 0 neutral, +1 in favour) and the stance the project needs. A score of 4 or 5 counts as high. The script applies the four-sector rule, flags high-power people who are not yet where they need to be, and also computes the numbers for steps 2 and 3.

python
# Teaching data. power and impact run 1-5; stance and needed run -1 (opposed) to +1 (in favour).
PEOPLE = [
    ("Maya, VP Claims (sponsor)",     5, 4,  1, 1),
    ("Tom, claims team lead",         3, 5,  0, 1),
    ("12 claims handlers",            2, 5,  0, 1),
    ("Priya, security reviewer",      5, 2,  0, 1),
    ("Dev, claims-system API owner",  4, 3,  0, 1),
    ("Lena, compliance counsel",      4, 2, -1, 0),
    ("Sam, finance partner",          3, 1,  0, 0),
]
HIGH = 4  # a score of 4 or 5 counts as high

def quadrant(power, impact):
    if power >= HIGH:
        return "Manage" if impact >= HIGH else "Satisfy"
    return "Inform" if impact >= HIGH else "Monitor"

print(f"{'stakeholder':<30}{'P':>2}{'I':>2}  {'sector':<8}{'move':>5}  flag")
for name, power, impact, stance, needed in PEOPLE:
    move = needed - stance
    flag = "win over before week 5" if power >= HIGH and move > 0 else ""
    print(f"{name:<30}{power:>2}{impact:>2}  {quadrant(power, impact):<8}{move:>+5}  {flag}".rstrip())

# Status at working day 15 of a 30-day appetite, one engineer, estimates in person-days.
CAPACITY, DAY_NOW, MUST_TOTAL, MUST_DONE, REQUEST = 30, 15, 24, 11, 5
plan_done = MUST_TOTAL * DAY_NOW / CAPACITY
left_work, left_days = MUST_TOTAL - MUST_DONE, CAPACITY - DAY_NOW
print(f"\nplan: {plan_done:.0f} d done by day {DAY_NOW}; actual: {MUST_DONE} d ({MUST_DONE - plan_done:+.0f} d)")
print(f"slack now: {left_days} - {left_work} = {left_days - left_work} d (plan: {left_days - (MUST_TOTAL - plan_done):.0f} d)")

need = left_work + REQUEST  # a request arrives: Spanish-language emails, 5 days
print(f"\nrequest adds {REQUEST} d: need {need} d, have {left_days} d")
for label, work in [("accept as is", need), ("swap out 'export report' (5 d)", need - 5), ("defer to phase 2", left_work)]:
    gap = left_days - work
    print(f"  {label:<30} {'ends with ' + str(gap) + ' d slack' if gap >= 0 else 'ends ' + str(-gap) + ' d late'}")

Three high-power people are not yet where the project needs them: Priya (security) and Dev (API owner) are neutral and need to become supporters, and Lena (compliance) is opposed and needs to become neutral. All three are in satisfy, the sector that gets forgotten, so each gets a short written brief every fortnight and is consulted before any decision that touches them. Tom and the handlers are in inform: little power, the most impact, so they get a weekly demo with a feedback slot. Maya is the only manage stakeholder, and Sam needs a monthly note.

Step 2: write the status update

The numbers say 11 of 24 days of must-have work are done against a plan of 12, and slack has fallen from 3 days to 2. That is thin, and one approval is outstanding. In this scenario the security review takes eight working days. To finish on day 28 and leave two days, it must be under way by the close of day 20 (Friday 2026-11-13), so that its eight days are days 21 to 28. Nobody has booked it. The update leads with the conclusion:

text
Status: at risk
Progress: 11 of 24 days of must-have work done; plan said 12. Slack is 2 days (plan: 3).
Risks: claims sandbox arrived 2 days late (owner: Dev); security review not booked (owner: Priya).
Asks: Maya to have Priya book the review by Wed 2026-11-11. Maya to decide the new request by the same date.
Next update: Fri 2026-11-13.

Step 3: answer the scope request

On the same Friday Maya forwards a request to handle Spanish-language emails as well, estimated at 5 person-days, which is why the update above asks her to decide it. The script shows that accepting it as it stands ends the engagement 3 days late, while swapping out the 5-day "export report" feature or deferring Spanish to phase 2 keeps 2 days of slack. The reply:

text
Spanish-language emails would add about 5 days. We have 15 days left and 13 days of must-have work, so accepting it as it stands ends 3 days after 2026-11-27.
1. Swap: drop the export report feature (5 days). Ends on time with 2 days slack.
2. Extend: move the end date by 3 working days, to 2026-12-02.
3. Defer: ship Spanish in phase 2. Ends on time with 2 days slack.
I recommend 3. We have not yet seen real Spanish-language emails, so the 5-day estimate is a guess, and deferring keeps every feature you asked for in the first release. Please choose by 2026-11-11.

What the result means

Each message ends in a decision that someone else owns, with a date. The map told you who those people were before you needed them. The status update put the thin slack and the unbooked review in front of the sponsor while there was still time to act. The scope reply turned "can you also?" into a choice with a price. The figures are pilot-grade: person-day estimates are guesses, and the 5-day request is the least certain of all, which the reply says.

Edge cases and limitations

The sponsor is keen but has no authority over the blocker
In the worked example Maya sponsors the pilot, yet Priya's security review does not report to her. A supportive sponsor does not clear a review she does not control. Ask who can say no as well as who can say yes, check the real reporting line, and ask the sponsor for an introduction in the first fortnight, not in the week the review is due.
The map goes stale
Reorganisations, a new executive or a departing champion change who matters. The NHS handbook says to revisit stakeholder tools and plans at regular intervals. Re-check at every phase of the engagement and whenever the sponsor changes. A warning sign is someone you have never heard of appearing on a thread to make a decision.
The bad news concerns the customer's own team
Missing data or an unfinished access request may sit with a manager who will read your update. Tell that person first, privately and with evidence, then state it neutrally in the update as what, impact and ask. Courtesy that keeps a known blocker out of the written status moves the surprise to a later and worse moment.
Updates that never change
A status that is green every week trains readers to skim, so the first red is missed or disbelieved. Report what changed since last time, and which pieces of work are still unknowns. Sending updates more often than anything changes also wears trust down; PagerDuty warns that very frequent incident updates can undermine confidence.
An extension looks justified
Shape Up allows an extension only in rare cases, where the outstanding tasks are true must-haves that survived every attempt to cut them and no open questions remain in the work. If the work is still uphill, the book advises stopping and rethinking. In a paid engagement an extension is also a commercial change, so bring in the account owner.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • More than one group has a say in the outcome, for example a sponsor, a user team and an approver such as security or compliance.
  • The engagement has a hard date or a long-lead approval, so a late surprise would move the date.
  • Requests keep arriving beyond the original brief and nobody is writing down what each one displaces.
  • A technical tradeoff must be decided by someone who will not read the technical detail.

Think twice when

  • The work is one short task for one person who is also the decision-maker. A two-line message does the job, and a formal map is overhead.
  • You are using the paperwork to avoid a hard conversation. A matrix or a status report does not replace speaking to the person.
  • The map would be shared with the people on it. Keep the version that rates people's power and stance private.

A simpler alternative. For a small engagement, write three lines on one page: who decides, who will use it, and who can block it, with a first name beside each. Send a one-paragraph note every Friday that says whether you are on track, what is next and what you need. This stops being enough when a second group joins, when an approval has a long lead time, or when requests start to change the date.

Key takeaways

  • A stakeholder map lists sponsors, users, approvers and influencers, rates each by power and impact, and records stance now against stance needed. High-power, low-impact approvers are the group most often forgotten.
  • A status update is worth sending only if it can change a decision. Lead with on track, at risk or off track, show progress against plan with numbers, name risks with owners and dates, and ask for specific decisions by a date.
  • Promise the time of the next update and keep it, and add something new each time instead of repeating the last message. Unknowns that have not moved say more than a percentage complete.
  • Escalate early, to a route agreed in advance, and write the escalation so that one reading is enough. State the decision needed, the date, two or three options with consequences, and your recommendation.
  • Explain a technical tradeoff in the listener's units of time, money, risk and what users see. When two groups disagree, agree a shared number in advance, as an error budget does.
  • Answer a scope request by sizing it against the time left and offering swap, extend or defer, with a recommendation and a decision date, then record the outcome in the next status update.

Example 1 of 5

NHS Institute handbook: stakeholder analysis and a communications matrix for improvement projects

NHS Institute for Innovation and ImprovementDocumented in official docs

The Handbook of Quality and Service Improvement Tools, published by the NHS Institute for Innovation and Improvement in 2010, documents a stakeholder analysis tool (section 3.1) and a communications matrix (section 3.2) for NHS staff who run improvement projects. Section 3.1 includes a worked example from a clinical coding project. [1]

The problem

Improving a health service means involving many groups at once: clinicians, administrative staff, patients and user groups. The handbook says a stakeholder analysis is one of the first steps in any change project, because it helps avoid the conflict and delay caused by inadvertently failing to involve key people. In its clinical coding example, the list of people who could be involved or affected by moving to source coding by consultants was long, so the team needed a way to decide who mattered most.

Where this topic fits

Stakeholder analysis comes at the start of a project, while its scope is being defined, and the handbook asks teams to revisit it at regular intervals. Its output feeds two decisions: who sits on the project board, and how each person is kept informed and involved, which the communications matrix records.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Brainstorm everyone likely to be affected with a group of experts who have good networks, then check the list against the nine Cs so that no category is missing.
  2. Place each name in a four-sector table by power and by impact or stake, giving the sectors the labels Manage, Satisfy, Inform and Monitor.
  3. Build a readiness-for-change matrix showing whether each key person is in favour, neutral or opposed, and mark the movement needed.
  4. Work out how each key stakeholder is likely to react, what they want to know and how they want to hear it, ideally by asking them directly.
  5. Turn the analysis into action: list the people and groups against the topics that must be communicated, and fill each cell with how that communication will be done.
  6. Send the matrix to participants with politically sensitive items removed, so that everyone knows who communicates what, how and when.
  7. In the clinical coding example, use the analysis to decide the membership of the project board and to write a communications plan, then run a PDSA pilot of the change.

Why this approach fits

The tool separates three questions that teams tend to blur: who matters (power and impact), where each person stands (in favour, neutral or opposed) and what to do about it (the communications matrix). Because the table has a sector for people with high power but low impact, it prompts a team to include approvers who would otherwise be forgotten. For an engineer arriving in a new organisation, it is a quick way to see which of the people they have not yet met matter most.

Limitation or tradeoff

The handbook is a 2010 healthcare-improvement document, and its clinical coding example is unnamed and reports no outcome, so it shows the method rather than evidence that the method works. Power and impact are judgments, not measurements, and two team members may place the same person differently. A map that rates people candidly is itself sensitive, so the handbook's advice to remove politically sensitive items before circulating the matrix applies to the map as well.

What the sources document

  • The handbook says a stakeholder analysis is one of the first steps in any change project and can help avoid conflict and delays caused by inadvertently failing to involve key people. [1]
  • It offers nine categories, called the 9 Cs, for checking a stakeholder list for gaps: commissioners, customers, collaborators, contributors, channels, commentators, consumers, champions and competitors. [1]
  • Stakeholders are placed in a four-sector table by power and by impact or stake holding, with the sectors labelled Satisfy, Manage, Monitor and Inform, followed by a readiness-for-change matrix of in favour, neutral and opposed that shows the movement needed. [1]
  • The communications matrix lists the people to communicate with and listen to along one axis and the topics along the other, and the handbook advises sending it to participants with politically sensitive items removed. [1]
  • In its clinical coding example, the team used the stakeholder analysis to design the membership of the project board and a communications plan, followed by a PDSA cycle pilot. [1]

Example 2 of 5

Microsoft's Dynamics 365 guidance: a steering-group status report that ends in decisions

MicrosoftDocumented in official docs

Microsoft Learn's Dynamics 365 Implementation Guide is Microsoft's published guidance for running implementations of its business applications. The page 'Use classic project governance structures' explains how steering groups, status reports, risk registers, stage gates and design boards should work in practice. [2]

The problem

Most business application projects have a steering group, where the project sponsor, senior business stakeholders and project leaders meet regularly to review and direct the project. Microsoft's guidance says such groups can be unclear about their purpose or rely on inaccurate or vague status reports. A steering group that cannot understand the status cannot steer.

Where this topic fits

The status report is the main channel from the project team to the steering group. It sits between the team's day-to-day work and the decisions only the group can take. The risk register and the stage gates feed it, and its asks set the agenda for the next meeting.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Agree and state the purpose of each steering meeting, and keep to it.
  2. Write the status report to answer questions such as: are we on track, what are the main risks or issues, and what do we need from the steering group.
  3. Use simple language without unnecessary jargon, and show how the data and estimates were gathered, analysed and summarised.
  4. Compare actual progress with planned progress, using a metric such as percentage complete or remaining work, and highlight each deviation with its causes and consequences.
  5. Propose actions for each problem or risk, and give the group clear options and recommendations so that it can steer.
  6. Keep a risk register that is reviewed regularly, with a clear owner and action for each risk.

Google SRE error budgets: a shared number for the reliability tradeoff, with a named escalation

GoogleDocumented in a case study

Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (chapter 3, Embracing Risk) describes how product development and SRE teams agree an error budget. The SRE Workbook (Appendix B) publishes an example error budget policy, for a fictional game service, that includes an escalation clause. [3][4]

The problem

The SRE book says product development teams are judged mainly on velocity, which rewards pushing code quickly, while SRE teams are judged on reliability, which rewards resisting change, and that information asymmetry amplifies the tension. Arguments about how much testing is enough, or how long to canary, tend to settle into a balance that reflects the negotiating skill of the engineers involved rather than something provable. The book says such decisions should not be driven by politics, fear or hope.

Where this topic fits

The error budget sits between product management and the SRE team. Product management sets the availability objective, monitoring measures it, and a policy states in advance what happens to releases when the budget is spent and who decides if the parties disagree. The workbook's example policy is a document both groups agree to before an argument starts.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Product management defines the service level objective (SLO), the share of requests that must succeed in a period, as a business decision about how much unreliability users will tolerate.
  2. A monitoring system that both sides treat as neutral measures actual performance against the SLO.
  3. The error budget is 100% minus the SLO. Measured failures use it up as they occur.
  4. While budget remains, releases proceed under the normal release policy.
  5. In the workbook's example, when the four-week budget is exceeded, all changes and releases stop except top-priority (P0) issues and security fixes, until the service is back within its SLO.
  6. In the workbook's example, a single incident that uses more than 20% of the four-week budget requires a postmortem with at least one P0 action item.
  7. If the parties disagree about how the budget is calculated or what action it requires, the example policy sends the question to the CTO to decide.

Why this approach fits

The budget turns an argument about words (too risky, too slow) into a quantity both groups agreed before the argument began. Decision rights are explicit: product management sets the target, monitoring measures it, and a named executive settles disputes. A forward deployed engineer can use the same shape with a customer: let the business owner pick the target, show the budget in minutes or requests, and agree in advance what happens when it runs out.

Limitation or tradeoff

The book notes that the control only works if the SRE team has the authority to stop launches when the SLO is broken. The workbook policy is an example for a fictional game service, not Google's policy for a real product. A budget is only as good as the measurement behind it: the example policy itself lists miscategorised errors as a way for budget to be wrongly spent or wrongly left unspent.

  • As the book describes Google's practice, product management defines the SLO, a neutral monitoring system measures actual uptime, and the difference is the error budget; new releases can be pushed while budget remains. [3]
  • The book says that when the budget is nearly drained the product developers themselves push for more testing or slower releases, and notes that this relies on the SRE team having authority to stop launches if the SLO is broken. [3]

PostHog's FDE handbook: a written brief, a weekly rhythm and a rule against silent scope growth

PostHogDocumented in official docs

PostHog's public company handbook documents how its forward deployed engineering (FDE) team runs customer engagements. Two pages, How forward deployed engineers work and Working with customers, describe scoping, communication rhythms and boundaries. [7][8]

The problem

A forward deployed engineer works inside a customer's account for a vendor. The FDE owns the technical outcome, but requests tend to grow, recurring reactive questions can look like part of the engagement, and customers should not be surprised by what happens next. PostHog's handbook addresses each of these in writing.

Where this topic fits

The practices span the whole engagement: a written brief before delivery, a weekly rhythm during it, a rule for scope growth when it happens, and a named approver at handover. They sit alongside the customer's account owner, who keeps the commercial relationship.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Before delivery starts, write a short brief with a measurable outcome, a rough timeline and a shared definition of done, and send it to the customer to confirm. Scope small, because a tight scope is cheaper to expand than a loose one is to unwind.
  2. Hold a 30-minute weekly sync at the same time each week, and cancel it if there is nothing to discuss.
  3. Post a one-paragraph status note in the customer channel each week that frames the next week, so that nobody is surprised on Monday.
  4. Reply to direct customer messages within one business day, or acknowledge within a day and give a real estimate.
  5. When a request would grow the work, push back on should we also. A deliverable that doubles in scope is a new quote, and quotes go through the account owner instead of being invented on the spot.
  6. If a customer's questions are recurring and reactive, reframe the work as support rather than absorbing it silently.
  7. Finish with a deliverable that a named person on the customer side approves, with follow-on work captured as explicit tasks and a list of assumptions and out-of-scope items.

Why this approach fits

When the brief, the rhythm and the scope rule are written down before the engagement starts, a scope conversation becomes a comparison with a document, not a personal refusal. The weekly note makes the coming week visible, and the commercial boundary keeps price discussions with the person who owns that relationship.

Limitation or tradeoff

The handbook states PostHog's intent, not measured results, and describes the FDE team as still new and figuring things out. PostHog's FDEs serve customers of its own product, so other settings, such as fixed-price consulting or government contracts, will differ. 4Common mistakes

  1. Treating the sponsor as the only stakeholder.

    Why it causes problems
    The sponsor can fund the work but cannot clear a security review they do not control, and the people who will use the tool had no say. The blockers and the users surface late, when changing course costs the most.
    How to recognise it
    Your contact list holds only people from the sponsor's team, and nobody on it can say who approves go-live or who will use the tool on Monday morning.
    How to fix it
    In the first week, ask who else must say yes and who will use it. Put each name on the power and impact grid with their stance, and book a first conversation with every high-power person.

  2. Sending status updates that report activity instead of status and asks.

    Why it causes problems
    Readers cannot tell whether they need to do anything. A list of what you did hides slippage, and the first message that says there is a problem arrives as a surprise.
    How to recognise it
    Your update has no overall status word, no comparison with plan and no sentence that starts with what you need. Replies are a thank-you or silence.
    How to fix it
    Use the five layers: headline status, progress against plan, risks with owners and dates, asks with dates, and the time of the next update. Put the conclusion in the first line.

  3. Escalating late, or escalating with only a problem.

    Why it causes problems
    A late escalation leaves the decision-maker no time to choose. A message that only describes a problem sends the work back to you with a request for more detail. Both cost days.
    How to recognise it
    A blocker has appeared in three weekly updates in a row without an owner or a date, or your escalations draw replies that ask what you want from the reader.
    How to fix it
    Agree the escalation route at kickoff. When a blocker's deadline is near, tell your counterpart, then send the decision needed, the date, two or three options with consequences and your recommendation.

  4. Explaining a tradeoff by describing the mechanism, or with words such as significant.

    Why it causes problems
    The listener cannot weigh a mechanism, so they defer to whoever sounds most certain or decide without understanding. A vague quantifier means different things to different readers.
    How to recognise it
    Your explanation uses terms the listener has to ask about, offers no options, or contains words such as significant, minor or large with no number attached.
    How to fix it
    State the decision, translate each option into days, money, risk and what users see, give every vague word a number and a unit, and finish with a recommendation.

  5. Saying yes to small additions and never recording what they displace.

    Why it causes problems
    Each extra is small, but together they use up the contingency. The date moves without anyone deciding that it should, and the sponsor hears about it late.
    How to recognise it
    Remaining slack shrinks week by week while the status stays green, or the backlog gains items and loses none.
    How to fix it
    Size every request against the time left, offer swap, extend or defer, and record the choice in the next status update. Route any change to price or contract through the account owner.

  6. Refusing a scope request with a bare no.

    Why it causes problems
    A bare no tells the requester you do not care about their need, and they go around you to your manager or the sponsor. A trade shows you took the need seriously and lets them choose.
    How to recognise it
    Your answer contains we cannot or out of scope with no cost, no alternative and no date when the request would be revisited.
    How to fix it
    Restate the need, give its cost in days, offer swap, extend or defer, and say when a deferred item would be picked up.

Classify a stakeholder register and lint a status update

python
import re

HIGH = 4  # a score of 4 or 5 counts as high
RHYTHM = {
    "Manage": "weekly 1:1 and the weekly update",
    "Satisfy": "fortnightly brief; consult before any decision that touches them",
    "Inform": "weekly demo with a feedback slot",
    "Monitor": "monthly note",
}
DATE = re.compile(r"\b\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}\b")
PEOPLE = [
    ("Asha, COO (sponsor)", 5, 5, 1, 1),
    ("Ben, warehouse supervisor", 3, 5, 0, 1),
    ("Chloe, IT security", 5, 2, 0, 1),
    ("Dmitri, ERP vendor contact", 4, 3, -1, 0),
    ("Eve, pickers (30 people)", 1, 5, 0, 1),
    ("Farid, procurement", 2, 2, 0, 0),
]
UPDATE = """Status: at risk
Progress: 11 of 24 days of must-have work done; plan said 12
Risks: ERP sandbox access still missing, owner: Dmitri
Risks: security review not booked
Asks: confirm swap of 'export report' for Spanish emails by 2026-11-06
Next update: Friday
"""

def sector(power, impact):
    if power >= HIGH:
        return "Manage" if impact >= HIGH else "Satisfy"
    return "Inform" if impact >= HIGH else "Monitor"

def classify(people):
    lines = []
    for name, power, impact, stance, needed in people:
        s = sector(power, impact)
        flag = " [win over]" if power >= HIGH and stance < needed else ""
        lines.append(f"{name:<28}{s:<8}{RHYTHM[s]}{flag}")
    return lines

def lint(update):
    fields = {}
    for line in update.strip().splitlines():
        label, _, body = line.partition(":")
        fields.setdefault(label.strip(), []).append(body.strip())
    problems = [f"missing '{label}' line" for label in ("Status", "Progress", "Risks", "Asks", "Next update") if label not in fields]
    problems += [f"status '{v}' is not on track, at risk or off track" for v in fields.get("Status", []) if v not in ("on track", "at risk", "off track")]
    problems += [f"risk without an owner: {v}" for v in fields.get("Risks", []) if "owner:" not in v]
    for label in ("Asks", "Next update"):
        problems += [f"{label.lower()} without a date: {v}" for v in fields.get(label, []) if not DATE.search(v)]
    return problems

print("\n".join(classify(PEOPLE)))
print()
found = lint(UPDATE)
print("\n".join(found) if found else "update OK")

  • If the German estimate were 1 day instead of 3, would your arithmetic still show the pilot ending late, and by how much?
