Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection finds observations that do not fit the pattern of the rest of the data, usually without labelled examples of what a problem looks like. A detector gives every observation a score for how unusual it is, and a threshold or a review budget turns scores into alerts.

1Overview

Why it matters

Most anomaly problems arrive without a usable training set. A risk team has a handful of confirmed fraud cases, an operations team has a few incidents a quarter, and an attacker changes behaviour as soon as the old pattern is blocked. A classifier needs many examples of every failure, so these problems are approached the other way round: model what normal looks like and rank everything by how far it sits from that.

Running a detector takes two lines of code. What goes wrong is everything around it. A z-score that cannot mathematically exceed 3 in a sample of ten readings is trusted to find the bad reading. Unscaled features are passed to a distance-based method that then looks only at the column with the biggest units. A contamination setting of 5 percent flags 5 percent of the fitted rows whether or not anything is wrong. A detector is declared good because it caught three known incidents. Data scientists, reliability engineers, risk analysts and anyone who owns an alert need to know which kind of oddness each method can see, because the same score column hides very different blind spots.

Key terms

Anomaly score
A number a detector assigns to each observation, higher meaning more unusual. A threshold or a review budget turns it into a yes or no.
Contamination
The fraction of the data assumed to be anomalous. Several tools use it to place the threshold, so it decides how many rows get flagged.
Contextual anomaly
A value that is ordinary overall but odd for its context, such as 300 requests per second at 4 a.m. on a Sunday.
Novelty detection
Fitting a detector on data believed to be clean and then judging new observations, as opposed to scoring the same data it was fitted on.
Z-score
The distance of a value from the mean in units of standard deviation. A cut-off of 3 is common.
Median absolute deviation (MAD)
The median of the absolute differences between each value and the median. It measures spread without being pulled by extreme values.
Path length
In an isolation tree, the number of random cuts needed to separate one point from all others. Short paths mean easy to isolate.
Local density
How tightly packed a point's nearest neighbours are. The local outlier factor compares a point's density with that of its neighbours.
Reconstruction error
The distance between a point and its rebuilt copy after passing through a compressed model of normal data, such as PCA or an autoencoder.
Precision at k
Of the k highest-scored items, the fraction that reviewers confirm are real anomalies.

2Explanation

Introduction

A bank's card system learns that you usually spend small amounts near home. A purchase ten times larger than usual, made in another country, stands out, and nobody had to describe fraud to the system first. That is the core of anomaly detection: learn what ordinary looks like from the data itself, then score each observation by how far it sits from ordinary. The highest scores are the anomalies, also called outliers.

Take server load. Each row is one minute with two measurements: requests per second, and CPU use in percent of one core summed over all cores, so a multi-core host can exceed 100. CPU normally rises in step with traffic. Two of the sixteen minutes plotted below are odd in different ways. Minute E has far more traffic than any other minute, but its CPU is exactly what that traffic should cost. Minute J has ordinary traffic and ordinary CPU when each is looked at alone, yet the pair is wrong: a busy server with an idle CPU, perhaps because requests fail before they reach the application.

A threshold on either column finds E and misses J. Finding J needs a method that looks at the columns together. Different detectors see different kinds of oddness, and this lesson is organised around that. You will meet statistical thresholds (z-scores, medians and quartile fences), isolation forests that separate points with random cuts, the local outlier factor, which compares a point's density with its neighbours', and reconstruction error, which asks how well a compressed model of normal data can rebuild a point. The last part covers the hard question: how to set a threshold and judge a detector when you have few labels or none.

Three terms recur. A point anomaly is a single observation that is odd by itself. A contextual anomaly is ordinary in general but odd for its context. A collective anomaly is a group or sequence that is odd although each member looks fine. Unlike classification, the data has no column saying which rows are anomalies, so a detector cannot be tuned against a loss function; people judge it afterwards. The lesson builds on k-means clustering, statistics for ML, decision trees and dimensionality reduction and PCA.

How it works

Three setups, one output

Whether labels exist decides the setup. With labels for both classes the task is imbalanced classification. With data believed to be clean you have novelty detection: fit on normal data, then judge new observations. With neither you have outlier detection: the data may contain anomalies, and the detector scores the same rows it was fitted on. scikit-learn's outlier detection guide (opens in a new tab) draws this line, and its LocalOutlierFactor has a novelty switch for it. Every method below ends in a score, and a threshold turns the score into a decision.

Metrics with a daily or weekly rhythm need a baseline that depends on the time. Either way, the history must exclude past incidents and deployments, or they become part of "normal".

Isolation forest

The original paper (opens in a new tab) rests on one observation: anomalies are few and different, so random cuts separate them quickly. An isolation tree picks a random feature and a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum in the current node, then repeats on both sides until every point is alone or a height limit of ⌈log⁡2ψ⌉\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil⌈log2​ψ⌉ is reached (ψ\psiψ is the subsample size). The path length h(x)h(x)h(x) is the number of cuts to reach a point's leaf. Anomalies end near the root; ordinary points sit deep. Averaged over the trees and normalised by the typical path length c(ψ)c(\psi)c(ψ) of a binary search tree with ψ\psiψ points, it becomes a score between 0 and 1:

A score near 1 is an anomaly, a score well below 0.5 is normal, and scores all near 0.5 mean the sample has no distinct anomalies. The paper suggests ψ=256\psi = 256ψ=256 and 100 trees, and scikit-learn's IsolationForest (opens in a new tab) uses the same defaults (n_estimators=100, max_samples='auto', meaning min⁡(256,n)\min(256, n)min(256,n)). Small subsamples are a feature: they limit masking, where a dense group of anomalies hides itself, and swamping, where normal points near anomalies look odd. No distances are computed, so it scales well, and because each cut is uniform within a feature's range, rescaling a feature does not change the trees. Note that scikit-learn's score_samples uses the opposite sign to the paper, so lower means more abnormal there.

Local outlier factor

Isolation forest compares a point with the whole data set. The local outlier factor (LOF) compares it with its own neighbourhood, so it can flag a point that is unremarkable globally but sparse next to a dense cluster. Let Nk(p)N_k(p)Nk​(p) be the kkk nearest neighbours of ppp and dk(o)d_k(o)dk​(o) the distance from ooo to its own kkk-th neighbour. The reachability distance smooths small distances, and LOF averages the neighbours' densities relative to the point's own:

A point deep inside a cluster scores about 1, and larger values mean a sparser neighbourhood than its neighbours have (Breunig et al. (opens in a new tab)). That paper advises kkk of at least 10 and above the size of the smallest group you want to treat as a cluster; scikit-learn's guide says k=20k=20k=20 works well in general and a larger kkk when more than 10 percent of points may be outliers. Because distances drive the score, scale the features first.

Reconstruction error

Fit a model that compresses normal data, rebuild each point from the compressed form, and score the gap: ∥x−x^∥2\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2∥x−x^∥2. With PCA, keep the first mmm components, project, and map back with inverse_transform. Ordinary points lie near the low-dimensional subspace and rebuild well; a point that breaks the correlations between features lies off it. An autoencoder does the same with a neural network that can follow curved structure, and BigQuery ML reports mean squared error (opens in a new tab) as the score for both its PCA and autoencoder detectors. The same idea works for time series, where a model predicts the next value from recent history and the prediction error is the score, as in the NASA example below. The blind spot is the mirror image: a point far out along the learned pattern still rebuilds well.

Choosing a threshold

Three common ways. A statistical rule (∣z∣>3|z| > 3∣z∣>3, ∣M∣>3.5|M| > 3.5∣M∣>3.5, fences). A contamination setting that treats the top fraction of scores as anomalous: scikit-learn places the LOF threshold at that percentile of the training scores, and BigQuery ML accepts a value from 0 to 0.5. Or a review budget: alert on the top kkk per day, where kkk is what people can actually investigate. A rule that flags the top fraction of the scores it is applied to flags that fraction by construction, even in a week with no incident. A cut-off fitted once from a contamination guess does not do that, but it is only as good as the guess.

Evaluating with few or no labels

With no labels, have people review the top-ranked items and a random sample of the rest, and record the verdicts; a few hours of review becomes a label set. Check stability too: a detector whose top ten changes with the seed, kkk or the scaling is not saying much. With a few labels, prefer measures that need no threshold: precision at kkk and the area under the ROC or precision-recall curve. scikit-learn's benchmark example (opens in a new tab) scores LOF and isolation forest with ROC curves against known labels, and Elastic's evaluate API (opens in a new tab) reports precision, recall and ROC at several thresholds. Labels are incomplete, so a flagged point nobody has labelled may be a real problem. Do not pick by reputation either: ADBench (opens in a new tab) found no unsupervised algorithm statistically better than the others across 57 data sets, and found that with 1 percent labelled anomalies most semi-supervised methods beat the best unsupervised one.

Part 2: sixteen minutes, two metrics

These are the sixteen minutes from the introduction: fourteen ordinary ones, minute J (380 requests per second, CPU 30) and minute E (800 requests per second, CPU 130). The script uses only the Python standard library. It standardises both columns, scores each minute with the one-component PCA reconstruction error and with LOF at k=3k = 3k=3, and prints the per-column z-scores for comparison. As a check, its LOF function returns 0.9821, 1.0370, 73.3697 and 0.9821 for [[-1.1], [0.2], [101.1], [0.3]] with k=2k = 2k=2, the values (printed with a minus sign) in the example of the scikit-learn LocalOutlierFactor documentation (opens in a new tab).

python
import math
import statistics as st

# (requests per second, CPU %) for 14 ordinary minutes, then minute J and minute E
rows = [(100, 26), (130, 28), (150, 33), (180, 38), (200, 39), (220, 44), (250, 46),
        (270, 52), (300, 53), (320, 59), (340, 60), (360, 66), (390, 67), (410, 73),
        (380, 30), (800, 130)]
names = [f"n{i}" for i in range(1, 15)] + ["J", "E"]

# 1. Standardise each column to mean 0 and sample standard deviation 1.
cols = list(zip(*rows))
Z = [tuple((v - st.mean(c)) / st.stdev(c) for v, c in zip(r, cols)) for r in rows]

# 2. PCA with one component. For two standardised columns that are positively
#    correlated the first principal direction is (1, 1) / sqrt(2).
def reconstruction_error(z):
    s = (z[0] + z[1]) / math.sqrt(2)               # coordinate along the component
    back = (s / math.sqrt(2), s / math.sqrt(2))    # rebuilt point in feature space
    return (z[0] - back[0]) ** 2 + (z[1] - back[1]) ** 2

# 3. Local outlier factor with k nearest neighbours and Euclidean distance.
def lof(points, k):
    n = len(points)
    d = [[math.dist(p, q) for q in points] for p in points]
    nbrs = [sorted((j for j in range(n) if j != i), key=lambda j: d[i][j])[:k] for i in range(n)]
    kdist = [d[i][nbrs[i][-1]] for i in range(n)]
    lrd = [k / sum(max(kdist[j], d[i][j]) for j in nbrs[i]) for i in range(n)]
    return [sum(lrd[j] for j in nbrs[i]) / (k * lrd[i]) for i in range(n)]

print("name   rps   cpu   z_rps  z_cpu  recon_err   LOF")
for name, row, z, score in zip(names, rows, Z, lof(Z, k=3)):
    print(f"{name:>4} {row[0]:5d} {row[1]:5d}  {z[0]:6.2f} {z[1]:6.2f}  {reconstruction_error(z):9.4f} {score:5.2f}")

  • Per-column z-scores find E and miss J. E sits at 3.03 and 3.05 (the ceiling for n=16n = 16n=16 is 3.75, so 3 is reachable here). J's scores of 0.49 and −0.90 are unremarkable, and nothing in either column alone reveals it.
  • Reconstruction error finds J and misses E. The two columns have correlation 0.93, and the first component keeps 96.5 percent of the variance. J lies far from that line: its error of 0.9575 is 75 times the largest ordinary error (0.0127). E lies almost exactly on the line, so it rebuilds perfectly at 0.0002.

Edge cases and limitations

A sample too small for a z-score cut-off
With n readings the largest possible absolute z-score is (n - 1)/sqrt(n), so a cut-off of 3 can only be reached when n is at least 11. In the worked example a latency of 250 ms among nine readings near 100 ms (ten readings in all) scores 2.84. Use the modified z-score or IQR fences for short windows, and remember that a z-score window that includes the suspect value is partly measuring that value.
Incidents inside the history the baseline learns from
In outlier-detection mode the detector fits the same data it scores, so a long outage or a bulk import in the history widens the band or raises the average and makes the next one look normal. CloudWatch lets you exclude time periods such as deployments from model training for this reason. Remove known incidents from the fitting window, or fit on a clean period and score only new data, which is what the novelty setting of LocalOutlierFactor is for.
Correlation breakers hidden from axis-parallel cuts
An isolation tree cuts one feature at a time, so a point inside each feature's range needs several cuts to isolate, just as a point on the edge of the normal band does. In our minimal forest on the sixteen worked-example minutes, J scored 0.53 and ranked fourth, below two ordinary minutes at the ends of the band. With 400 generated ordinary minutes in place of the fourteen it scored 0.70 and ranked second. The Extended Isolation Forest paper describes related artifacts in score maps and cuts with random slopes instead. PCA reconstruction error or LOF sees J directly.
LOF depends on scale and on k
On the raw worked-example columns, minute J has a LOF of 1.02 against an ordinary maximum of 1.19, because requests per second dominate the distance; after standardising it is 2.69. With standardised data, J scores 3.96 at k = 2, 2.69 at k = 3, 1.90 at k = 5 and 1.15 at k = 8, because as k nears the number of ordinary minutes every neighbourhood covers most of the data. The published advice of k at least 10 is hard to follow with sixteen rows. Elastic standardises by default for the same reason.
Contamination always flags that fraction
scikit-learn places the LOF threshold at the percentile of training scores that matches the contamination value, and BigQuery ML also derives its cut-off from a contamination argument, although its page does not say how. Fitting and scoring the same 2,000 rows with 0.05 flags 100 of them in a quiet week and may still miss a real cluster of 150. Treat the fraction as a review budget or a starting guess, watch the share of flagged rows over time, and rank by score rather than relying on the yes or no.
Reconstruction error cannot see extreme but consistent points
A point far out along the principal direction is still close to the subspace, so its error is near zero. Minute E has an error of 0.0002 although it carries almost three times the traffic of the median minute (285 requests per second). Add a second score for position inside the subspace, such as the z-score of each component coordinate, and flag a point when either score is high.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You have few or no reliable labels for the failures you care about, or the failures keep changing, as in abuse and fraud.
  • Anomalies are rare and look different from normal behaviour, and people can review a ranked list of the top few.
  • You are monitoring a metric with a stable rhythm and want an alert relative to what is normal at that time rather than a fixed limit.
  • Several signals must be read together, so that a combination can be odd while each signal alone is ordinary.

Think twice when

  • You have plenty of reliable labels for the failure types that matter. A supervised classifier on imbalanced data will usually find them better.
  • The anomalies are common, or form large dense groups that look like another normal mode. They are no longer few and different.
  • A hard rule exists, such as a disk above 95 percent or a latency above the contractual limit. A fixed threshold is clearer and cheaper.
  • Nobody will review the flagged items. Without feedback you cannot tell a useful detector from a noisy one.

A simpler alternative. A fixed limit, or a robust threshold (median and MAD, or IQR fences) on one metric compared with its own history at the same time of week. It is enough while a single signal carries the problem and you can state what is too high. It stops being enough when the oddness lies in combinations of signals, or when so many series are monitored that no one can set a limit for each.

Key takeaways

  • Anomaly detection scores how unusual each observation is relative to the data, and a threshold or review budget decides what gets flagged. The score is only as meaningful as the notion of normal behind it.
  • A plain z-score cannot exceed (n - 1)/sqrt(n), so it misses a single outlier in a sample of ten. The modified z-score and IQR fences use the median and quartiles and are not pulled by the outlier.
  • An isolation forest scores points by how few random cuts isolate them, local outlier factor by how sparse a point's neighbourhood is relative to its neighbours', and reconstruction error by how badly a compressed model of normal data rebuilds it.
  • Each method has a blind spot, such as the correlation breaker J for per-column thresholds or the extreme but consistent point E for reconstruction error, so match the method to the kind of oddness you expect.
  • Scale features for distance-based methods, keep known incidents out of the fitting data, and remember that a contamination setting flags a fixed fraction of the fitted data whether or not anything is wrong.
  • With few or no labels, judge a detector by reviewing its top-ranked items, by precision at k and ranking metrics on whatever labels exist, and by its stability, not by one headline number.

Example 1 of 5

GitLab: z-score alerts on service request rates in Prometheus

GitLabDocumented in an engineering article

An engineering post on GitLab's blog (23 July 2019), written up from a Monitorama 2019 talk by GitLab distinguished engineer Andrew Newdigate. It shows how Prometheus recording and alert rules turn a service's request rate into a z-score against its own history. [12]

The problem

GitLab wants to notice when a service leaves its normal operating range: to find incidents sooner, to catch regressions such as an n+1 query that makes one service call another at a very high rate, and to spot abuse of its free CI/CD and Pages hosting. Services carry different traffic levels and follow a weekly rhythm, so one fixed limit for all of them would not fit; the post derives each service's range from that service's own history instead.

Where this topic fits

The statistical threshold is computed inside Prometheus. Raw request counters are first aggregated to one series per service and environment. The z-score, or a z-score against a seasonal prediction, is the anomaly score, and an alert rule on its absolute value is the threshold. The statistics are recording rules inside Prometheus, so no separate model is trained.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Aggregate the http_requests_total counter to a five-minute rate per service and environment, dropping labels such as method, controller and status code.
  2. Record the one-week average and standard deviation of that rate as new series with recording rules.
  3. For metrics with a weekly rhythm, build an expected value for the current time of day from the same four-hour window one week earlier, adjusted for week-on-week growth.
  4. Take the median of three such weekly predictions so that one holiday week does not distort the expectation.
  5. Compute the distance between the current rate and the expectation in standard deviations.
  6. Alert when the absolute distance stays above 2 for ten minutes, and route the alert to chat instead of paging the on-call engineer.

Why this approach fits

Prometheus already provides averages, standard deviations and time offsets over a range, so a z-score threshold costs no new infrastructure and adapts to each series' own scale, which a single fixed limit cannot do. Comparing with the same hour of earlier weeks removes the weekly rhythm that would otherwise be mistaken for anomalies, and a threshold stated in standard deviations is easy to explain and tune.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post itself warns that z-scores assume a roughly normal distribution and that error rates, latencies and queue lengths probably do not have one. Seasonal predictions also fail around irregular events such as holidays, and aggregating too coarsely hides problems inside a subset of the traffic while aggregating too finely produces false positives from small samples.

Google Cloud's BigQuery documentation for the ML.DETECT_ANOMALIES function and for CREATE MODEL on PCA models describes anomaly detection that runs inside the data warehouse. [16][17][18]

The problem

Tabular data already sits in BigQuery and the people who know it write SQL. Exporting rows to a separate environment adds a pipeline, and many analysts have no labelled anomalies anyway. They need a way to score independent rows for oddness and to decide how many to flag, using only SQL.

Where this topic fits

A PCA or autoencoder model is trained on the rows with CREATE MODEL. ML.DETECT_ANOMALIES then applies it: each row gets a mean squared error score, and the score is compared with a cut-off that comes from the contamination setting. The output table adds is_anomaly and the error. The docs name the metric but do not define the PCA error; reading it as the gap to a copy rebuilt from the compressed form is ours, and it is backed for autoencoders by the separate reconstruction-loss function.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Train a PCA model on rows believed to be mostly normal, choosing either a number of components or a share of variance to keep.
  2. The model centres the numeric features and, by default, scales them to unit variance before projecting them.
  3. Call ML.DETECT_ANOMALIES with the model, a contamination value and the table or query to score.
  4. For each row the function reports a mean squared error; in a typical PCA implementation this compares the original features with a copy rebuilt from the retained components.
  5. The contamination value fixes a cut-off on the error, learned from the training data, and rows above it get is_anomaly set to true.
  6. Sort the output by mean_squared_error to review the worst rows first.

Why this approach fits

Reconstruction error needs no labels and works on several columns at once, so it can find combinations that are odd although each column is normal. Putting it in SQL means scheduled queries, existing permissions and no data movement. Scaling features by default addresses the scale problem that breaks many distance methods.

Limitation or tradeoff

The contamination value fixes the share of training rows treated as anomalous, so the cut-off reflects an assumption, not evidence. PCA is linear, and a point far out along a learned direction (minute E in the worked example) is rebuilt almost perfectly and scores low. Rows are scored independently, so the function has no sense of time, which is why the docs send time series to ARIMA_PLUS models. Every query that calls the function is billable.

What the sources document

  • ML.DETECT_ANOMALIES supports ARIMA_PLUS and ARIMA_PLUS_XREG models for time series and k-means, PCA and autoencoder models for independent and identically distributed data, always returns an is_anomaly column, and is billable on every query regardless of the pricing model. [16]
  • For autoencoder and PCA models the target metric is mean squared error, returned as mean_squared_error; for k-means it is the normalised distance, the distance to the nearest centroid divided by that cluster's radius. [16]
  • The contamination argument, a value from 0 to 0.5, is the proportion of anomalies in the training data; it sets the cut-off, and input whose target metric exceeds the cut-off is identified as anomalous. [16]
  • A PCA model is created with either NUM_PRINCIPAL_COMPONENTS or PCA_EXPLAINED_VARIANCE_RATIO but not both. SCALE_FEATURES defaults to TRUE, and numerical features are always centred. [17]
  • A separate function, ML.RECONSTRUCTION_LOSS, computes the reconstruction losses between the input and output data of an autoencoder model and returns mean absolute error, mean squared error and mean squared log error. [18]

NASA JPL: prediction-error anomaly detection on SMAP and Curiosity telemetry

NASA Jet Propulsion LaboratoryDocumented in a paper

A KDD 2018 paper by NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory authors describes anomaly detection for telemetry from the Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) satellite and the Curiosity rover (Mars Science Laboratory, MSL), an evaluation against expert-labelled incidents, and a pilot on SMAP. [19]

The problem

Spacecraft send thousands of telemetry channels. Out-of-limits alarms need expert-set ranges and are prone to miss anomalies that occur within the limits or that have a temporal element. The precision and recall of the existing alarms were not captured, and labelled anomalies are scarce, so the authors needed a detector that sets its own thresholds without labels and a way to measure it with the few labels they could build.

Where this topic fits

The paper uses the reconstruction-error idea in its forecasting form: a model predicts each telemetry value from recent history and commands, and the prediction error is the anomaly score. The thresholding stage that turns smoothed errors into alerts is the statistical-threshold step of this lesson, made adaptive.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Train a recurrent LSTM network per channel to predict the next value from recent values and encoded commands.
  2. Compute the absolute prediction error at each step and smooth it with an exponentially weighted moving average.
  3. Choose a threshold from candidates of the mean plus z standard deviations of the smoothed errors, namely the one whose removal of the values above it most reduces their mean and standard deviation, with a penalty for flagging many values.
  4. Group consecutive values above the threshold into anomaly sequences and give each a severity score.
  5. Prune sequences whose largest error is too close to the highest error that is not anomalous, which removes many false positives.
  6. Pass the surviving sequences to operations engineers, and measure precision and recall against anomalies mined from incident reports.

Why this approach fits

Telemetry has context that a static limit cannot express, so predicting what a channel should do next and scoring the miss catches anomalies that stay inside the limits. The adaptive threshold avoids hand-setting a range for each of thousands of channels, and making no Gaussian assumption suits error distributions that are not Gaussian. Mining incident reports gives a small test set tied to real spacecraft issues, even though the anomalous time ranges still had to be labelled by hand.

Limitation or tradeoff

The authors say the precision they report is optimistic: they assume an anomaly every five days and leave out streams with no anomaly, which would add false positives. The upper bound on recall is set by the forecaster, because poor predictions leave no signal for a threshold to find, and recall was lower for contextual anomalies. They name false positives as the main obstacle to adoption, since engineers have little time to check them.

What the sources document

  • An LSTM predicts each telemetry value, the absolute prediction error is smoothed with an exponentially weighted average, and an unsupervised, nonparametric threshold is chosen without labelled data or assumptions about the error distribution. [19]
  • The expert-labelled evaluation data was built by mining incident, surprise and anomaly (ISA) reports and labelling the anomalous time ranges by hand: 105 anomaly sequences across 82 channels and 47 reports, 62 of them point anomalies and 43 contextual. [19]
  • With pruning, the nonparametric thresholding reaches a reported precision of 87.5 percent and recall of 80.0 percent overall; without pruning, precision falls to 48.9 percent and recall rises to 84.8 percent, and Gaussian-tail thresholds do worse on F0.5. [19]
  • The authors note that the precision shown is overly optimistic compared with the deployed system, because the experiment assumes one anomaly every five days and omits streams that contain no anomaly. [19]
  • In the SMAP pilot, over 700 channels were monitored in near real time with models retrained offline every three days, several confirmed anomalies had been found since October 2017, and the false-positive rate was named as the main obstacle to wider use. [19]

4Common mistakes

  1. Using a plain z-score threshold on a short window or on a skewed metric.

    Why it causes problems
    The suspect value inflates the mean and standard deviation it is judged by, and with n readings the z-score cannot exceed (n - 1)/sqrt(n). A real outlier slips through, while a skewed metric such as latency produces false alarms every busy hour because its tail is longer than a normal distribution allows.
    How to recognise it
    The largest z-score in the window sits just under the bound (2.84 against 2.85 for ten readings), or alerts cluster at the same hours every day. GitLab's check is to compute the maximum and minimum z-scores over a week: about plus or minus 4 is tolerable and plus or minus 20 means the tail is too long.
    How to fix it
    Use the modified z-score or IQR fences for short windows, take a logarithm or a percentile of skewed metrics, and compare against a time-of-week baseline instead of one global mean.

  2. Fitting the baseline on history that contains the incidents you want to catch.

    Why it causes problems
    A long outage or a bulk import widens the learned band and raises the average, so the next one looks normal. In outlier-detection mode the anomalies are part of what the detector learns from.
    How to recognise it
    The band or the standard deviation stays inflated for days after a known incident, and replaying the incident dates through the detector gives only mediocre scores for them.
    How to fix it
    Exclude known incidents and deployments from the fitting window, as CloudWatch lets you do. Fit on a clean period and score only new data, using the novelty setting where the library has one, and refit regularly.

  3. Giving unscaled features to a distance-based detector such as LOF or k nearest neighbours.

    Why it causes problems
    Euclidean distance is dominated by the column with the largest units, so the detector effectively looks at one feature and misses anomalies in the others. Changing a unit from metres to kilometres changes which rows are flagged.
    How to recognise it
    Flagged rows differ from the rest only in one column, or the ranking changes when units change. In the worked example, minute J has a LOF of 1.02 on raw columns, indistinguishable from ordinary minutes, and 2.69 after standardising.
    How to fix it
    Standardise or robust-scale numeric features first. scikit-learn's benchmark example suggests a RobustScaler when outliers are present and notes that the best preprocessing depends on the data set; Elastic standardises by default, and BigQuery ML scales PCA features by default.

  4. Treating the contamination setting as a known fact about the data.

    Why it causes problems
    A rule that flags a fixed fraction of the scores it sees does so in every period. Reviewers see noise in a quiet week, and a real incident larger than the fraction is partly ignored. People start to distrust the alerts.
    How to recognise it
    The share of flagged rows is nearly constant over time and does not move during known incidents.
    How to fix it
    Rank by score and set a review budget, track the flagged share over time, and calibrate the cut-off on reviewed samples instead of a guessed percentage.

  5. Judging a detector by accuracy, or by whether it caught a few known incidents.

    Why it causes problems
    With rare anomalies accuracy is meaningless: in the HTTP data set of the isolation forest paper 0.4 percent of 567,497 connections are attacks, so flagging nothing is 99.6 percent accurate. Catching three known incidents says nothing about how many false alerts the team will receive.
    How to recognise it
    The report has recall on known events but no alert count, no precision and no look at the flagged points that nobody has labelled.
    How to fix it
    Report precision at k, a ranking metric such as average precision or ROC-AUC, and alerts per week. Have people review the top-ranked items and a random sample of the rest. The NASA JPL authors show why: they warn that their own precision figures are optimistic because quiet streams were left out of the test.

  6. Choosing an algorithm by reputation or from a single benchmark.

    Why it causes problems
    No detector wins everywhere. ADBench found none of the unsupervised algorithms it tested statistically better than the others, and scikit-learn's own benchmark has isolation forest ahead on one data set and LOF ahead on another. A method picked blind may be blind to the anomalies you care about.
    How to recognise it
    The method was chosen before anyone wrote down what kind of oddness to expect, and the ranking changes when you swap the data set.
    How to fix it
    State whether you expect global outliers, local outliers, contextual anomalies or broken relations between features, shortlist two or three methods that fit, and compare them on a small reviewed sample, including their stability across seeds.

Hint 1

A tree node is either a leaf holding a count of points, or a split holding a feature index, a split value and two children. Stop when the depth reaches the limit, the node holds one point or fewer, or every value of the chosen feature is equal.

Draw the subsample with a partial shuffle of a list of indices, using rng.random() so that a single random.Random(seed) drives every random choice.

Show solution

A reference implementation follows. It uses the same rng.random() call order described in the prompt, so it reproduces the printed output exactly.

python
import math
import random
import statistics as st


def c(n):
    """Average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree of n points."""
    if n <= 1:
        return 0.0
    if n == 2:
        return 1.0
    return 2 * (math.log(n - 1) + 0.5772156649) - 2 * (n - 1) / n


def build(points, depth, limit, rng):
    """Grow one isolation tree: random feature, random split between its min and max."""
    if depth >= limit or len(points) <= 1:
        return ("leaf", len(points))
    q = int(rng.random() * len(points[0]))
    lo, hi = min(p[q] for p in points), max(p[q] for p in points)
    if lo == hi:
        return ("leaf", len(points))
    split = lo + (hi - lo) * rng.random()
    left = [p for p in points if p[q] < split]
    right = [p for p in points if p[q] >= split]
    return ("node", q, split, build(left, depth + 1, limit, rng), build(right, depth + 1, limit, rng))


def path_length(x, node, depth=0):
    if node[0] == "leaf":
        return depth + c(node[1])          # c(size) stands in for the unbuilt subtree
    _, q, split, left, right = node
    return path_length(x, left if x[q] < split else right, depth + 1)


def isolation_scores(points, trees, sample_size, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    limit = math.ceil(math.log2(sample_size))
    forest = []
    for _ in range(trees):
        idx = list(range(len(points)))
        for i in range(sample_size):          # partial shuffle = sample without replacement
            j = i + int(rng.random() * (len(idx) - i))
            idx[i], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[i]
        forest.append(build([points[i] for i in idx[:sample_size]], 0, limit, rng))
    return [2 ** (-(sum(path_length(p, t) for t in forest) / trees) / c(sample_size)) for p in points]


def standardise(rows):
    cols = list(zip(*rows))
    return [tuple((v - st.mean(col)) / st.stdev(col) for v, col in zip(r, cols)) for r in rows]


def report(label, rows, j_index, e_index, **settings):
    scores = isolation_scores(standardise(rows), seed=0, **settings)
    ranked = sorted(range(len(rows)), key=lambda i: -scores[i])
    others = [s for i, s in enumerate(scores) if i not in (j_index, e_index)]
    print(f"{label}: J score {scores[j_index]:.3f} (rank {ranked.index(j_index) + 1}), "
          f"E score {scores[e_index]:.3f} (rank {ranked.index(e_index) + 1}), "
          f"highest ordinary minute {max(others):.3f}")


small = [(100, 26), (130, 28), (150, 33), (180, 38), (200, 39), (220, 44), (250, 46),
         (270, 52), (300, 53), (320, 59), (340, 60), (360, 66), (390, 67), (410, 73),
         (380, 30), (800, 130)]
report("16 minutes ", small, 14, 15, trees=200, sample_size=16)

rng = random.Random(42)
ordinary = []
for _ in range(400):
    rps = 100 + 310 * rng.random()
    ordinary.append((rps, 10 + 0.15 * rps + 8 * (rng.random() - 0.5)))
big = ordinary + [(380, 30), (800, 130)]

Expected behaviour

The plan quantifies the mismatch: a 1 percent rule flags about 259 five-minute points, against a budget of roughly 25 alerts in 90 days. It uses a time-of-week baseline and a robust score, defines an alert as a short run of breaches, picks the threshold from the budget, evaluates by event with leave-one-incident-out, and reviews unlabelled alerts by hand. It does not report accuracy.

Hint 1

90 days is about 12.9 weeks. Multiply by the weekly budget, then compare with 1 percent of 25,920.

Hint 2

A model on the raw column sees the daily peak as unusual. Ask what value it should be compared with instead.

Hint 3

Three events is a tiny test set. Work out the chance of catching all three if the detector's true recall were only 50 percent.

Show solution

The proposal. A 1 percent contamination flags 0.01 x 25,920 = 259 points whatever happens. The alert budget is about 2 x 12.9 = 25 alerts in 90 days. Even if neighbouring flags merge into events, 259 flagged points are likely to produce far more alerts than that, and the rule flags the same share in a quiet quarter. A forest on raw latency has no notion of time, so the daily peak scores high every day and incidents at quiet hours are underrated. These are contextual anomalies, which need a contextual baseline.

A better detector.

  • Baseline: for each five-minute slot of the week, the median of the same slot in the previous four weeks.
  • Score: the difference between the current value and its baseline divided by a robust spread of recent differences (a MAD-based modified z-score), so a few past spikes do not inflate the scale.
  • Exclusions: leave the three labelled incidents, deployment windows and public holidays out of the baseline and the spread, or they become part of "normal". CloudWatch lets you exclude such periods from model training, and GitLab's post takes the median of three weekly predictions so one holiday week does not distort the expectation.
  • Alert rule: three consecutive five-minute breaches of the score threshold, which removes single-point spikes and gives one alert per event instead of several.
  • Threshold: choose the smallest value whose alerts on the incident-free history fit the budget of about 25 in 90 days.

Evaluation with three incidents.

  • The incidents cover 12 to 27 of 25,920 points, about 0.05 to 0.1 percent, so accuracy is meaningless: a detector that never alerts is about 99.9 percent accurate.
  • Use event-level measures: how many of the three incidents had an alert inside the incident window, how many minutes after onset, and alerts per week on the rest of the history.
  • Use leave-one-incident-out: set the baseline exclusions and threshold on history without incident A and test on A, then rotate. This avoids tuning on all three.
  • Be modest about the result. If the true recall were 50 percent, the chance of catching all three is 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 12.5 percent, so 3 of 3 is weak evidence. Report it with the alert rate.
  • Have an engineer review every alert that matches no label, and label a random sample of the quiet periods too. Unlabelled alerts may be real problems, so the precision you can compute is a floor.

After launch. Review the alerts per week against the budget, and the reviewed false alarms to see which time slots or deployments cause them. Add confirmed events to the label set so that precision at k and lead time can be tracked, and refit the baseline on a rolling window.

Check your answer

  • Did you compare 259 flagged points with an alert budget of about 25 and conclude that the proposal is far over budget?
  • Does your baseline depend on the time of week, and does it exclude the incidents and deployments?
  • Is your threshold derived from the alert budget and your evaluation event-level, with no accuracy figure?
  • Did you say how unlabelled alerts will be reviewed and why 3 of 3 caught incidents is weak evidence?
