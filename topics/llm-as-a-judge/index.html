LLM-as-a-Judge

LLM-as-a-judge means asking a language model to grade another model's output against written criteria, returning a score, a pass or fail verdict, or a choice between two answers. It makes quality checks that code cannot express runnable at scale, but only after you have compared the judge's verdicts with human labels.

1Overview

Why it matters

Most LLM features produce free text that has no single correct string. A support answer can be right in a hundred wordings, so an exact-match test fails good answers and passes some bad ones. Human review gives the best signal but cannot keep up with a prompt change every day or with thousands of production traces. A judge model fills the gap: it applies a written rubric to every output within minutes, so free-text behaviour can be regression-tested on each change.

The risk is that a judge is itself a model with habits. Published studies find that judges favour the answer shown first, favour longer answers, and can favour text written by their own model family. A judge that has never been compared with human labels returns confident numbers that may measure those habits instead of quality. Anyone who ships an LLM feature, reports its quality to others, or picks between two prompts or models using automatic scores needs to know how to build a judge and how to test it.

  • Run a calibration loop on a small labelled set and decide whether the judge is trustworthy enough to use.
  • State what to monitor after launch, namely the judge version, fresh human spot checks and signs that the system is being tuned to the judge.

Key terms

LLM-as-a-judge
A pattern in which one language model grades the output of another model (or of itself) using a rubric supplied in the prompt.
Rubric
The written criteria a judge applies, ideally one criterion per judge, with each score level or verdict rule described in plain words.
Pointwise scoring
Grading one output on its own, either on a small numeric scale or as pass or fail. Also called single-answer grading.
Pairwise comparison
Showing the judge one input and two candidate outputs and asking which is better, or whether they tie.
Reference-guided grading
Giving the judge a reference answer or the source text, so it checks the output against known material instead of relying on its own knowledge.
Position bias
A judge's tendency to prefer an answer because of where it appears in the prompt, usually first, rather than because of its content.
Verbosity bias
A judge's tendency to prefer longer answers even when the extra length adds no information.
Self-preference bias
A judge's tendency to rate outputs from its own model family higher than equally good outputs from others. Zheng et al. call it self-enhancement bias.
Golden set
A fixed collection of real outputs labelled by a human expert, used to measure how closely a judge agrees with people. Also called a calibration set.
Cohen's kappa
An agreement statistic that subtracts the agreement two labellers would reach by chance. It is 1 for perfect agreement and 0 for agreement no better than chance.

2Explanation

Introduction

When you test an ordinary function, you write an assertion: the output must equal the expected value. That stops working as soon as the output is written language. If an assistant summarises a support ticket, thousands of different summaries are acceptable, and a string comparison marks most of them wrong. Cheaper checks such as format validation and keyword rules cover part of the problem, and human review covers the rest (see Evaluating LLM Applications). LLM-as-a-judge is a third option for the questions that remain: is this answer faithful to its source, is the tone right, which of these two replies is better?

Think of a teaching assistant who marks essays using your marking scheme. The assistant is fast and never tires, so you can mark every essay instead of a sample. You would not trust the marks blindly, though. You would mark twenty essays yourself, compare, find where the assistant disagrees with you, and clarify the scheme. You would also watch for habits, such as giving more marks to longer essays. A judge model is the assistant, the rubric is the marking scheme, and calibration is that comparison with your own marks.

You will write a rubric a model can apply consistently, measure the judge against human labels with the right statistics, and learn the biases that are documented for current judge models and how to counter them. A worked example runs the loop on 20 labelled answers in plain Python. Five documented cases then show the approach in a research benchmark, a company's document question-answering work, a public leaderboard, a calibration against a domain expert, and a production monitoring product.

How it works

What a judge receives and returns

A judge is an ordinary model call with a grading job. The request holds the task the application was given, the output to grade, the criteria and, when you have them, source documents or a reference answer. The reply holds a verdict. Zheng et al. (2023) (opens in a new tab), the paper that named the approach, describes three ways to ask:

  • Pairwise comparison. The judge sees one question and two answers and picks the better one or declares a tie. Relative results are more stable than absolute scores, but each test case needs two outputs.
  • Single-answer grading (pointwise). The judge scores one output on its own. This scales to many systems, but the paper notes that scores can miss subtle differences and are less stable than relative comparisons. A pass or fail verdict is a pointwise score with two levels.
  • Reference-guided grading. The judge also receives a reference answer or the source text and checks the output against it. Zheng et al. found this helps on math and reasoning questions, where a judge that must solve the problem itself is often wrong.

Which format to pick depends on the question. Eugene Yan's survey (opens in a new tab) reports research findings that pairwise comparison tends to beat direct scoring on subjective qualities such as coherence, while for objective ones such as faithfulness, where an answer either is or is not supported by its source, direct scoring is enough.

Writing the rubric

The rubric is the part you control most. Five habits make judges more consistent.

  1. Judge one criterion per call. "Is it accurate, complete and polite?" hides which part failed. The Databricks rubric for grading document question answering kept correctness, comprehensiveness and readability as separate components and weighted them afterwards (Leng et al., 2023 (opens in a new tab)).
  2. Define every level, not only the ends. A bare "rate from 1 to 5" leaves the middle to chance. Say in a sentence what a 2, a 3 and a 4 look like, or use a verdict rule such as "fail if any claim is unsupported".
  3. Use few levels. Databricks found that GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 ranked systems consistently on low-precision scales and recommended 0 to 3 or 1 to 5 over 0 to 10. Hamel Husain (opens in a new tab) goes further and advises binary pass or fail verdicts with a written critique, which are easier to check against a person. Both views reject fine scales that nobody can define.
  4. Ask for reasoning before the verdict. In a Hugging Face cookbook (opens in a new tab) experiment on a small set of human-rated answers (the notebook's code samples at most 28), a bundle of prompt changes raised the Pearson correlation between a Mixtral judge and the human ratings from 0.567 to 0.843. The bundle included a 1 to 4 integer scale instead of 0 to 10, a description of each level, and a field where the judge writes its evaluation before the score. Its two human raters correlated only 0.563 and each change was not measured separately, so treat it as one small experiment, not a controlled study.
  5. Show examples. Databricks reports that GPT-3.5 gave very inconsistent results without the example-based rubric, but with an example for each score it became a usable judge at about a tenth of GPT-4's cost and more than three times the speed. GPT-4's own consistency changed little with the examples. Hamel recommends using the domain expert's written critiques as the examples.

Anthropic's evaluation guide (opens in a new tab) gives matching advice for LLM-based grading: write detailed, clear rubrics, ask for a specific output such as "correct" or "incorrect" or a 1 to 5 scale, and let the grader reason before it scores.

A minimal judge prompt that applies these habits is shown below. The tags keep the text under test apart from your instructions.

text

<question>{question}</question>

First write a critique of one to three sentences that quotes any unsupported claim.
Then give the verdict. Reply in exactly this format:
CRITIQUE: <text>

Turning the reply into a number

Ask for a fixed format and parse it strictly. A reply that cannot be parsed is a failed judgement, not a pass, so count those separately and investigate them.

python
import re

def parse_verdict(reply: str) -> str:
    match = re.search(r"^VERDICT:\s*(PASS|FAIL)\s*$", reply, re.MULTILINE)
    if match is None:
        raise ValueError("no verdict line in judge reply")
    return match.group(1).lower()

Where the model lets you set it, use a low temperature so the same input tends to get the same verdict; otherwise repeat a sample of calls to see how much verdicts vary. When you need finer scores than integers, G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023 (opens in a new tab)) reads the probability the model gives to each score token and computes the expected score, score=∑ip(si) si\text{score} = \sum_i p(s_i)\,s_iscore=∑i​p(si​)si​. The authors added this because one digit usually dominates the score distribution and integer outputs produce many ties. It needs an API that returns token probabilities, which not every provider offers.

Calibrating against human labels

A judge is a measuring instrument, so check it against something you trust before using its output.

  1. Collect a golden set of real outputs, including the failures you worry about. Have the person whose judgement defines quality label them as pass or fail, with a short written reason. Hamel calls this person the principal domain expert.

  2. Run the judge on the same items without showing it the human labels.

  3. Compare. Raw agreement pop_opo​ is the share of items with the same label. Two labellers who guess also agree some of the time, so also compute Cohen's kappa, which removes the agreement expected by chance (scikit-learn documentation (opens in a new tab)):

    κ=po−pe1−pe,pe=∑cPhuman(c) Pjudge(c)\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = \sum_{c} P_{\text{human}}(c)\,P_{\text{judge}}(c)κ=1−pe​po​−pe​​,pe​=∑c​Phuman​(c)Pjudge​(c)

    Report two more numbers: the share of human-fail items the judge also fails, and the share of human-pass items it passes.

  4. Read every disagreement, change the rubric or examples, and run again.

  5. Freeze the judge (model version, prompt, settings) and keep the golden set for later re-checks.

The plot shows why step 3 asks for more than raw agreement. When failures are rare, a judge that never fails anything already agrees with humans almost every time. Hamel's illustration: if errors occur in 5% of examples, an always-pass judge agrees 95% of the time while detecting none of them. He starts with around 30 examples to find failure modes, aims for about 100 examples per failure mode when validating, and warns that below about 60 examples the confidence intervals are often too wide.

Known biases and what to do about them

  • Position bias. Zheng et al. judged pairs of very similar answers (two GPT-3.5 samples per question) twice with the order swapped. GPT-4 gave the same winner in both orders 65.0% of the time. Claude-v1 did so 23.8% of the time and preferred the first slot in 75.0% of cases. Fix: call the judge twice with the order swapped and count a win only if both calls agree, otherwise a tie.
  • Verbosity bias. When a list answer was padded with repeated, reworded items, Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 preferred the padded version 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7% (23 answers). Fix: state in the rubric that length is not quality, and check whether judge scores correlate with output length in your own data.
  • Self-preference. Zheng et al. report that GPT-4 favoured its own answers with a 10% higher win rate and Claude-v1 with a 25% higher one, though the authors say their limited data cannot settle whether this is self-enhancement. In the G-Eval paper GPT-4 scored GPT-3.5 summaries above human-written ones even where people preferred the human text. Fix: a code comment in Anthropic's evaluation guide says it is generally best practice to grade with a different model than the one that wrote the output (Anthropic docs (opens in a new tab)).
  • Weak reasoning. In the paper's math test (10 questions, each judged in both orders) the default GPT-4 judge accepted an incorrect answer in 14 of 20 judgements; chain-of-thought cut that to 6 and a reference answer to 3. Fix: give the judge a reference.

Running a judge in a live system

Pin the judge model version and prompt, and re-run the golden set whenever either changes, because scores from different judges are not comparable. Offline, managed services such as Amazon Bedrock (opens in a new tab) run evaluation jobs in which a judge model scores responses to a prompt dataset you supply and explains each score. In production, grade a sample rather than everything; the examples below include a monitoring product that samples live observations. Finally, treat the judge as a metric that can be gamed: if you tune a prompt or a model to raise the judge's score, re-check with fresh human labels (see Evaluating RAG Systems for the same check on retrieval pipelines).

Worked example: Calibrate a pass/fail judge on 20 labelled answers, then test it for position bias

This example runs the loop from the diagram with the standard library only. The data is invented for teaching, and the judge verdicts are recorded in the script, so you can reproduce every number without calling a model.

The scenario

A support assistant answers refund questions using a policy excerpt. An expert labelled 20 answers against one rule: every claim must be supported by the excerpt. Items 4, 9, 13, 17 and 20 failed and the other 15 passed. You have two drafts of the judge:

  • Draft v1 asks "Is this answer good and accurate? Reply PASS or FAIL." It failed only item 9.
  • Draft v2 uses the prompt from the previous section, with the explicit rule and a critique before the verdict. It failed items 4, 6, 9, 13 and 20.

Step 1: measure agreement

Save this as calibrate.py and run python3 calibrate.py.

python
import math
from collections import Counter

N_ITEMS = 20
HUMAN_FAIL = {4, 9, 13, 17, 20}      # items the expert marked "fail"
JUDGE_V1_FAIL = {9}                  # recorded verdicts, draft v1
JUDGE_V2_FAIL = {4, 6, 9, 13, 20}    # recorded verdicts, draft v2


def labels(fail_items):
    return ["fail" if i in fail_items else "pass" for i in range(1, N_ITEMS + 1)]


def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    """95% Wilson score interval for a proportion k/n."""
    p = k / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
    return max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half)


def report(name, human, judge):
    n = len(human)
    p_o = sum(h == j for h, j in zip(human, judge)) / n
    p_e = (human.count("pass") / n) * (judge.count("pass") / n) \
        + (human.count("fail") / n) * (judge.count("fail") / n)
    kappa = (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
    fails = [i for i, h in enumerate(human) if h == "fail"]
    passes = [i for i, h in enumerate(human) if h == "pass"]
    caught = sum(judge[i] == "fail" for i in fails)
    kept = sum(judge[i] == "pass" for i in passes)
    low, high = wilson(caught, len(fails))
    print(f"{name:11s} agreement={p_o:.2f} kappa={kappa:.3f} "
          f"fails caught={caught}/{len(fails)} (95% CI {low:.2f}-{high:.2f}) "
          f"passes kept={kept}/{len(passes)}")


human = labels(HUMAN_FAIL)
report("always pass", human, labels(set()))
report("judge v1", human, labels(JUDGE_V1_FAIL))
report("judge v2", human, labels(JUDGE_V2_FAIL))

# Position check: each pair was judged twice, the second time with the answers swapped.
# A pick is the slot the judge chose. Answer a sat in slot A on call 1 and in slot B on call 2.
PICKS = [("A", "B"), ("A", "A"), ("B", "A"), ("A", "B"), ("A", "A"),
         ("A", "B"), ("B", "B"), ("B", "A"), ("A", "B"), ("A", "A")]


def verdict(first, second):
    if first == "A" and second == "B":
        return "a"      # answer a won in both orders
    if first == "B" and second == "A":
        return "b"      # answer b won in both orders
    return "tie"        # the winner changed with the order: treat as position-driven


print()
print("a wins if only the first order is used:", sum(first == "A" for first, _ in PICKS), "of", len(PICKS))
print("with swapping:", dict(Counter(verdict(*pick) for pick in PICKS)))
print("always slot A:", sum(p == ("A", "A") for p in PICKS),
      "| always slot B:", sum(p == ("B", "B") for p in PICKS),
      "| consistent:", sum(verdict(*p) != "tie" for p in PICKS))

Step 2: check position bias

The second half of the script judged 10 pairs twice, swapping the order. If you had used only the first order, answer a would appear to win 7 of 10 comparisons. With swapping it wins 4, answer b wins 2 and 4 are ties. Only 6 of the 10 pairs produced the same winner in both orders, and of the 4 that did not, 3 picked slot A both times and 1 picked slot B both times. Most of the apparent advantage for a came from the slot it was shown in. These picks are invented to show the procedure; Zheng et al. measured 65.0% consistency for GPT-4 on their test pairs. Report the ties, use the swapped verdicts, and add worked examples to the prompt, which raised GPT-4's consistency from 65.0% to 77.5% in the paper.

Edge cases and limitations

The judge and the generator come from the same model family
Zheng et al. report that GPT-4 favoured its own answers with a 10% higher win rate and Claude-v1 with a 25% higher one (the authors caution that their limited data cannot settle whether this is self-enhancement), and the G-Eval authors saw GPT-4 prefer GPT-3.5 summaries over human-written ones that people preferred, in a test where the human annotators themselves agreed only weakly. The AlpacaEval README warns that its evaluators may favour outputs from similar models. Use a judge from a different family than the system under test, or at least check on your golden set that the judge does not score its own family's outputs higher than humans do.
Optimising against the judge turns the metric into a target
In the length-controlled AlpacaEval paper, the win rate of GPT-4 Turbo moved between 22.9% and 64.3% just by changing how verbose the prompt told it to be. OpenAI's grader documentation likewise warns about grader hacking and reward hacking when a model is optimised against a grader (the page also announces that OpenAI is deprecating graders in its evals and fine-tuning workflows, but the warning applies to any judge). If a score rises while human spot checks do not, suspect the judge and compare outputs by hand before celebrating.
Judges miss defects they are meant to catch
Eugene Yan's survey cites studies in which a model identified only 30 to 60% of inconsistent summaries and gpt-3.5-turbo reached 58.5% accuracy at telling factual summaries from hallucinated ones. A judge can be fluent and still have low recall on exactly the failures you care about. Measure the share of known-bad outputs it fails, and plant known-bad examples in the golden set on purpose.
Human labels are noisy, so perfect agreement is not the target
On first-turn MT-bench votes that were not ties, Zheng et al. report 81% agreement between two human experts and 85% between GPT-4 and humans; counting ties, the figures are 63% and 66%. A judge cannot be validated beyond the consistency of the people who labelled the data. Have two people label a subset, measure their kappa, and treat that as the ceiling the judge is compared with.
Changing the judge silently changes the metric
A model upgrade, a provider-side update or a one-line prompt edit shifts every score, so a dashboard line can jump without any change in the product. Pin the judge model version, store the prompt with the results, and when you must change the judge, score the same golden set and a sample of old outputs with both versions before switching.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The quality you care about cannot be decided by code, such as faithfulness to a source, tone or helpfulness, and there are more outputs than people can read.
  • You are comparing two prompts, models or retrieval settings on a fixed set of test inputs and need a repeatable verdict on every change.
  • You want to monitor a sample of live traffic for regressions and send only the flagged items to human reviewers.
  • You can label at least a few dozen outputs yourself, so the judge can be checked against your own judgement before anyone relies on it.

Think twice when

  • A rule can decide: exact match, schema validation, unit tests for generated code or arithmetic. Code checks are cheaper, faster and fully reproducible.
  • The judge would be the only safeguard on a high-stakes output such as medical, legal or safety-critical text. Judges miss defects, so a person must review.
  • Nobody can label even a small sample. Without human labels you cannot tell whether the judge measures what you intend.

A simpler alternative. Start with code-based checks (format, required fields, keywords, schema) and a human read of 30 to 50 outputs per release. Add a judge when the same kind of failure keeps appearing in those reviews and is too subtle for code, and keep the human sample running to check the judge.

Key takeaways

  • A judge is a model applying a written rubric to another model's output as a score, a pass or fail verdict or a choice between two answers, and the quality of the rubric decides most of its consistency.
  • Never trust a judge before comparing it with human labels on a golden set, and report Cohen's kappa and the recall on failures and passes, because raw agreement looks high whenever failures are rare.
  • Judges have documented biases toward the first answer, longer answers and their own model family, so swap the answer order, control for length and grade with a different model than the one that wrote the output.
  • Few-level scales and pass or fail verdicts with a critique before the verdict are easier to define, calibrate and parse than fine-grained scores.
  • Treat the judge as a pinned, versioned instrument: re-run the golden set when its model or prompt changes and keep sampling fresh human labels, because optimising against a judge invites gaming.

Example 1 of 5

GPT-4 as a pairwise judge for MT-bench and Chatbot Arena

MT-Bench and Chatbot ArenaDocumented in a paper

The paper by Zheng et al. (2023), which gave the approach its name, tests strong models as judges of chat assistants on two sets of conversations: MT-bench, a set of 80 multi-turn questions in eight categories, and Chatbot Arena, where users vote between two anonymous models. Its sections on judge variants, biases and agreement describe the setup and results. [1]

The problem

Open-ended chat answers have no reference string, so benchmarks with fixed answers do not show which assistant people prefer, and collecting human votes is slow and expensive. The authors ask whether a strong model can stand in for human preferences closely enough to be useful, and which biases make it unreliable.

Where this topic fits

The judge is the scoring step of the benchmark. For each question it reads two assistant answers, or one answer in single-answer grading, and returns a verdict. Its verdicts are then compared with votes from human experts and Chatbot Arena users to measure agreement. Swapped answer order, few-shot examples, chain-of-thought and reference answers are the mitigations tested on top.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Build the question set: 80 MT-bench questions with two turns each, across eight categories including writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math and coding.
  2. Collect answers from several assistants and have the judge compare answer pairs for the same question.
  3. Run the judge twice per pair with the order swapped, and treat a pair whose winner changes with the order as inconsistent. In the paper's first agreement setup (S1) such pairs count as ties.
  4. Compare the judge's verdicts with human expert votes in two settings, one that keeps ties and one that keeps only non-tie votes.
  5. Probe for biases with controlled tests: swapping very similar answers, padding a list answer with repeated items, and comparing each judge's behaviour on its own answers.
  6. Apply mitigations where the tests show weakness: chain-of-thought or a reference answer for math questions, and swapped runs plus few-shot examples for order effects.

Why this approach fits

Pairwise comparison avoids asking the judge for an absolute grade, which the authors note is less stable than a relative one. It also matches how Chatbot Arena collects human votes, with two answers and one preference, so judge and human verdicts can be compared directly.

Limitation or tradeoff

The paper names position and verbosity biases and weak reasoning on math questions as limits, and says its data cannot settle whether self-enhancement bias exists. Agreement depends on how ties are handled, with 85% against humans when ties are dropped and 66% when they are counted on first-turn MT-bench votes. The judges tested were 2023 models, so the absolute numbers should not be assumed for current judges.

What the sources document

  • The paper evaluates LLM judges on MT-bench, 80 multi-turn questions across eight categories, and on Chatbot Arena, where users pose a question to two anonymous models and vote for the response they prefer. [1]
  • It describes three judge variants: pairwise comparison, single-answer grading and reference-guided grading. [1]
  • The abstract reports that a strong judge such as GPT-4 can match human preferences with over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement as between two humans. [1]
  • For first-turn MT-bench votes the paper reports 85% agreement between GPT-4 pairwise and humans when ties are excluded, against 81% between two humans, and 66% against 63% when ties are included. [1]

Databricks testing GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 as judges of documentation Q&A answers

The problem

A chatbot that answers questions about product documentation faces a different question every time, and every change to its prompt, retriever or model needs a quality check. The judge sits at the end of the evaluation loop. How it is applied, step by step

  1. Build a benchmark of 100 questions with context drawn from Databricks documentation.
  2. Write a rubric with three components, correctness, comprehensiveness and readability, combined with weights of 60%, 20% and 20%.

Length-controlled AlpacaEval correcting verbosity bias on a public leaderboard

AlpacaEval (tatsu-lab)Documented in a paper

AlpacaEval is an open-source benchmark in which a GPT-4-based judge compares each model's answers with a reference model's answers on 805 instructions. Dubois et al. (2024) describe its length-controlled win rate, and the project's README documents the default judge, the metric and its caveats. [3][4]

The problem

Judges prefer longer answers, so a leaderboard that reports the judge's raw win rate rewards verbosity, and a developer can raise a model's rank by telling it to write more. The leaderboard exists to give a cheap stand-in for human preference, so a metric that can be gamed this easily defeats its purpose.

Where this topic fits

The judge is the leaderboard's scoring function: for each instruction it sees the evaluated model's answer and the baseline's answer and returns a preference between them (the README says its weighted GPT-4 Turbo annotator turns token log-probabilities into a continuous preference). The length correction is a statistical step applied after the judge, which estimates what the win rate would have been if both answers had the same length.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Collect the judge's pairwise preferences between the evaluated model and the baseline across the 805 instructions.
  2. Fit a generalised linear model that predicts the preference from the model that wrote the answer, the difference in length between the two answers, and the difficulty of the instruction.
  3. Remove the length term from the fitted model and compute the win rate it implies. That value is the length-controlled win rate.
  4. Validate the corrected metric against an external human-preference ranking, Chatbot Arena, using a Spearman rank correlation.
  5. Test robustness by changing the verbosity instruction given to one model and measuring how far its win rate moves before and after the correction.

Why this approach fits

The judge's preference for length is a measurable, systematic bias, so a regression can estimate what the verdict would have been at equal length. The approach keeps the cheap automatic judge and removes the easiest way to game it, without re-judging anything.

Limitation or tradeoff

A regression can only correct the confounders it models, and length is only one of them. The README warns that the evaluators tend to favour style over factuality, may prefer outputs of similar models, and do not measure harm. Its evaluator table also shows that human annotators prefer the longer output with probability 0.64, against 0.68 for the alpaca_eval_gpt4 annotator, so part of the length preference may reflect a real human taste rather than a judge flaw.

What the sources document

  • AlpacaEval contains 805 instructions and computes win rates as the fraction of times a strong LLM prefers a model's outputs over a reference model's; the default AlpacaEval 2.0 annotator is based on GPT-4 Turbo. [4]
  • Before correction, the win rate of GPT-4 Turbo fluctuated from 22.9% to 64.3% when only the verbosity instruction in its prompt was varied; after length control it fluctuated only from 41.9% to 51.6%. [3]
  • The method fits a generalised linear model with terms for the model that produced the answer, the length difference and the instruction difficulty, then removes the length term and computes the implied win rate. [3]
  • Controlling for length raised the Spearman correlation of AlpacaEval with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98. [3]
  • The README's evaluator table gives a probability of 0.68 that the alpaca_eval_gpt4 annotator prefers the longer output, against 0.64 for human annotations, and it states that AlpacaEval measures instruction following rather than harm. [4]

Honeycomb Query Assistant judge calibrated against a domain expert's critiques

The problem

Query Assistant has to generate useful queries from free-form questions, and the team needed a way to tell whether the AI was generating good queries. A generic quality score would not reflect what an expert at the company considers a good query, which is why the guide anchors the judge on that expert's pass or fail judgements and written critiques.

Where this topic fits

The judge is a binary classifier over pairs of a natural-language question and the query the assistant generated for it. It is built from the expert's labelled examples and checked against the expert before it is used to scan more data, so the calibration step is the heart of the case.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A domain expert reviews question and generated-query pairs and records a pass or fail for each, with a written critique that explains the reason.
  2. A first judge prompt is written that includes a few of the expert's critiques as examples (the post's example prompt has three), each showing the reasoning before the pass or fail.
  3. A spreadsheet with the question, the generated query, the critique and the outcome is shared with the expert, who fills in their own version so that the two sets of labels can be compared row by row.
  4. Compare the two sets of labels, read the disagreements, and revise the judge prompt.
  5. Stop when agreement is high enough. The post reports more than 90% agreement between the judge and the expert after three iterations.

Why this approach fits

A binary pass or fail with a written reason is a judgement an expert can give quickly and consistently, and the written reasons double as examples that carry the expert's standard into the judge prompt. Whether a query is good for this product's users is a question only someone who knows them can define, so the judge has to be built from that person's labels.

Limitation or tradeoff

The more-than-90% figure is raw agreement, which the same guide warns can mislead when failures are rare, and it rests on one expert's labels. The post gives no per-iteration figures, no count of the rows compared and no count of failures among them, so we cannot tell how many failures the judge actually caught. Example 5 of 5

Langfuse scoring sampled production observations with judge evaluators

LangfuseDocumented in official docs

Langfuse is an LLM observability platform, and its documentation describes LLM-as-a-judge evaluators that automatically score observations captured from a live application. [13]

The problem

Once an LLM application is live, quality problems appear in real traffic that no test set anticipated, and nobody can read every trace. Teams need an automatic quality signal on production data that is attached to the traces themselves, so that bad cases can be found, filtered and reviewed.

Where this topic fits

The judge runs after the application has answered, outside the request path. An evaluator defines the judge prompt, model, score definition and variable mappings. An evaluation rule then chooses which live observations are scored, through filters and a sampling rate.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Set up an LLM connection that the platform uses to call the judge model.
  2. Define an evaluator: a judge prompt that holds the criteria and variables for the input and output to grade, plus a score definition (numeric, categorical or boolean).
  3. Map the prompt variables to fields of the observation, such as its input, output or metadata.
  4. Create an evaluation rule with filters and a sampling rate, so that only matching live observations are judged.
  5. A background worker takes each matching observation from a queue, renders the prompt, calls the judge and writes the score, with its reasoning, back to the observation.
  6. Reviewers filter for low scores, read the flagged traces, and compare the judge's scores with human annotation scores on the same data to see whether the judge can be trusted.

Why this approach fits

Filters and a sampling rate keep the cost of judge calls proportional to the slice of traffic you choose, and running the judge in a worker means grading never slows a user's request. Attaching the score and its reasoning to the observation lets a team move straight from a bad score to the trace that produced it.

Limitation or tradeoff

Live traffic usually has no reference answers, so the judge grades without ground truth. A judge prompt that was never compared with human annotations returns plausible numbers that may not track quality, which is why the documentation itself advises calibrating against labelled examples. Every scored observation costs a judge call, so the sampling rate is a cost control as well as a statistical choice.

What the sources document

  • An evaluator defines how data is scored through its judge prompt, model, score definition and default variable mappings, and evaluation rules define which live observations are evaluated through filters, a sampling rate and evaluator assignments. [13]
  • Evaluators are executed by the Langfuse worker, which picks up each matching observation from a queue, renders the evaluation prompt, calls the model through the project's LLM connection and writes the score back. [13]
  • Scores can be numeric, categorical or boolean, and the judge returns a score together with reasoning that explains its assessment. [13]
  • Observation-level evaluators are the recommended target for live production data, and trace-level evaluators are deprecated. [13]
  • The documentation advises calibrating an evaluator against labelled examples, or comparing its scores with human annotation scores on the same data in Score Analytics. [13]

4Common mistakes

  1. Using a judge without ever comparing it with human labels

    Why it causes problems
    The scores look precise and move with every change, but nothing shows that they measure quality. A judge can be consistently wrong, for example by rewarding length or fluent but unsupported text, and a team can ship or roll back real changes based on those numbers.
    How to recognise it
    Ask how many outputs a person labelled and what the judge's agreement with them was. If nobody can answer, the judge is uncalibrated.
    How to fix it
    Label a golden set that includes known failures, report kappa and the recall on failures and on passes, and repeat the check whenever the judge model or prompt changes.

  2. Reporting only raw agreement or accuracy against human labels

    Why it causes problems
    When failures are rare, a judge that passes everything agrees with humans almost all the time. A high agreement figure then hides a judge that finds none of the defects you built it to find.
    How to recognise it
    Compare the judge with an always-pass baseline on the same labels. If the margin is small, or the judge fails only a fraction of the human-fail items, raw agreement is flattering it.
    How to fix it
    Report kappa plus the share of human-fail items the judge fails and the share of human-pass items it passes, and label enough failures that those shares have usable confidence intervals.

  3. Asking one prompt to rate overall quality from 1 to 10

    Why it causes problems
    The judge weighs the hidden criteria differently on every call, scores cluster on a few values, and when the average drops you cannot tell which quality got worse.
    How to recognise it
    Two people given the same rubric cannot say what separates a 6 from a 7, and the judge's scores take only three or four distinct values across hundreds of outputs.
    How to fix it
    Write one judge per criterion, use pass or fail or three to five levels with each level described, ask for the reasoning before the verdict, and include one worked example per level.

  4. Comparing two answers in only one order, or grading with the model that wrote them

    Why it causes problems
    Position bias and self-preference turn the comparison into a test of the judge's habits. A change that only moves an answer into the first slot, or only matches the judge's own style, can look like an improvement.
    How to recognise it
    Swap the order for a sample of pairs and see how often the winner changes, and score outputs from two different model families on items that humans rated equal.
    How to fix it
    Run every pair in both orders and count a win only when both agree, and use a judge from a different model family than the system under test.

  5. Treating small score changes as real improvements

    Why it causes problems
    Judge scores vary with sampling, prompt wording and small test sets, so a gain of a few points on 50 items can be noise. Teams then keep prompt changes that did nothing, or discard ones that helped.
    How to recognise it
    Re-run the same judge on the same outputs, with different seeds or sampling, and compare the spread with the improvement you are celebrating.
    How to fix it
    Report intervals or repeated-run spread, require a gain larger than the run-to-run variation, and confirm the items that changed verdict by reading them.

Audit a judge from its logs with agreement, kappa and a swap test

HUMAN_FAIL = {2, 5, 11, 14, 19, 23}      # items 1..24 the expert marked "fail"

For kappa, compute p_e as the sum over the two labels of the share the human gave that label times the share the judge gave it.

Hint 2

A pair is consistent only when the picks are (A, B), which means a won, or (B, A), which means b won. Every other combination is a tie.

python
from collections import Counter

HUMAN_FAIL = {2, 5, 11, 14, 19, 23}
JUDGE_FAIL = {2, 5, 7, 14, 15, 19}
PICKS = [("A", "B"), ("A", "B"), ("B", "A"), ("A", "A"), ("A", "B"), ("B", "B"),
         ("A", "B"), ("A", "A"), ("B", "A"), ("A", "B"), ("A", "A"), ("A", "B")]


def labels(fail_items, n=24):
    return ["fail" if i in fail_items else "pass" for i in range(1, n + 1)]


def agreement_report(human, judge):
    n = len(human)
    p_o = sum(h == j for h, j in zip(human, judge)) / n
    p_e = sum((human.count(c) / n) * (judge.count(c) / n) for c in ("pass", "fail"))
    kappa = 0.0 if p_e == 1 else (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
    fails = [i for i, h in enumerate(human) if h == "fail"]
    passes = [i for i, h in enumerate(human) if h == "pass"]
    return {
        "agreement": round(p_o, 3),
        "kappa": round(kappa, 3),
        "fail_recall": round(sum(judge[i] == "fail" for i in fails) / len(fails), 3),
        "pass_recall": round(sum(judge[i] == "pass" for i in passes) / len(passes), 3),
    }


def swap_report(picks):
    def outcome(first, second):
        if (first, second) == ("A", "B"):
            return "a"
        if (first, second) == ("B", "A"):
            return "b"
        return "tie"
    results = Counter(outcome(*p) for p in picks)
    n = len(picks)
    return {
        "a_wins_first_order_only": sum(f == "A" for f, _ in picks),
        "with_swapping": dict(results),
        "consistency": round((n - results["tie"]) / n, 3),
        "slot_a_both_times": sum(p == ("A", "A") for p in picks),
        "slot_b_both_times": sum(p == ("B", "B") for p in picks),
    }


human, judge = labels(HUMAN_FAIL), labels(JUDGE_FAIL)
print("always pass", agreement_report(human, labels(set())))
judge_stats = agreement_report(human, judge)
print("judge      ", judge_stats)
swap = swap_report(PICKS)
print("swap       ", swap)
ready = judge_stats["kappa"] >= 0.6 and judge_stats["fail_recall"] >= 0.8 and swap["consistency"] >= 0.75
print("ready" if ready else "not ready")

Exercise 2

Design a judge, golden set and bias controls for ticket summaries

DesignIntermediateAbout 45 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Your company's support tool uses an LLM to summarise each customer ticket in two sentences for the agent who picks it up. Quality problems you have seen: summaries that invent a detail the customer never wrote, summaries that leave out the customer's actual request, and summaries that are tidy but too long. You have 5,000 tickets a week and one senior agent who can label about 60 summaries per week.

Write a one-page evaluation design on paper or in a text file that covers the rubric, the judging format, the golden set, the judge model choice, the bias controls and the acceptance rule. No code or paid services are needed.

Requirements

  • Define one judge per criterion for at least two criteria and say, for each, whether it is pointwise pass or fail, a short scale or pairwise, with a one-sentence reason.
  • Write the rubric text for one of the judges, including the rule, the reply format and when to use a reference or the ticket text as context.
  • Describe the golden set: size, how you pick the tickets, how many failures it should contain, who labels them and how you check the labeller's consistency.
  • Name the biases that apply to this task, and the control for each, including how you choose the judge model relative to the summariser.
  • State the acceptance rule for putting the judge into use and the plan for checking it after launch.

Expected behaviour

A good design separates faithfulness from completeness and length, uses pass or fail with a critique for faithfulness against the ticket text, and does not ask for a single quality score. It sets a labelling plan the one senior agent can actually deliver, includes enough known failures to measure recall on them, and states numeric acceptance thresholds. It uses a judge from a different model family than the summariser and addresses verbosity, because one of the observed problems is length.

Hint 1

Faithfulness can be checked against the ticket text, so the judge receives the ticket as context and needs no separate reference summary.

Hint 2

With 60 labels per week, plan the golden set over several weeks and oversample suspected failures so the set contains enough of them.

Hint 3

Length is directly measurable, so ask whether it needs a judge at all.

Show solution

One acceptable design follows. Yours may differ if each choice is justified.

Judges. Faithfulness: pointwise pass or fail against the ticket text, because a claim is either supported or not, and a binary verdict is the easiest to calibrate. Completeness: pass or fail on whether the customer's main request appears in the summary. Length needs no judge: a code check that counts sentences or words. Using code for it is cheaper and exact.

Faithfulness rubric. Context: the ticket text in tags. Rule: PASS only if every factual claim in the summary, including names, amounts and dates, is supported by the ticket. FAIL if it adds any detail the customer did not write. Reply with a critique of one to three sentences that quotes the unsupported claim, then VERDICT: PASS or FAIL, parsed strictly. Two or three expert critiques go in as examples.

Golden set. Aim for about 200 summaries labelled over four weeks at 50 to 60 per week. Sample tickets across categories and lengths, and oversample summaries flagged by simple heuristics, such as numbers in the summary that do not appear in the ticket, so that at least 60 of the 200 are known failures. Have the senior agent relabel a random 40 after a gap of a few weeks, or ask a second agent to label them, and compute the kappa between the two sets, which sets the ceiling for the judge.

Biases. Verbosity: the rubric states that length is not quality, and the code length check is separate. Self-preference: use a judge from a different model family than the summariser. Position bias does not apply to a pointwise verdict. If you later compare two summariser prompts pairwise, run each pair in both orders and count disagreement as a tie.

Acceptance and monitoring. Accept the judge when kappa against the expert is at least 0.6 and it fails at least 80% of the human-fail items, with confidence intervals reported. Pin the judge model and prompt. After launch, judge a sample of live summaries, send every FAIL and a random 20 passes per week to the senior agent, and re-run the golden set whenever the judge or the summariser changes.

Check your answer

  • Did you give each criterion its own judge instead of one overall score?
  • Could the one senior agent realistically produce your golden set in the time you planned?
  • Does the golden set contain enough known failures to measure how many the judge catches?
  • Did you choose a judge from a different model family than the summariser, and say why?
  • Did you leave length to a code check, or justify using a judge for it?
