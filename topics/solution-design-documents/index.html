Solution Design Docs, PRDs and Technical Writing

A solution design document set is the written record that carries a piece of work from problem to build. A PRD states the problem and how success will be checked, a design doc explains how the solution will be built and which trade-offs were accepted, and an architecture decision record (ADR) captures one significant decision with its context and consequences.

1Overview

Why it matters

A forward deployed engineer designs inside someone else's organisation and then leaves. The customer's security team has to approve a data flow they did not design. The sponsor has to sign off a scope they will be held to. An engineer who joins in month four has to change the system without breaking a constraint nobody told them about. If the reasoning lives only in calls and chat threads, each of those people must either trust you blindly or ask you again.

The price of a wrong design also rises once code exists. The ISE engineering playbook from Microsoft, whose design-review guidance expects customer subject-matter experts to take part, gives a concrete case: choosing Azure Kubernetes Service instead of App Services at the design phase likely only requires updating documentation, while making that pivot after implementation has started is much more costly (ISE playbook, design reviews (opens in a new tab)). A short document, reviewed by the right people before the build, is the cheapest place to discover a missed requirement, a system with no API, or two stakeholders who want opposite things. Anyone who has to get a technical plan agreed by people outside their own team needs this skill: forward deployed and solutions engineers, tech leads, and product engineers who work with customers.

Before you start

  • Technical Scoping and SequencingIntermediate

What you will be able to do

  • Choose between a PRD, a design doc and an ADR for a given question, and name the readers of each.
  • Write a one-page PRD with evidence, measurable goals, non-goals and requirements that each have an acceptance check.
  • Structure a design doc around context, goals and non-goals, the design, alternatives considered and cross-cutting concerns.
  • Record a decision as an ADR with a status, context, decision and consequences, and supersede it without rewriting history.
  • Run a review with named approvers, a closing date and a recorded outcome.
  • Keep each kind of document trustworthy after approval with an owner, a status and a review date.

Key terms

PRD
A product requirements document, which states the problem, who has it, the goals, what is out of scope and the requirements a solution must meet.
Design doc
A document written before coding that explains how a system or change will be built and which trade-offs the author accepted.
RFC
Request for comments, a name many teams give to a design doc that is circulated for feedback before work is approved.
ADR
An architecture decision record, a one-to-two-page note that captures a single significant decision, the situation that forced it and its consequences.
Decision log
The numbered collection of a project's ADRs, read in order as the history of how the system came to be the way it is.
Non-goal
Something a reader could reasonably expect the work to achieve that the author has deliberately excluded.
Alternatives considered
The section of a design doc that lists the other designs that were weighed and the reason each one lost.
Cross-cutting concern
A quality such as security, privacy or observability that affects every part of a design instead of belonging to one component.
Approver
A named person whose yes is required before work proceeds, as opposed to a reviewer who comments or a reader who is informed.
Freshness date
A note in a document recording when it was last reviewed and by whom, so readers can judge whether to trust it.

2Explanation

Introduction

Think about the last time you joined something already under way: a codebase, a spreadsheet model, a shared kitchen. Sooner or later you met a choice that looked odd, asked why, and heard "it was like that when I arrived". Michael Nygard describes where that leaves a newcomer on a software project. With the decision in front of them and no record of the reasons, they can accept it blindly or change it blindly, and neither is safe (Documenting Architecture Decisions (opens in a new tab)).

Teams avoid this with a small set of written documents, each answering a different question:

  • A PRD (product requirements document) says what problem is being solved, for whom, and how everyone will know it has been solved.
  • A design doc says how the solution will be built and which trade-offs were accepted.
  • An ADR (architecture decision record) captures one significant decision: the situation, the choice and its consequences.

The names vary more than the ideas. A design doc may be called an RFC (request for comments), a tech spec or a one-pager. HashiCorp reads PRD as "Problem Requirements Document" (PRD template (opens in a new tab)). Kubernetes folds the tracking record, the requirements and the design into one file called a KEP (KEP process (opens in a new tab)). The three questions stay the same.

For a forward deployed engineer these documents do a job that conversation cannot. By the time you have scoped a first slice, the people who must agree to it sit in two organisations: a sponsor who cares about the outcome, an IT or security team who care what touches their systems, engineers who will build it, and a team who will run it after you leave. They read at different times and for different reasons, and most read only the part that concerns them. A document that tells each reader what is being asked of them gets agreement without a meeting for every question, and it keeps the reasoning available after the engagement ends.

This lesson covers five things: choosing which document a question needs, the structure of each, writing for readers who skim, running a review that ends in a decision, and keeping documents true after approval. The worked example takes a PRD, a design doc and ADRs for one small system, checks them with a script and repairs what the check finds.

How it works

Three documents carry a piece of work from "we understand the problem" to "someone else can run this". They differ in the question they answer, in who reads them and in what happens to them after approval.

1. Decide whether to write, and which document

Writing costs time, so ask what would go wrong without the document. Malte Ubl's account of design docs at Google says one is worth writing when the benefits (agreement on the design, a record, review by experienced engineers) outweigh the extra work, and that it is not needed when the solution is not ambiguous (Design Docs at Google (opens in a new tab)). HashiCorp's rule of thumb: if implementing the idea takes less time than writing it up, go ahead without an RFC, then consider writing one afterwards to keep a history of the work (HashiCorp (opens in a new tab)).

Let the open question pick the document:

  • What problem are we solving, and how will we know it is solved? Write a PRD.
  • How will we build it, and what did we trade away? Write a design doc.
  • Why did we choose X over Y? Write an ADR.

A small job can answer all three on one page, if each answer is easy to find.

2. Name the reader before you write

Google's technical writing course defines good documentation as the knowledge and skills your audience needs to do a task, minus what they already know (Audience (opens in a new tab)). It also names the curse of knowledge: experts forget what newcomers do not know, and their explanations leave out the steps and terms a new reader needs.

In a customer engagement one document usually has four kinds of reader:

ReaderWhat they need to decideWhere they look
SponsorWill this solve my problem, and at what risk?Summary, goals, non-goals
Customer IT and securityWhat touches our systems and data?Data flows, access, cross-cutting concerns
Engineers building itIs there enough detail to start?Design, alternatives, open questions
Whoever maintains it laterWhy is it built this way?ADRs

Three habits serve all four. Say who the document is for, and state its scope and non-scope. Put the conclusion in the first paragraph; the same course advises imagining that your peers might read only that paragraph (Documents (opens in a new tab)). Keep sentences short: one idea per sentence, a list instead of a chain of "and", and plain words such as now for at this point in time and can for is able to (Short sentences (opens in a new tab)). Applied to one sentence, 26 words become 10:

Before. At this point in time, the retrieval of order information is able to be performed by agents only by means of manually accessing two separate systems.

After. Agents look up each order by hand in two systems.

Amazon goes furthest. Werner Vogels describes product teams starting with the press release and FAQ that would accompany the launch, then working backwards towards documents that are closer to the implementation (Working Backwards (opens in a new tab)).

3. The PRD: problem, evidence, checks

Atlassian's template defines a PRD as a guide to the requirements of a product or feature, covering its purpose, features and functionality (Atlassian (opens in a new tab)). A PRD that fits on one page has these parts:

markdown
# PRD: <the outcome, not a feature name>
Owner | Status | Last reviewed | Approvers
## Summary                 three sentences a sponsor could repeat
## Problem and evidence    who is blocked, by what, with numbers from discovery
## Goals                   success metrics with thresholds
## Non-goals               what a reader might expect that is excluded
## Requirements            numbered, each with an acceptance check
## Assumptions and open questions

Two published templates show what to stress. HashiCorp calls the user research section the most important, because it grounds the document in problems users really have, and asks for acceptance criteria written like test cases (PRD template (opens in a new tab)). Basecamp's equivalent, the pitch, adds an appetite (the time the team wants to spend) and a list of no-gos (what is excluded) (Shape Up (opens in a new tab)). The requirements themselves come out of discovery; see Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering.

4. The design doc: the how and the trade-offs

Ubl's anatomy of a design doc is a sound default:

markdown
# Design: <system or change>
Owner | Status | Last reviewed | Approvers
## Context and scope        objective background; what is being built
## Goals and non-goals      link to the PRD; add technical non-goals
## Design                   overview first, then detail: a system
                            context diagram, APIs, data storage
## Alternatives considered  each option with the reason it lost
## Cross-cutting concerns   security, privacy, observability
## Open questions

Non-goals are not negated goals such as "the system should not crash". They are things that could reasonably be goals and were chosen not to be. Alternatives considered is where reviewers look hardest. Ubl's sign of a document that should not have been written is one that says "this is how we are going to implement it" and discusses no trade-offs. On length, he suggests around 10 to 20 pages for a larger project and 1 to 3 pages for a "mini design doc".

A design doc for an AI feature needs sections that general templates lack. Eugene Yan's template for machine learning design docs (opens in a new tab) adds data, experimentation and validation, human-in-the-loop, monitoring and cost. For an LLM feature, name the evaluation set and its pass threshold (see Evaluating LLM Applications), say what the system does when the model is wrong, and estimate the cost per request.

5. The ADR: one decision, kept as history

Nygard's format is one or two pages, stored in the repository and numbered in sequence:

markdown
# ADR-0007: <short noun phrase>
Status: proposed | accepted | deprecated | superseded by ADR-0012
## Context       the forces at play, stated as neutral facts
## Decision      "We will ..." in full sentences, active voice
## Consequences  what becomes easier and what becomes harder

One rule sets ADRs apart from every other document: an accepted ADR is not edited to say something new. AWS's guidance treats an accepted ADR as immutable. A new insight means a new ADR, and when that is accepted the old one is marked superseded (AWS Prescriptive Guidance (opens in a new tab)). The UK Government Digital Service allows clarifications, and lets a decision that was never implemented be updated in place if reviewers agree (GDS Way (opens in a new tab)). The MADR template adds two useful parts: considered options, and confirmation, which says how compliance with the decision can be checked (MADR (opens in a new tab)).

6. Review: who decides, and by when

A review does three jobs, which Google's engineering book separates: a technical review for accuracy by a subject expert, an audience review for clarity by someone unfamiliar with the domain, and a writing review for consistency (Software Engineering at Google, chapter 10 (opens in a new tab)). In an engagement, ask someone on the customer side to be the audience reviewer.

State four things when you send the document:

  1. The approvers, by name, separate from people who are only informed.
  2. The feedback you want: "direction only" on a first draft, "ready to approve" on a final one.
  3. The date on which comments close.
  4. Where the outcome will be recorded: the status line of the document, with any open conditions.

Hold the review in writing or in a meeting that starts with reading. The ISE playbook notes that reviews by pull request suit teams spread across time zones. For a meeting, AWS suggests starting with 10 to 15 minutes in which everyone reads the ADR and adds comments, after which the owner goes through each comment with the team. HashiCorp asks authors to acknowledge and resolve every comment, including the ones they decide not to act on.

7. Keep documents current, by type

Documents age in different ways, so each kind needs its own rule.

  • PRD and design doc. Update them while the work is in flight. Ubl's rule of thumb for design docs, which fits a PRD too: if the system has not shipped yet, definitely update the doc. After launch they are a record of intent. People are bad at updating documents, Ubl notes, so later changes often go into new documents linked from the original.
  • ADRs. Never rewritten, only superseded. The log is the history.
  • Reference documents such as a README, a runbook or an architecture overview. These must be true today, so they need an owner and a review date. Google attaches freshness dates recording when a document was last reviewed, with reminders after a set period such as three months.

Keep all three in the repository beside the code, so that a change in behaviour can update the document in the same pull request. The Google chapter calls this treating documentation like code: source control, clear owners, review on change.

Worked example: Tool-hire lookup panel: checking a PRD, a design doc and ADRs before review

This continues the teaching scenario from Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering. A tool-hire company's support agents spend most of an order-status ticket looking things up, and the scoped first slice is a read-only lookup panel. All data is invented.

The script holds five condensed documents (a real section is a paragraph, not a line) and checks four things before review: required sections, review age, owners and supersession, and whether the design addresses every requirement in the PRD. It needs only Python 3 and its standard library.

python
import re
from datetime import date

TODAY, MAX_AGE_DAYS = date(2026, 10, 10), 90
REQUIRED = {
    "prd": ["Summary", "Problem and evidence", "Goals", "Non-goals", "Requirements"],
    "design": ["Context and scope", "Goals and non-goals", "Design",
               "Alternatives considered", "Cross-cutting concerns"],
    "adr": ["Context", "Decision", "Consequences"],
}
DOCS = {
    "prd.md": ("prd", """# PRD: Order-status lookup for support agents
Owner: Priya (support lead) | Status: approved | Last reviewed: 2026-09-28
## Summary
Agents spend about 230 of 347 seconds per order-status ticket finding the order
in two systems. We want that lookup done for them.
## Problem and evidence
6 of 12 observed tickets were order status; lookup was 66.3% of their handling time.
## Goals
- Median handle time for order-status tickets <= 150 s over 50 consecutive tickets.
## Non-goals
- Drafting or sending replies. Writing anything to the ERP.
## Requirements
- R1: Show ERP status and latest tracking event within 5 s in 95 of 100 replayed tickets.
- R2: Show the matching order when the ticket contains a valid order number.
- R3: List candidate orders, none selected, when the sender matches no order or several.
- R4: Show ERP status labelled "tracking unavailable" while a carrier source is failing.
- R5: Issue only read requests to the ERP.
"""),
    "design.md": ("design", """# Design: Order-status lookup panel
Owner: Sam (engineer) | Status: in review | Last reviewed: 2026-10-02
Approvers: Priya (support lead), Imran (IT manager)
## Context and scope
A helpdesk sidebar shows order and tracking data for the open ticket. It never writes.
## Goals and non-goals
As in prd.md. Extra non-goal: no change to the ERP schema.
## Design
- The sidebar calls a lookup service with the ticket id; 5 s budget end to end (R1).
- The service extracts an order number from the ticket text and queries by it (R2).
- ERP access is one read-only account on one read-only view (R5, ADR-0001).
- Tracking comes from carrier APIs; on failure the panel shows the label (R4).
- Lookups are exported weekly to the BI dashboard (R6).
## Cross-cutting concerns
Security: read-only grant, nothing stored. Observability: log latency per lookup.
"""),
    "adr-0001.md": ("adr", """# ADR-0001: Read orders through a read-only ERP view
Status: accepted | Date: 2026-09-30
## Context
The ERP has no API. IT will not grant table access. A nightly export is up to 24 h old.
## Decision
We will query one read-only view with one read-only account.
## Consequences
Data is live. The panel depends on ERP uptime. IT owns the view definition.
"""),
    "adr-0002.md": ("adr", """# ADR-0002: Read tracking by scraping carrier portals
Status: superseded | Date: 2026-09-30
## Context
No carrier API keys were available in week 1.
## Decision
We will scrape the three portals.
## Consequences
The panel breaks whenever a portal changes its pages.
"""),
    "runbook.md": ("other", """# Runbook: lookup panel
Owner: unassigned | Status: draft | Last reviewed: 2026-05-04
"""),
}

findings = []
for name, (kind, text) in DOCS.items():
    headings = re.findall(r"^## (.+)$", text, flags=re.M)
    for section in REQUIRED.get(kind, []):
        if section not in headings:
            findings.append(f"{name}: missing section '{section}'")
    reviewed = re.search(r"Last reviewed: (\S+)", text)
    if reviewed:
        age = (TODAY - date.fromisoformat(reviewed.group(1))).days
        if age > MAX_AGE_DAYS:
            findings.append(f"{name}: last reviewed {age} days ago (limit {MAX_AGE_DAYS})")
    if "Owner: unassigned" in text:
        findings.append(f"{name}: no owner")
    if "Status: superseded" in text and not re.search(r"superseded by ADR-\d{4}", text):
        findings.append(f"{name}: superseded, but does not name its replacement")

in_prd = set(re.findall(r"\bR\d+\b", DOCS["prd.md"][1]))
in_design = set(re.findall(r"\bR\d+\b", DOCS["design.md"][1]))
for rid in sorted(in_prd - in_design):
    findings.append(f"design.md: requirement {rid} has no design element")
for rid in sorted(in_design - in_prd):
    findings.append(f"design.md: cites {rid}, which is not in prd.md")

print(f"{len(DOCS)} documents checked on {TODAY}: {len(findings)} findings")
for line in findings:
    print(" -", line)
text
5 documents checked on 2026-10-10: 6 findings
 - design.md: missing section 'Alternatives considered'
 - adr-0002.md: superseded, but does not name its replacement
 - runbook.md: last reviewed 159 days ago (limit 90)
 - runbook.md: no owner
 - design.md: requirement R3 has no design element
 - design.md: cites R6, which is not in prd.md

Repairing the findings

FindingWhy it mattersRepair
No alternatives sectionIT cannot see why a live view beats an exportAdd it, one line per rejected option
ADR-0002 has no successorThe design uses carrier APIs, and no record says whyWrite ADR-0003; mark ADR-0002 superseded by ADR-0003
Runbook is 159 days old, no owner4 May to 10 October is 159 days, past the 90-day limitName an owner, who reviews and re-dates it
R3 has no design elementThe unclear-sender case would surface in testingAdd a design line that cites R3
R6 is not in the PRDA BI export is scope the sponsor never approvedDelete the line, or propose a PRD change

The new record leaves the old reasoning readable:

markdown
# ADR-0003: Read tracking from carrier APIs
Status: accepted | Date: 2026-10-06
## Context
Two of three carriers issued API keys on 2026-10-01. In a week of testing, scraping
broke twice when a portal changed its page layout.
## Decision
We will call the two carrier APIs and show a link to the third carrier's portal.
## Consequences
Tracking for two carriers no longer depends on page layout. The third still needs
one click by the agent. We must rotate two API keys. This supersedes ADR-0002.

With the five repairs made, the script prints 6 documents checked on 2026-10-10: 0 findings.

What the result means. Zero findings says the documents are complete enough to deserve a reviewer's time. It does not say the design is right. Whether a read-only view is the right call is for Imran and Priya to judge, and the alternatives section is what lets them judge it.

Edge cases and limitations

The customer requires its own template or architecture review board
Large customers often have a mandatory format and a board that meets on a fixed schedule. Use their template for the submission, since their reviewers know where to look in it, and find out the board's dates in the first week because they can set your timeline. Keep your ADRs in the repository regardless; the board approves a snapshot, while the decision log has to keep growing after the approval.
The decision was made and built months ago, with no record
Write the ADR now. Spotify's engineers call this backfilling and note that such decisions can surface in code review, when someone introduces a competing pattern. Give the record today's date, say in the context that it documents an earlier decision, and write down only the reasons you can confirm with someone who was there. Label anything else as a guess.
The design changes after it was approved
If the change touches a goal, a non-goal, a data flow or an access right, the approval no longer covers what you are building. Update the document, add a dated change note at the top, and send that note to the approvers who care about the changed part. If only an internal detail changed, a new ADR linked from the design doc is enough.
The document would have to contain sensitive details
Hostnames, account names, network layouts and sample customer records make a design doc risky to share and easy to leak. Never paste credentials or real personal data. Refer to secrets by the name of the secret store entry, use invented sample records, and ask the customer where the document may be stored before you write it.
Prompts, model versions and evaluation thresholds change weekly
In an LLM feature a prompt or model change can alter behaviour as much as a code change. Rewriting the design doc each time is not workable. Keep the doc at the level of approach and acceptance thresholds, version prompts with the code, and write an ADR only when the approach itself changes, for example from a single prompt to retrieval or from one provider to another.
Two documents disagree
A PRD that says replies are never sent automatically and a design doc that describes an auto-send queue cannot both be current. Decide the order of authority in advance (the PRD for scope, the design doc for mechanism, accepted ADRs over both for the specific decision they record) and link between documents instead of copying text, so there is one place to correct.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • More than one team or organisation has to agree before work starts, and they cannot all attend the same conversation.
  • The decision is expensive to reverse, such as a data model, an integration method, a vendor or where customer data is processed.
  • The work will be handed to people who were not present when it was designed, which is the normal end of a forward deployed engagement.
  • There are real alternatives with different trade-offs, so a reviewer could reasonably ask why you did not choose another one.
  • The design touches security, privacy or compliance and will need sign-off from a specialist who reads documents, not code.

Think twice when

  • The solution is unambiguous and has no meaningful alternatives. Build it, and describe it in the pull request.
  • Implementing the idea would take less time than writing it up. Do the work, then consider a short ADR so there is a history.
  • You are building a throwaway prototype whose purpose is to learn. Record what you learned afterwards instead of designing up front.
  • The document is being used to postpone a decision that someone already has the evidence and authority to make.

A simpler alternative. A one-page mini design doc kept in the repository, with five short parts (problem, goals, non-goals, approach, one alternative and why it lost), plus one ADR for each decision that would be costly to reverse. This stops being enough when more than one team must approve, when customer IT or security needs to review data flows, or when the work will run past a few weeks.

Key takeaways

  • A PRD answers what problem is being solved and how success will be checked, a design doc answers how it will be built and what was traded away, and an ADR answers why one option was chosen.
  • Name the readers before writing, put the conclusion in the first paragraph, and state non-goals, because most readers look only at the part that concerns them.
  • The alternatives considered section is what shows a design was reasoned about, and a document with no trade-offs in it is an implementation manual.
  • An accepted ADR is not rewritten. A change of mind is a new ADR, and the old one is marked superseded with a link to its replacement.
  • A review needs named approvers, a closing date and a recorded outcome, and a conditional yes is often more useful to an author than an unexplained no.
  • Documents stay true when each has an owner, a status and a review date, and when it lives in the repository so it can change in the same pull request as the code.

Example 1 of 5

Google design docs: agreeing a design and its trade-offs before coding

GoogleDocumented in an engineering article

Malte Ubl's 2020 article Design Docs at Google describes how the company's engineers write and review design docs. The Documentation chapter of the book Software Engineering at Google, written by Tom Manshreck, describes the same practice from the documentation side. [1][2]

The problem

A design made by one engineer can miss things that other people know: a security or privacy constraint, an existing system that does the same job, a failure someone has seen before. Finding that out after the code is written is expensive. Ubl lists what the document is for: identifying design issues early while changes are still cheap, reaching consensus around a design, making sure cross-cutting concerns are considered, spreading the knowledge of senior engineers through the organisation, and forming an organisational memory of design decisions.

Where this topic fits

The design doc is written after the problem is understood and before coding starts. The book describes the discussion of a design document as a form of code review before any code is written. After approval the document is archived as a record, and the book suggests reviewing it before launch to check that the goals stated at design time are still the goals.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The primary author writes a relatively informal document before starting to code, usually in Google Docs so that others can comment.
  2. The document follows a loose anatomy: context and scope, goals and non-goals, the actual design, alternatives considered and cross-cutting concerns.
  3. Within the design section the author starts with an overview and then covers the parts where trade-offs were made, such as a system context diagram, APIs and data storage.
  4. The author iterates with close colleagues until the document reaches a first, relatively stable version.
  5. The document is shared with a wider audience for review. Reviews range from a message to the team list with discussion in comment threads to a formal meeting with senior engineers.
  6. Implementation starts. If the design has to change before the system ships, the author updates the document.
  7. Later changes usually go into new documents, so the original is linked to its amendments. The author re-reads the doc a year or two later to see what held up.

Why this approach fits

Text is cheaper to change than code, and a comment thread reaches more reviewers than a meeting can hold. The structure does part of the reviewing. A non-goals section forces a scope decision, and an alternatives section shows whether the author weighed options or wrote down the first idea. Reviewers can then spend their attention on the trade-offs.

Limitation or tradeoff

The practice has a cost, and the article says so: a design doc is overhead and is not worth writing when the solution is unambiguous. It also admits that people are bad at updating documents, so the written design drifts from the built system and the truth ends up spread across an original and its amendments. Both sources describe the practice and report no measurement of its effect on defects or delivery time.

What the sources document

  • Ubl describes design docs as relatively informal documents that the primary author or authors of a system create before starting the coding project, covering the high-level implementation strategy and key design decisions with emphasis on the trade-offs considered. [1]
  • The anatomy the article gives is context and scope, goals and non-goals, the actual design, alternatives considered and cross-cutting concerns. It says non-goals are not negated goals but things that could reasonably be goals and are explicitly chosen not to be. [1]
  • The article puts the sweet spot for a larger project at around 10 to 20 pages and says a 1 to 3 page mini design doc is possible for incremental improvements or sub-tasks. [1]
  • The lifecycle it describes is creation and rapid iteration, review, implementation and iteration, then maintenance and learning. Its rule of thumb is to update the doc if the designed system has not shipped yet. [1]
  • The book states that most teams at Google require an approved design document before starting work on any major project, and that the canonical templates require engineers to consider security implications, internationalisation, storage requirements and privacy concerns. [2]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

HashiCorp: a problem document approved before the solution document is written

HashiCorpDocumented in official docs

HashiCorp publishes its PRD template, its RFC template and an article on its writing practices in a public handbook called How HashiCorp Works. The article says the company has grown past 1,000 employees and that writing remains a foundation of how it works. [3][4][5]

The problem

Teams tend to argue about solutions before they agree what the problem is. HashiCorp's templates separate the two. The PRD template says it is designed to help team members fully understand a problem and define what is needed to address it, and that it is most helpful for an ambiguous challenge with many stakeholders. The writing practices article gives the wider reasons for writing: to clarify ideas, to include colleagues wherever they are based, and to preserve the history of how a decision was reached once the team has doubled in size and people are in new roles.

Where this topic fits

The PRD comes first and is about the problem. The article says a PRD is generally the first step toward an RFC, though not always. The RFC then proposes a solution and carries a header field that links back to the PRD. Stakeholders approve the PRD before kick-off and RFC writing, and the RFC is approved before implementation starts.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Collect user research: interview notes, workflow descriptions and key takeaways for each customer. The template calls this the most important section.
  2. Reduce the research to problem statements that each map back to the research and to a persona, which is a generalised version of the users who were interviewed.
  3. Group the requirements into phases. Each phase must deliver end-to-end value, and requirements inside a phase get no individual priority because all of them are required.
  4. Give each requirement acceptance criteria written like test cases, and a considerations entry phrased as a question for whoever writes the RFC.
  5. Write the introduction last, so that it summarises the finished document.
  6. Obtain approval from each representative stakeholder before kick-off and RFC writing.
  7. Write the RFC: a background section that a newcomer could follow, the proposal, the abandoned ideas with the reason each was dropped, and an outline of the implementation.
  8. Acknowledge and resolve every comment, then get explicit approval from the appropriate team leads before implementing.

Why this approach fits

Approving the problem first stops a favourite solution from writing the requirements. Acceptance criteria in the form of test cases give the RFC author a target and give reviewers something to hold the proposal against. Considerations written as questions pass on what the PRD author noticed without constraining the design. For a company spread across locations, a document also lets people comment in their own working hours.

Limitation or tradeoff

Two documents and two approval rounds take calendar time that a short engagement may not have. The article itself says that whether to write an RFC is a qualitative decision and that work which takes less time to do than to write up can go ahead without one. The RFC template includes an Obsolete status, but the pages we read do not describe a routine for revisiting approved documents, so nothing in the published process stops an approved RFC from drifting away from what was built. The pages describe intended practice and report no measurements.

What the sources document

  • HashiCorp expands PRD as Problem Requirements Document. Its template says the document is designed to help team members fully understand a problem and define what is needed to address it. [3]
  • The template calls User Research the most important section because it grounds the PRD in real, experienced user problems, and calls the Problem section the heart of the PRD. [3]
  • Acceptance criteria should be written like test cases for the requirement. Each requirement has a Considerations entry for the RFC writer that should almost always be a question, not a suggestion. [3]
  • Each of the representative stakeholders must approve the PRD before it proceeds to kick-off and RFC writing. [3]

Spotify: writing ADRs so that decisions outlive the meeting where they were made

The problem

Decisions at Spotify are usually reached in RFC discussions or in engineering meetings. The post is about what happens afterwards. A decision that was made, or a standard that formed by habit, is not clear to everyone if it was never documented, and least of all to new hires. The post asks whether a decision that was never recorded can be called a standard at all.

Where this topic fits

The ADR is written at the point where a decision becomes real: after an RFC concludes for a large change, straight away for a small one, and in retrospect for a decision that was taken without a record. It sits after the debate and before the next person needs to know the outcome.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. A decision is reached, usually through an RFC or at one of the team's engineering meetings.
  2. The engineers ask which of three cases applies: a decision exists but was never documented, a large change is being proposed, or a small change with little impact is being made.
  3. For an undocumented decision they backfill an ADR. The post notes that peer review is one place such decisions come to light, when a change introduces a competing code pattern or library.
  4. For a large change they write an RFC first to reach consensus, then write the ADR once the RFC has concluded with a solution.
  5. For a small decision they write the ADR directly and keep it lightweight.
  6. Other teams read the ADR and can adopt the decision. In the post's example, teams in the Stockholm office adopted a decision recorded by engineers based in New York.

Why this approach fits

An ADR is small enough to write in the same week as the decision, while the context is still known. It separates the outcome from the debate. An RFC thread can run to many comments, while the ADR states what was decided and what follows from it. That makes it usable by a new hire, or by a team in another office, who needs the answer and not the argument.

Limitation or tradeoff

The trigger is a judgement call. The post says an ADR should be written whenever a decision of significant impact is made and leaves each team to define significant, so two teams can keep very different logs. A log that records everything becomes too long to read, and one that records little will lack the decision someone later needs. The post reports the benefits as the author's experience and gives no counts or measurements.

What the sources document

  • The post defines an ADR as a document that captures a decision, including the context of how the decision was made and the consequences of adopting it. [6]
  • It names three benefits: onboarding, because future team members can read a history of decisions; ownership handover, because new owners of a system can learn how and why its architecture evolved; and alignment on best practices across Spotify. [6]
  • It describes three situations for writing an ADR: backfilling decisions that were made but never documented, proposing large changes, and proposing small or no changes. [6]
  • In 2019 the Creator web engineers decided to adopt React Hooks at one of their bi-weekly web engineering meetings, after running a small experiment in a small part of the application. They stopped creating new class components in favour of function components. [6]
  • Teams in the Stockholm office were able to read, reference and adopt the React Hooks ADR written by the New York-based Creator web engineers. The post says an ADR should be written whenever a decision of significant impact is made, and that each team aligns on what counts as significant. [6]

Squarespace: repairing design review with named approvers and a conditional yes

A 2019 post on the Squarespace Engineering blog by Tanya Reilly, a principal engineer there, describes how the Infrastructure organisation changed the way it reviews RFCs, its name for design documents. [7]

The problem

The organisation already wrote RFCs, and the reviews were not working. The post lists the symptoms. Designs did not always get deep review, and engineers complained of discovering systems that had been implemented with subtle flaws or were more complicated than they needed to be. Authors made decisions that were good locally and did not fit the broader architecture. Reviewers were not sure what they should be looking for. Authors felt that criticism was being piled on, could not tell whether reviewers liked the overall design, and did not know when the review period was finished and it was acceptable to start implementing.

Where this topic fits

The change is to the review step between writing a design document and starting implementation. Who writes an RFC did not change. What changed is the template the author fills in, the forum that reviews major RFCs, and the form the reviewers' answer takes.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The RFC template gained an approvers section in which approvers sign off on the design, and a free-text status field.
  2. The template asks for the alternatives considered and prior art, the systems the design depends on, and the operational work it adds along with who is expected to do that work.
  3. Major RFCs go to Architecture Review, a twice-weekly meeting of a small group of the most senior engineers. Each major RFC gets a one-hour session, and the aim is to have no more than ten people in the room.
  4. The group spends 50 minutes in deep discussion, taking detailed notes of objections, concerns and caveats, and uses the final 10 minutes to make the decision.
  5. Approvers answer yes or not yet. A yes may carry caveats, phrased as "yes, if" followed by the condition.
  6. Architecture Review is added to the RFC as an approver, with a link to the notes of the meeting. The notes are open to all of engineering.
  7. The fortnightly Infrastructure Council, where RFCs used to be presented and discussed, was kept and turned into a forum for building and maintaining community.

Why this approach fits

Each symptom had a missing piece of structure behind it. Not knowing when a review is finished is the absence of named approvers. A flood of comments on a first draft is the absence of a stated draft stage. Not knowing whether reviewers like the design is the absence of an explicit answer. The conditional yes does the most work. It turns an objection into a statement of what would make the design acceptable, so the author knows the next step and the reviewer is working with the author instead of guarding a gate.

Limitation or tradeoff

The process spends the scarcest people. A one-hour session with up to ten senior engineers costs up to ten hours of their time for one RFC, so it only makes sense for major decisions. A small senior group can also look closed to those outside it, which publishing the notes is meant to offset. The post reports that RFC authors found it a good experience and gives no figures for review time or for defects avoided.

[8][9][10][11][12]

The problem

Kubernetes is built by many groups, called special interest groups (SIGs), and a feature usually takes several releases to move from alpha to stable. There was no standard way to describe an important enhancement, and the project needed to track one from conception through implementation. Where this topic fits

A KEP is the tracking record, the requirements and the design in one file. It is written before implementation and edited for as long as the feature is in development. It is stored as Markdown with a small metadata file in a Git repository, so changes are proposed as pull requests that the KEP's owners review.

  1. The author picks a hosting SIG, opens an enhancement issue, copies the template directory, and fills in as much of the kep.yaml metadata as possible plus at least the Summary and Motivation sections.
  2. The first pull request is merged early, with the KEP in the provisional status. The template tells authors to merge early and iterate instead of getting hung up on details.
  3. The author adds the proposal, design details, test plan and graduation criteria. When the approvers named in the metadata agree, the status becomes implementable.
  4. For each release stage the author answers the production readiness questionnaire inside the KEP, which covers rollout and rollback, monitoring, dependencies, scalability and troubleshooting. A reviewer from a separate team must approve it by the enhancements freeze.

A feature that takes years to mature needs one place that stays true about it. Keeping status and milestones in a metadata file makes the state of every enhancement readable by tools as well as by people. Tying a release gate to the document, so that an enhancement without an approved readiness review is not part of the release, gives authors a reason to update it that does not depend on goodwill. The process is heavy, and its own design document says so. Gates do not prevent drift either. 4Common mistakes

  1. Writing a design doc that describes only the chosen implementation.

    Why it causes problems
    Reviewers cannot tell whether other options were weighed, so they either re-argue the design from scratch in the comments or approve something nobody examined. The first person to ask "why not X?" after launch gets no answer.
    How to recognise it
    The document has no alternatives section, or it has one whose entries are dismissed with a phrase such as "too complex" and no stated trade-off.
    How to fix it
    List at least two real alternatives, including the simplest option or doing nothing, and give the specific trade-off that made each one lose. If no alternative exists, the work may not need a design doc.

  2. Putting the solution in the PRD and calling it a requirement.

    Why it causes problems
    A requirement such as "use a vector database" fixes the design before anyone has reviewed it and cannot be traced to a user problem. The sponsor ends up approving a technology they have no way to evaluate.
    How to recognise it
    Requirements name a product, a model, a screen or a library. Take the name away and nothing is left about what a user can do or which number should change.
    How to fix it
    Rewrite each requirement as an observable behaviour with an acceptance check. Move the technology choice to the design doc and record it in an ADR.

  3. Sending a document for review without saying who approves or when comments close.

    Why it causes problems
    Comments arrive for weeks and nobody says yes, so the author either waits or starts building without agreement. Squarespace's engineers reported this symptom before they changed their process, with authors unsure when review was finished.
    How to recognise it
    The request reads "thoughts?" or "feedback welcome". The document has no approvers line, and its status has said draft for more than two weeks.
    How to fix it
    Add an approvers list and a comments-close date to the header, say what kind of feedback you want at this stage, and record each approver's answer in the document.

  4. Editing an accepted ADR so that it describes a later decision.

    Why it causes problems
    The reason for the first choice disappears, along with the evidence that the context changed. Anyone reading an old pull request or incident report finds an ADR that contradicts what the code did at the time.
    How to recognise it
    Version control shows the decision section changing after the status became accepted, or a project that has clearly changed direction has no superseded entries in its log.
    How to fix it
    Write a new ADR with the new context and decision. Set the old one's status to superseded with the number of its replacement, and leave the rest of the old text alone.

  5. Opening with background and leaving the request to the last page.

    Why it causes problems
    A sponsor or security reviewer who reads one paragraph learns the history of the project and not what they are being asked to approve. Approval is delayed, or it is given without understanding.
    How to recognise it
    Cover everything below the first paragraph. If a reader could not say what is proposed, what result is expected and what is needed from them, the summary is missing.
    How to fix it
    Write the summary last and place it first. It states what is proposed, the expected result as a number, the main risk, and the decision needed from each named reader.

  6. Treating approval as the end of the document's life.

    Why it causes problems
    The build diverges from the design within weeks. A later reader trusts the document, plans a change around a component that was never built, and loses days finding out.
    How to recognise it
    The document has no owner, no status and no last-reviewed date, or its date is older than the last major change to the code it describes.
    How to fix it
    Put an owner, a status and a review date in the header of every document. Keep it in the repository, update it in the pull request that changes the behaviour, and mark it obsolete with a pointer when it stops being true.

python
import re

ADRS = {
    "0001-use-postgres-for-case-data.md": "# ADR-0001: Use PostgreSQL for case data\nStatus: accepted\nDate: 2026-06-02\n",
    "0002-call-llm-api-directly.md": "# ADR-0002: Call the model provider's API directly\nStatus: superseded by ADR-0005\nDate: 2026-06-09\n",
    "0003-store-prompts-in-repo.md": "# ADR-0003: Store prompts in the repository\nStatus: approved\nDate: 2026-06-16\n",
    "0005-route-calls-through-gateway.md": "# ADR-0005: Route model calls through an LLM gateway\nStatus: proposed\nDate: 2026-08-25\n",
    "0006-nightly-sync-of-policy-docs.md": "# ADR-0006: Sync policy documents nightly\nStatus: superseded by ADR-0009\nDate: 2026-09-01\n",
}
PLAIN_STATUSES = {"proposed", "accepted", "rejected", "deprecated"}


def parse(filename, text):
    title = re.search(r"^# ADR-(\d{4}): (.+)$", text, flags=re.M)
    status = re.search(r"^Status: (.+)$", text, flags=re.M)
    decided = re.search(r"^Date: (\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2})$", text, flags=re.M)
    return {
        "file": filename,
        "number": int(title.group(1)),
        "title": title.group(2),
        "status": status.group(1).strip(),
        "date": decided.group(1),
    }


def check(records):
    problems = []
    by_number = {record["number"]: record for record in records}
    for expected in range(1, max(by_number) + 1):
        if expected not in by_number:
            problems.append(f"ADR-{expected:04d}: number missing from the sequence")
    for record in records:
        label = f"ADR-{record['number']:04d}"
        replaced = re.fullmatch(r"superseded by ADR-(\d{4})", record["status"])
        if replaced:
            target = by_number.get(int(replaced.group(1)))
            if target is None:
                problems.append(f"{label}: superseded by ADR-{replaced.group(1)}, which does not exist")
            elif target["status"] != "accepted":
                problems.append(f"{label}: superseded by ADR-{replaced.group(1)}, "
                                f"which is '{target['status']}', not accepted")
        elif record["status"] not in PLAIN_STATUSES:
            problems.append(f"{label}: unknown status '{record['status']}'")
    return problems


records = sorted((parse(name, text) for name, text in ADRS.items()), key=lambda r: r["number"])
print("| ADR | Decision | Status | Date |")
print("| --- | --- | --- | --- |")
for r in records:
    print(f"| {r['number']:04d} | {r['title']} | {r['status']} | {r['date']} |")
print()
print("In force:", ", ".join(f"ADR-{r['number']:04d}" for r in records if r["status"] == "accepted"))
problems = check(records)
print(f"{len(problems)} problems")
for line in problems:
    print(" -", line)

# ADR-0004: Answer guideline questions by retrieval over a nightly-synced index

Status: accepted on 2026-10-07 (scope: Mina; data access: Ravi)

## Context
The underwriting guidelines are 212 PDFs in SharePoint, about 1,900 pages.
Legal updates 5 to 10 documents every week and publishes before 18:00.
Compliance requires a citation on every answer. Staff may see only the
guidelines for lines of business they are cleared for, and SharePoint
groups already hold those permissions.

We considered three options.

1. Fine-tune a model on the guidelines. Rejected: it would need
   retraining every week to stay current, and its answers could not
   cite a source page.
2. Put whole documents in the prompt. Rejected: it works for one
   document (the largest is 140 pages), but a question that spans
   lines of business needs several documents and becomes slow and
   expensive.
3. Retrieve the relevant passages from an index and answer from them
   with citations.

On 60 test questions written by two underwriters, option 3 answered
51 correctly, 6 partially and 3 wrongly. All 3 wrong answers involved
tables split across pages.

## Decision
We will answer questions by retrieving passages from an index of the
guidelines and generating an answer that cites the source document and
page. We will rebuild the index every night after 18:00. We will filter
passages by the user's SharePoint groups before the model sees them.

## Consequences
- Every answer carries a citation, which meets the compliance requirement.
- A guideline published today cannot be found until the next morning.
- We must run and monitor a sync job and an index, which fine-tuning
  would have avoided.
- Tables split across pages are answered wrongly today (3 of 60 test
  questions). Fixing table extraction is open work.
- Access is only as correct as the SharePoint groups.
- Open: how a permission change made during the day takes effect
  before the nightly sync.

Summary for Mina. The assistant will look up the relevant guideline passages and answer from them, showing the document and page for every answer. On 60 questions written by two underwriters it answered 51 correctly (85%), 6 partially and 3 wrongly, and all 3 wrong answers involved tables that run across pages. It will not know about a guideline until the morning after Legal publishes it, it will only answer from guidelines the person asking is already cleared to read, and it should not yet be relied on for tables that span pages.

Non-goals.

  • Same-day availability of newly published guidelines. The index is rebuilt nightly.
  • Changing who may see which guideline. Permissions stay in SharePoint, and the assistant only applies them.

Approvers. Mina approves the scope, the non-goals and the accuracy the team will accept. Ravi approves data access and permission filtering. Lee approves the retrieval approach and who operates the nightly sync. Compliance is informed and confirms the citation format.

Notice what the ADR leaves out. It does not name a vector database, an embedding model or a chunk size, because the thread did not decide those, and each can get its own ADR when it is decided. The 85% figure is 51 divided by 60. The last consequence came from asking what the thread did not cover, and it is recorded as open instead of being answered with a guess.

Check your answer

  • Could a new team member explain from your ADR alone why fine-tuning was rejected?
  • Does your decision section start with "We will", and does it avoid naming tools the thread never chose?
  • Does your summary for Mina say what the assistant will not do, in words an underwriter would use?
  • Did you report the test result as 51 of 60, or 85%, with the 3 wrong answers and their cause?
  • Is at least one of your consequences something that makes the team's life harder?
  • Does each of Mina, Ravi and Lee have a specific part to approve?
