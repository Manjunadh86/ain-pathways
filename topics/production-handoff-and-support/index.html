Production Handoff and Support

Production handoff is the planned transfer of a running system from the team that built it to the team that will operate it, backed by evidence that it is ready, documents that let others run it and training that proves they can. Support is the work that follows: watching service health against agreed targets, responding to incidents and learning from them.

1Overview

Why it matters

A prototype that works while its builder is in the room has not yet been shown to work without them. When a forward deployed engineer rotates off, the customer's own team gets the pages at night, the questions from users and the bill. If the only operating manual is in one person's head, the customer has received a dependency rather than a product, and the first failure after the exit comes straight back to you.

AI systems add operating duties that ordinary services lack. A model version can be retired on a published date, after which requests to it fail, so someone must own that calendar (Anthropic's deprecations page (opens in a new tab) promises at least 60 days' notice for publicly released models). Quality can also fall while every uptime graph stays green: in its postmortem of three 2025 bugs (opens in a new tab), Anthropic says its own evaluations did not capture the degradation users reported. This lesson is for forward deployed engineers, solutions engineers and platform engineers who build something that another team must run.

What you will be able to do

  • Run a production readiness review as a list of evidence-backed checks, and turn the failures into a go or no-go decision with an owner for each gap.
  • Write a runbook entry that someone who did not build the system can follow, and validate it by having them run it.
  • Define SLIs and SLOs for an AI-backed service, then calculate its error budget and burn rate from request counts.
  • Set up incident response with severity levels, named roles and a blameless postmortem that ends in owned action items.
  • Plan a training path from walk-through to reverse shadowing that finishes with a drill the customer team passes without you.
  • Write an exit plan that lists who owns every account, key and recurring date, how long support lasts and what counts as new work.

Key terms

Production readiness review
A structured check, made before a service goes live or changes owner, that compares the system with a written checklist and records the evidence, the gaps and a decision.
Runbook
A documented, step-by-step procedure for one operational task or known failure, written so that someone other than its author can carry it out. A playbook is the investigative counterpart that leads to the right runbook.
Service level indicator (SLI)
A measurement of one aspect of service quality, usually written as the number of good events divided by the number of events that count toward it.
Service level objective (SLO)
A target for an SLI over a stated window, for example "99% of draft requests return a draft over 28 days".
Error budget
The share of events that may miss the SLO, equal to 100% minus the SLO. It is what the team can spend on failures and risky changes.
Burn rate
How fast the error budget is being used relative to the SLO. A burn rate of 1 uses exactly the whole budget by the end of the window; 10 uses it ten times too fast.
Incident commander
The person who coordinates an incident response and makes the decisions, while other people do the technical work.
Blameless postmortem
A written record of an incident, its causes and its follow-up actions that looks for weaknesses in the system rather than a person to blame.
Reverse shadowing
A training step in which the trainee leads the real work while an experienced person watches, ready to take over.
Exit plan
A written plan, started at the beginning of an engagement, that says what is handed over, to whom, by when and what support continues afterwards.

2Explanation

Introduction

When a builder hands over a house, the owner does not just receive keys. There is a list of small faults still to fix, a folder of manuals, a walk-through that shows where the stopcock and the fuse box are, and an agreement about whom to call during the first year. Software handed to a customer's team needs the same things, and they are easy to skip when the demo went well.

Production handoff is that transfer: a running system moves from the team that built it to the team that will operate it. Five pieces make it real. A production readiness review checks the system against a written list and records the gaps. Runbooks tell the next person what to do when something breaks. Service level objectives say in numbers what "healthy" means, so both sides can tell. An incident process and postmortems decide who acts when it fails and what the team learns. Training and an exit plan make sure the receiving team can do all of this without you, and that the engagement has an end.

Read the diagram from the exit plan outward. The plan is drafted in the first week, not the last, because it fixes what "ready" means and when you leave. The review is a gate that can send you back to fix gaps. Training moves the work across in stages, and you leave only after the customer's team has passed a drill without you.

Most of the practice described here was published by teams that run large services: Google's two Site Reliability Engineering books, the AWS Well-Architected Framework, PagerDuty's public incident documentation and GitLab's open runbooks. They were written for teams that own what they run. This lesson adapts them to the harder case, where the people who built the system are leaving, and it says "our adaptation" wherever the text goes beyond a source. It also marks the places where AI changes the work: a hosted model that is retired on a schedule, and quality that cannot be read from uptime. It builds on Deploying in Customer Environments and LLM and Agent Observability, and sits at the end of the engagement lifecycle.

How it works

1. Check readiness with a production readiness review

A production readiness review (PRR) is how Google's SRE teams decide whether to take over a service. The reviewers verify that it meets accepted standards of production setup and operational readiness and that its owners can work with SRE (Google SRE book, chapter 32 (opens in a new tab)). Its six stages are engagement, analysis against a checklist, improvements negotiated with the developers, training, onboarding and continuous improvement. AWS builds its Operational Readiness Review from incidents: it distils lessons from past incidents into curated questions and advises organisations to draw their own checklist from their own post-incident analysis (AWS Operational Readiness Reviews (opens in a new tab)).

In a customer handoff the roles flip: you are the developer and the customer's engineers are the reviewers. Turn each line into evidence they can see, not an assurance you give. "Rollback works" becomes "a customer engineer rolled back to the previous release in staging". Each failed line gets an owner and a date. The decision is one of three: go, go once named gaps close, or not yet.

2. Write runbooks that someone else can run

The AWS Well-Architected Framework defines a runbook (opens in a new tab) as a documented process for a specific outcome, at its simplest a checklist, and a playbook (opens in a new tab) as a step-by-step guide for investigating an incident. When a playbook finds the cause, it points to the runbook that fixes it. Both should name tools, permissions, error handling, escalation and owner, and a new one should be validated by someone else running it. GitLab's public runbooks repository (opens in a new tab) recommends entries with symptoms, pre-checks, resolution, post-checks and an optional rollback, and expects every alert to link its runbook; an alert that does not is to be reported as toil.

Our adaptation is one short runbook per alert:

markdown
# R-04 Provider errors, no drafts shown
Alert: drafts_availability_burn | Owner: IT on-call lead | Last run by someone else: 2026-10-02
## Symptoms
- Agents see "Draft unavailable"; 1-hour and 5-minute burn rates both above 13.44 (over 13% of drafts failing)
## Pre-checks
1. Provider status page: incident listed?
2. Error mix in logs: 429 rate limit, 5xx provider, 401 key rejected?
## Resolution
- 429: lower concurrency from 8 to 4 and redeploy the configuration
- 5xx: turn on the no-draft flag so agents reply by hand
- 401: rotate the key from the secrets vault
## Post-checks
- Error rate under 1% for 15 minutes; a test ticket receives a draft
## Escalate after 30 minutes
- Incident commander, provider support, then the FDE (during the support window)

3. Agree what healthy means: SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

Google's workbook writes an SLI as good events divided by total events and the error budget as 100% minus the SLO: with a 99.9% success target, a service handling 3 million requests in four weeks has a budget of 3,000 errors (Implementing SLOs (opens in a new tab)). The SRE book advises starting from what users need, keeping SLOs few, starting loose and not anchoring targets on current performance (Service Level Objectives (opens in a new tab)). An AI service usually needs three: availability (an answer came back), latency (fast enough) and quality (the answer was good). Quality needs a grader and a sample, so it is noisier.

Burn rate compares how fast errors arrive with how fast the SLO allows them:

burn rate=observed error rate1−SLO,budget share used=burn rate×hours elapsedwindow hours\text{burn rate} = \frac{\text{observed error rate}}{1 - \text{SLO}}, \qquad \text{budget share used} = \frac{\text{burn rate} \times \text{hours elapsed}}{\text{window hours}}burn rate=1−SLOobserved error rate​,budget share used=window hoursburn rate×hours elapsed​

Google's alerting chapter (opens in a new tab) pages on a fast burn seen in a long window and a short one together (for a 30-day window, 14.4 over one hour is 2% of the budget; 6 over six hours is 5%) and opens a ticket for a slow burn of 1 over three days. The multiplier is budget share times window hours divided by alert hours, so for a 28-day window (672 hours) 2% in one hour gives 13.44.

An error budget policy says what happens at the edge. Google's example policy (opens in a new tab) halts all releases except P0 and security fixes when the budget is spent over four weeks, and requires a postmortem when one incident uses over 20% of it. Treat it as an example: agree your own with the customer's product owner and record it.

4. Respond: roles, severity and a shared record

Google's incident framework separates incident command, operations (the only people changing the system), communication and planning, with a live incident document (Managing Incidents (opens in a new tab)). PagerDuty's public process adds that the incident commander (opens in a new tab) coordinates and does not resolve, and uses five severity levels (opens in a new tab) where unsure cases are treated as the higher level and argued about afterwards. Its author declares an incident when a second team is needed, the outage is customer-visible or the problem survives an hour of concentrated analysis.

A small customer team will have one or two people in every role. Keep the roles as hats anyway: they stop the person fixing the system from also choosing whom to tell. Write down three severity levels, who may declare an incident, who tells the users, and whom to call at the model provider, the cloud provider and your firm.

5. Learn: blameless postmortems

A postmortem is a written record of an incident: impact, actions taken, root causes and follow-up. Google's guidance is to keep it blameless and to agree triggers in advance (user-visible degradation, data loss, on-call intervention, monitoring that failed) (Postmortem Culture (opens in a new tab)). PagerDuty requires one for its two highest severities and schedules the review within three calendar days or five business days (Postmortem Process (opens in a new tab)). Every action needs an owner and a date, tracked elsewhere. For AI systems add two questions: what should have caught this earlier, and which check joins the readiness list.

6. Train the people who will run it

Documents are not the handoff; competence is. Google's PRR has a training phase led by the reviewers (design overviews, request-flow deep dives, the production setup, hands-on exercises), then onboarding while the developers stay available. The on-call training chapter (opens in a new tab) adds reading postmortems, simulated incidents, shadowing the on-call rotation and revising documentation as a learning task. PagerDuty trains commanders the same way: shadow a full week, then reverse shadow (opens in a new tab).

Our adaptation has four stages: you work while they watch, they watch live incidents, they lead while you back them up, and they pass a drill alone. The drill is your acceptance test: roll back a bad release, handle a provider outage, rotate a key and move a staging copy to a replacement model, each against a time the customer agrees. Write for four readers: a day-one tutorial, how-to guides (the runbooks), reference (settings, endpoints) and an explanation of why the design is what it is.

7. Plan the exit from the first week

PostHog's forward deployed engineering handbook asks for a deliverable approved by a named person on the customer side, explicit follow-on tasks and written answers to the engagement's questions, and treats recurring reactive questions as support (PostHog (opens in a new tab)). Turn that into an exit plan:

  • Ownership register. Every account, API key, repository, cloud resource, dashboard, evaluation set and prompt, with one named customer owner each.
  • Dates that expire. Model retirements, certificates and key rotations. Requests to a retired model fail, and Anthropic's page describes a usage export by API key and model for finding what still depends on one (model deprecations (opens in a new tab)).
  • Support window. Start and end dates, what it covers (questions about the delivered system, defects against agreed requirements) and what it does not (new features, repeated questions that signal missing training).
  • Exit criteria. The drill passed, runbooks run by customer engineers, the SLO dashboard owned by the customer, the register signed.

Worked example: Handing over a reply-drafting assistant: readiness check, SLOs and a page decision

This is a teaching scenario with invented numbers. It continues the tool-hire support desk from Customer Discovery and Requirements. After the lookup panel, the team built an assistant that drafts replies to order-status emails from the panel's data, using a hosted language model; agents edit and send. Your engagement ends in two weeks, and the customer's IT team of three engineers will run the assistant.

Two customer engineers ran the checklist and you supplied the evidence.

Five checks pass and three fail, so the decision is "go once the gaps close". Each gap gets an owner and a re-test date before the drill: you write the missing runbook and pin the version, and the IT manager creates a replacement key in the company's vault.

Step 2: choose three SLIs and SLOs over 28 days

Latency counts only drafts that were shown, because a missing draft is already an availability failure.

Step 3: compute from the 28-day counts

served = requested - failed
line("availability", served, requested, 0.99)
line("latency", served - slow, served, 0.95)
line("quality", good, sample, 0.90)

half = z * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / sample + z**2 / (4 * sample**2)) / (1 + z**2 / sample)
print(f"quality 95% interval {centre - half:.1%} to {centre + half:.1%}")

Step 4: read the numbers

  • On the point estimates all three SLOs are met. Availability has used 62.5% of its budget, latency 70.1%.
  • Quality is the weak number. It has used 16 of 20 allowed bad samples, and the 95% interval for 200 samples (87.4% to 95.0%) reaches below 90%. The data is compatible with meeting the SLO but does not prove it. Raise the weekly sample to 100, or report the SLO as at risk.
  • For a 28-day window the paging thresholds are 13.44 (2% of budget in one hour) and 5.6 (5% in six hours). The last hour burns at 15.0 and the last five minutes at 21.4. Both windows are above 13.44, so the alert pages the on-call engineer, and the page links to runbook R-04 from the runbook section above.
  • If 25 failures an hour continued, the remaining 420 events would last 16.8 hours, and 20% of the budget (224 events) would be gone after about 9 hours. Under a policy modelled on Google's example, passing that line makes a postmortem mandatory.

What the result means. "All SLOs met" is not the end of the handoff. It ends when the customer's team has closed the three gaps, has run R-04 itself in the drill and owns the error budget policy. Each gap is the kind of thing a builder stops noticing, which is why the customer ran the review.

Edge cases and limitations

The customer team is too small to carry a pager around the clock
Google's SRE book advises that engineers spend no more than 25% of their time on call, and says a 24/7 rotation with a primary and a secondary needs at least eight engineers at one site. A customer IT team of three cannot meet that. Agree the real coverage in the exit plan: business-hours support, a managed on-call service, or a degraded mode such as the no-draft flag so that nothing needs a night-time fix.
Low traffic makes burn-rate alerts jumpy
Burn-rate alerts assume enough events in the short window. A service that handles five requests an hour shows a 20% error rate after a single failure, a burn rate of 20 against a 99% SLO, even on a good day. For low-volume services use longer windows, alert on counts of failed tickets, or run a synthetic request every few minutes so the denominator is steady.
A quality SLI measured on a small sample
In the worked example 184 good drafts out of 200 gives 92%, but the 95% interval runs from 87.4% to 95.0%, so a 90% SLO is neither proven met nor proven missed. Report the interval, grow the sample or lengthen the window before the number is allowed to freeze releases. If a model does the grading, compare its grades with a person's on a sample from time to time.
The provider keeps changing after you leave
Anthropic's page says older models are retired regularly, requests to a retired model fail, and deprecated models are likely to be less reliable than active ones. Its dates apply to Anthropic-operated platforms, while Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud set their own schedules, so check the platform you call. It also lists a parameter change - temperature, top_p and top_k return a 400 error when set to non-default values on Claude 4.7 and later models. Treat a model swap as a tested change with its own runbook.
The customer environment limits what you can see after the exit
If the system runs in an air-gapped or locked-down environment you cannot log in to look at it, so the support window is only as good as the diagnostics the customer can send. Agree a support bundle with versions, configuration with secrets removed, recent logs and an SLO dashboard export, and test it during the drill. See [Deploying in Customer Environments](/topics/deploying-in-customer-environments/).

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • A team other than the builders will run the system, so the builders' memory leaves with them.
  • A failure costs the customer money, safety or trust, or is visible to their own users.
  • The system depends on a component the vendor controls, such as a hosted model that is retired on a published schedule.
  • The engagement has a fixed end date and someone must be named as owner afterwards.

Think twice when

  • The work is a throwaway prototype or demo with no users; a one-page note with an owner and a delete date is enough.
  • The work is a one-off analysis run by its author, where nothing stays in production.
  • No customer team can or will own the system. A handoff pack does not replace finding an owner or choosing a managed service.

A simpler alternative. A one-page handoff note: a named owner, how to deploy and roll back, runbooks for the three most likely failures, one availability SLO, whom to call and a 30-day support window. Move to a full readiness review when an outage would cost real money, when more than one team is involved or when the system pages people outside office hours.

Key takeaways

  • A handoff succeeds when the customer's team can run the system without you, so evidence such as a tested rollback and a passed drill counts for more than documents.
  • A production readiness review turns "is it ready?" into checks with evidence, a list of gaps with owners and a go or no-go decision; AI systems add checks for fallbacks, pinned model versions and quality monitoring.
  • An SLI is good events divided by total events, the error budget is one minus the SLO, and burn rate shows how fast that budget is being spent, which is what decides whether to page.
  • Runbooks, incident roles and postmortems are written before they are needed, tested by someone other than the author, and end in action items with owners and dates.
  • Quality can fall while uptime stays green, so an AI service needs a graded-sample SLI and a route for user feedback as well as availability and latency targets.
  • Train in stages from walk-through to reverse shadow to a solo drill, and write the exit plan in the first week with an ownership register, a support window and the dates that will expire.

Example 1 of 5

Google SRE takes over a service through a readiness review, training and a shared pager

GoogleDocumented in a case study

Chapter 32 of Google's Site Reliability Engineering book describes the Production Readiness Review that SRE teams run before they take responsibility for a service, and chapter 28 describes how new SREs are prepared for on-call. Both are written by the people who run the practice. [1][2]

The problem

A development team asks SRE to run its service in production. SRE has a limited number of engineers, and a service that is not ready becomes a steady source of pages. Google needs a way to decide whether to accept a service, to find and fix its weaknesses first, and to move responsibility to the new owners without leaving them on their own.

Where this topic fits

The PRR is the readiness review of this lesson. Its training and onboarding stages are the training of the receiving team, and the period in which developers stay on the rotation plays the part of a support window. The same sequence can be used with a customer's engineers as the reviewers.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. SRE leadership decides which SRE team is a good fit for the service, and usually one to three SREs are chosen or volunteer to carry out the review.
  2. In the analysis stage the reviewers learn the service and check it for production shortcomings, using a checklist the SRE team maintains for this stage.
  3. The reviewers rank the improvements by their importance for reliability, negotiate them with the development team and agree a plan of execution.
  4. The reviewers who led the PRR train the receiving team with design overviews, deep dives on request flows, a description of the production setup and hands-on exercises.
  5. During onboarding the developers stay available to back up and advise the SRE team as it settles in. In the book's Shakespeare example SRE took over the pager once the issues filed during the review were fixed, and two developers stayed in the on-call rotation.
  6. New SREs reach the pager through on-call learning checklists, reading postmortems, simulated incidents and shadowing the rotation before they take a primary shift.
  7. In continuous improvement the SRE team shares what it learns from operating the service with the developers as suggestions and proposals.

Why this approach fits

Handoffs often fail because the receiving team discovers the gaps by being paged. A review before takeover turns unknowns into a list that both sides have agreed, and the training stage turns documents into skill. Keeping the developers reachable during the first weeks puts expert help where the risk is highest.

Limitation or tradeoff

Google can choose which services to accept; the chapter says SRE weighs the importance of the service and the availability of SRE teams. A customer cannot decline the system it paid for, and an FDE has an end date instead of an open-ended developer team. The simple PRR also happens after a service is built, which is why the chapter goes on to describe early engagement during design and build. A checklist turns into a formality if reviewers accept assurances instead of seeing evidence.

What the sources document

  • A PRR aims to verify that a service meets accepted standards of production setup and operational readiness, and that its owners are prepared to work with SRE. [1]
  • The review has six stages - engagement, analysis, improvements and refactoring, training, onboarding and continuous improvement - and usually one to three SREs are selected or volunteer to conduct it. [1]
  • In the training stage the SRE reviewers who led the PRR take ownership of training the team, covering design overviews, deep dives on request flows, the production setup and hands-on exercises. [1]
  • During onboarding the development team must be available to back up and advise the SRE team for a period. In the Shakespeare example SRE took over the pager after the filed issues were fixed, and two developers were also in the on-call rotation. [1]
  • The on-call training chapter describes on-call learning checklists, postmortem reading, simulated disasters (the Wheel of Misfortune) and shadowing the on-call rotation, with reverse shadowing as an optional final step, as ways to prepare new SREs. [2]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

Evernote agrees an uptime SLO between operations and development and reviews it every month

Evernote (case study in Google's SRE Workbook)Documented in a case study

Chapter 3 of Google's Site Reliability Engineering Workbook contains Evernote's own account of adopting SLOs. The chapter pairs it with a second case study from The Home Depot. [10]

The problem

Evernote's operations team protected production while its developers pushed features. The case study says the developers felt constrained by lengthy operational requirements, and operations was frustrated when new code introduced new issues. The two teams needed one objective measure of service quality that both accepted, so that release and reliability decisions stopped being arguments about opinion.

Where this topic fits

The SLO is the shared definition of "healthy" at the boundary between the team that changes a system and the team that keeps it running. A customer handoff creates the same boundary, so an agreed SLO and a regular review are what keep it from turning into blame.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start with one SLO that covers most, but not all, user interactions as a proxy for quality, instead of waiting for a perfect set of indicators.
  2. Define it exactly - 99.95% uptime measured over a monthly window for certain services and methods - and write down the measurement method, including how maintenance windows are treated.
  3. Measure from outside with a third-party prober that polls every minute from several locations in North America and Europe, and count a node as down only when two geographically separate probers fail.
  4. Review the result each month. Teams from both Evernote and Google look at SLO performance and set improvement actions when the target was missed.
  5. Use the number in decisions. The authors report using SLO calculations to reduce the number of release windows from five to two.
  6. Review the SLO itself on a six-month cycle. After nine months it was on its third version, with plans to move beyond simple uptime to API-level and client-side measures.

Why this approach fits

One number gives developers and operations something to argue from. It replaces opinions about whether releases are too risky or controls too heavy, and it lets the teams review misses on a schedule. When a customer's team inherits a system, the same arrangement lets it decide when to slow changes down without asking the builders.

Limitation or tradeoff

A probe-based uptime SLO measures only whether the endpoint answers. 4Common mistakes

  1. Handing over documents instead of showing that the customer's team can operate the system

    Why it causes problems
    A folder of pages proves nothing about whether anyone can use them under pressure. The first real incident after the exit reaches the engineer who left, and the customer concludes that the system depends on that person.
    How to recognise it
    No customer engineer has run a rollback, a runbook or an alert response with the builder out of the room, and the acceptance criteria are written as deliverables ("runbook delivered") instead of abilities.
    How to fix it
    Add a drill to the exit criteria. The customer's engineers roll back a release, handle a provider outage, rotate a key and swap a model in staging against agreed times while you watch without helping. Repeat until it passes.

  2. Writing runbooks from memory and never testing them with someone else

    Why it causes problems
    The author's knowledge fills the gaps in the steps, so the runbook works only for its author. AWS lists letting runbooks drift out of sync with the system as a common anti-pattern.
    How to recognise it
    Steps say "restart the usual service" or "check the dashboard" with no names or links, nobody except the author has run the runbook, and its last-tested date is missing or older than the last release.
    How to fix it
    Have a customer engineer run each runbook in staging while you stay silent, fix every place they get stuck, record who ran it and when, and re-test after significant changes.

  3. Copying SLO numbers from a template or from current performance

    Why it causes problems
    A target set at today's performance leaves no room for change, and one copied from a larger company's service is a promise nobody measured. The budget is then always empty or never touched, and nobody trusts it.
    How to recognise it
    The SLO has no named approver or rationale, the budget sits at zero or at full every month, and the team cannot say what happens when the budget runs out.
    How to fix it
    Choose indicators from what users notice, start with a loose target, keep SLOs few and agree a short error budget policy with the customer's product owner. Review the target after a quarter or two.

  4. Monitoring only uptime for an AI feature

    Why it causes problems
    Model output can get worse while every request returns a normal response. Anthropic's postmortem says its evaluations missed degradation that users reported, so uptime and error-rate dashboards give false comfort.
    How to recognise it
    The dashboard shows availability and latency only, complaints about answer quality reach the team before any alert fires, and there is no graded sample or link between user reports and recent changes.
    How to fix it
    Add a quality SLI from a weekly graded sample, run the evaluation set before every prompt or model change, keep a feedback route for users, and log the prompt and model version with each response so reports can be tied to changes.

  5. Leaving accounts, keys and cloud resources in the builder's name

    Why it causes problems
    When the engagement ends and the builder's access is removed, the system stops working. Or it keeps working on an account that nobody at the customer controls, with a bill nobody expects.
    How to recognise it
    A key, billing account or repository belongs to a personal or vendor account, the customer cannot rotate a credential without asking you, or the ownership register is missing or has blank owners.
    How to fix it
    Create every account and key inside the customer's organisation in the first week, give yourself delegated access, and list each item with a named customer owner. Remove your access as the last exit step and check that the system still works.

  6. Leaving support open-ended

    Why it causes problems
    Without an end date and a scope, every question becomes the engineer's problem, the next engagement is starved of time and the customer never builds the habit of using its runbooks.
    How to recognise it
    The same kinds of question return week after week, nobody can say when support ends, and fixes arrive through direct messages instead of tickets.
    How to fix it
    Agree a support window with start and end dates, a channel and a list of what counts as support. Turn each repeated question into a runbook or training item, and reframe recurring reactive questions as support work.

Find when the burn-rate alerts would have paged from an hourly request log

CodingIntermediateAbout 40 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

A document-summary service for a legal team has a 99.5% availability SLO over 28 days (672 hours). It handles about 420,000 requests in that window, so the error budget is 2,100 failed requests. Below are one day's requests and failures per hour, with hour 0 starting at midnight.

python
requests = [310, 280, 260, 250, 270, 330, 450, 600, 780, 900, 950, 980,
            920, 940, 960, 930, 880, 800, 700, 620, 540, 460, 400, 350]
failures = [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 25, 29, 30, 31,
            29, 30, 115, 130, 85, 20, 5, 4, 3, 2, 2, 1]

Write a Python script that works out when a fast-burn alert and a slow-burn alert would first have paged, and how much of the budget the incident used. Then write the Symptoms and Escalate parts of a runbook for the alert.

Requirements

  • Compute each hour's burn rate as the hour's error rate divided by the allowed error rate (1 minus the SLO).
  • Compute a rolling 6-hour burn rate over the current and previous five hours (fewer at the start of the day). A production slow-burn alert would also need a short window such as 30 minutes to confirm it; hourly data cannot show that, so use the 6-hour window alone.
  • Derive the two thresholds from the formula instead of typing them in - the fast alert is 2% of the budget in 1 hour, the slow alert is 5% of the budget in 6 hours.
  • Report the first hour at which each alert would page, and the share of the 28-day budget used by hours 8 to 18 taken together as one incident.
  • Write a runbook fragment with the symptoms and the escalation conditions, using numbers from your results.

Expected behaviour

The script prints a fast threshold of 13.44 and a slow threshold of 5.60. The slow-burn alert pages first, at hour 12, because the error rate sits near 3% for several hours; the fast-burn alert pages at hour 14, when the rate jumps. Hours 8 to 18 use 529 failures, 25.2% of the budget, which is over a 20% single-incident line such as the one in Google's example policy.

Hint 1

The threshold for an alert is budget share times window hours divided by alert hours. Check that 2% in 1 hour gives 13.44 before using it.

Hint 2

For the rolling window, add up the failures and the requests over the window first, then divide. Averaging hourly rates gives a different answer when traffic changes.

Hint 3

Slice the lists with failures[start:hour + 1] and make sure start never goes below 0.

Show solution

One way to do it:

python
SLO = 0.995
WINDOW_HOURS = 28 * 24
BUDGET = (1 - SLO) * 420_000  # 2,100 failed requests allowed in 28 days

requests = [310, 280, 260, 250, 270, 330, 450, 600, 780, 900, 950, 980,
            920, 940, 960, 930, 880, 800, 700, 620, 540, 460, 400, 350]
failures = [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 25, 29, 30, 31,
            29, 30, 115, 130, 85, 20, 5, 4, 3, 2, 2, 1]

FAST = 0.02 * WINDOW_HOURS / 1  # 13.44: 2% of the budget in 1 hour
SLOW = 0.05 * WINDOW_HOURS / 6  # 5.60: 5% of the budget in 6 hours

def burn(bad, total):
    return (bad / total) / (1 - SLO)

first_fast = first_slow = None
for hour in range(24):
    one = burn(failures[hour], requests[hour])
    start = max(0, hour - 5)
    six = burn(sum(failures[start:hour + 1]), sum(requests[start:hour + 1]))
    if first_fast is None and one >= FAST:
        first_fast = hour
    if first_slow is None and six >= SLOW:
        first_slow = hour
    if 10 <= hour <= 15:
        print(f"hour {hour}: 1h burn {one:5.1f}   6h burn {six:5.1f}")

print(f"thresholds: fast {FAST:.2f}, slow {SLOW:.2f}")
print(f"first slow-burn page: hour {first_slow}; first fast-burn page: hour {first_fast}")
day = sum(failures)
print(f"day total: {day} failures = {day / BUDGET:.1%} of the 28-day budget ({BUDGET:.0f})")
incident = sum(failures[8:19])
print(f"hours 8-18 as one incident: {incident} failures = {incident / BUDGET:.1%} of the budget "
      f"({'over' if incident > 0.2 * BUDGET else 'under'} the 20% postmortem line)")

hour 11: 1h burn   6.3   6h burn   5.2
