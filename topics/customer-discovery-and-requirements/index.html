Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering

Customer discovery is the work of finding out what problem a customer actually has, by interviewing the people who do the work, watching them do it and mapping the data and systems involved. Requirements gathering turns what you learned into statements that can each be checked with a test.

1Overview

Why it matters

A forward deployed engineer rarely receives a problem. They receive a solution someone has already chosen: "we need a chatbot", "build us a dashboard", "integrate these two systems". If you build exactly that, you can deliver what was asked and still leave every number the customer cares about unchanged, because the request was a guess made by someone who knows their work but not what you can build.

Discovery decides three things that are expensive to get wrong later: whether the first thing you build touches the real bottleneck, whether a missing data source or an access approval is found in the first week instead of the sixth, and whether "done" is something both sides can check. This matters most for AI features, where a requirement such as "accurate answers" has to become a dataset, a metric and a threshold before anyone can say the system works. Anyone who builds software for another team's workflow needs this skill: forward deployed engineers, solutions engineers, and product engineers working from customer requests.

Before you start

  • The FDE Engagement LifecycleBeginner

What you will be able to do

  • Plan and run a discovery interview that asks about specific past events instead of opinions or predictions.
  • Observe a workflow and record its steps, tools and timings well enough to show where the time goes.
  • Rewrite a stated request as a problem statement that names the user, the goal, the barrier and the cause.
  • Build a data and systems inventory that lists the owner, access path, join key and known gaps of every source.
  • Write requirements in EARS form with a measurable threshold and a stated verification method.
  • Spot untestable wording and solution-anchored requirements, and correct them.

Key terms

Stated request
What the customer asks for in their own words, usually phrased as a solution such as a chatbot, a dashboard or a new field.
Underlying problem
The situation in the customer's work that is slow, error-prone or impossible and that made them ask in the first place.
Contextual inquiry
A research method in which you watch a person do their real work in their real setting and ask questions as they go.
Problem statement
A short description of who has the problem, what they are trying to do, what stops them and why, written without naming a solution.
Data and systems inventory
A table of every source a workflow touches, recording what it holds, who owns it, how to reach it and how it joins to the others.
Functional requirement
A statement of a capability the solution must provide, describing what it does rather than how it is built.
Testable requirement
A requirement precise enough that a test, demonstration, inspection or analysis can show whether it has been met.
EARS
The Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax, a small set of sentence patterns that fix the order of trigger, system and response in a requirement.
Acceptance criterion
A concrete, checkable condition that must hold for a requirement to count as delivered, often written as a Given, When, Then scenario.
Baseline
A measurement of how the work performs today, taken before anything is built, so that any later improvement can be shown rather than claimed.

2Explanation

Introduction

Most people have asked for something in a shop and been asked a question back. You ask a pharmacist for a strong painkiller; they ask where it hurts and for how long. The request was your guess at a solution. The question is how they find out what the problem is, and sometimes the answer is a different product, or a doctor.

Customers of software teams make the same kind of guess. A support lead asks for "an AI chatbot". A finance manager asks for "a dashboard". These are stated requests: solutions proposed by someone who knows their own work well but has not seen what you can build. Customer discovery is the work of getting from the stated request to the underlying problem, the thing in the customer's working day that is slow, error-prone or impossible, and to evidence of how big it is.

A small documented case shows the gap. A customer once asked the Basecamp team for more complex permission rules, a change the team judged could take six weeks. When they dug into what had happened, they learned that someone had archived a file without realising it would vanish for everyone else. The fix the team arrived at was a warning on the archive action, which the book calls a one-day change instead of a six-week project (Shape Up, chapter 3 (opens in a new tab)).

In a forward deployed engagement, discovery is the first phase of the engagement lifecycle. It has four activities, which this lesson covers in order:

  1. Interview the people who do the work, asking about specific past events.
  2. Observe the workflow, because people leave out the steps they no longer notice.
  3. Map the data and systems the workflow touches: where each record lives, who owns it and how you would get access.
  4. Write testable requirements: statements precise enough that you and the customer can agree, by running a check, whether each one is met.

The arrows that point backwards matter. A system map can reveal that the data a solution needs does not exist, and reading requirements back to the customer often shows that you misunderstood the problem. The output is not a long document. It is a short problem statement, an inventory of sources and a numbered list of requirements, each with a way to verify it.

How it works

Discovery is a loop with seven moves. None needs special tooling. Each needs a way of recording what you learn, so that a requirement can later be traced back to something a person said or did.

1. Set a goal and turn the solution back into a problem

Decide what the discovery has to let you decide: build or not, and what to build first. The UK government's service manual, which describes a discovery phase that comes before any building, tells teams that when they are handed a pre-defined solution they should question it and reframe it as a problem, and that reframing includes agreeing what is not part of the problem (GOV.UK, How the discovery phase works (opens in a new tab)). Its illustration: building an interactive map of contact centres is a solution; the problem is closer to how people can find their nearest contact centre when they need to book a face-to-face appointment. The same guidance says a government discovery typically runs around 4 to 8 weeks and that teams should not start building during it. A forward deployed engagement usually has far less time, so the same questions are compressed into the first days on site.

2. Interview about what happened

Talk to the people who do the work, not only the sponsor who signed the contract. An interview produces reported behaviour, and reports are distorted in known ways: people forget details, leave out what they assume is irrelevant, and shift answers towards what sounds acceptable (NN/g, User Interviews 101 (opens in a new tab)). Jakob Nielsen's version of the rule is to watch what people do, distrust what they say they do, and distrust most of all what they predict they would do (NN/g (opens in a new tab)). The questions that work are about one specific past event:

Weak questionWhy it failsStronger question
Would you use an AI assistant for this?Asks for a predictionTell me about the last time you handled one of these. What did you do first?
What features do you want?Asks the user to designWhere did the last one take longest? What did you do then?
Is the current tool slow?Leading; invites agreementHow long did the last one take? How do you know?
Why do you want a calendar?Invites a rationalisationWhat were you doing when you thought of asking for it?

Write down what was said in the person's own words and keep your interpretation in a separate column. "Agent finds the ERP search frustrating" is an interpretation. "Typed the customer name three ways before the order appeared" is an observation.

3. Observe the work

Contextual inquiry is the method of watching someone do their real work in their real setting and asking questions as they go. A session has four parts: a primer to build rapport, an explicit transition into "you work, I watch and interrupt", the contextual interview itself, and a wrap-up in which you play back your understanding for correction (NN/g, Contextual Inquiry (opens in a new tab)). The author of that guide gives an example from redesigning an insurance data-entry tool. In interviews, specialists described copying vehicle data from a spreadsheet and pasting it in. When the author then observed them at work, they were also cross-referencing a second tool for missing fields and pressing Save after every entry although the program saved automatically. Manufacturing has the same rule under the name genchi genbutsu: go and see the place where the problem exists (Toyota UK (opens in a new tab)).

For each step you watch, record what triggered it, which system or document was used, what was typed or copied, how long it took, and any workaround such as a sticky note or a private spreadsheet. Time the steps. Timings turn "lookups are annoying" into "lookups are two thirds of handling time", and that is what lets you choose what to build first.

4. Separate the request from the problem

Treat every request as evidence that something went wrong somewhere, and go looking for that event. Then write a problem statement that contains no solution. A template used at Intuit has five lines: I am (a narrow description of the person), I am trying to (the outcome), But (the barrier), Because (the root cause) and Which makes me feel (the emotion) (Intuit (opens in a new tab)). The Because line is the one discovery has to earn. If you cannot fill it from something you saw, you are not finished.

A usable problem statement names one kind of user, can be measured (how often, how long, what it costs), contains no feature, and is recognised as true by the customer when you read it back.

5. Map the data and systems

List every system and document the workflow touched, then draw them: the people, the systems and the arrows where information moves, including the arrows that are a person retyping something. This is the idea behind a system context diagram, which shows one system surrounded by its users and the other systems it interacts with (C4 model (opens in a new tab)). For each source, record in a data and systems inventory:

  • what it holds and at what grain (one row per order, per ticket, per event)
  • who owns it and who can approve access
  • how you would reach it: an API, a database view, a file export, or only a web login
  • the key that joins it to other sources, and how often that key is missing
  • how the data was collected and what has changed in the last couple of years
  • legal or contractual limits on moving or processing it

Several of these questions come from the checklists Microsoft's ISE team publishes for customer engagements (data exploration (opens in a new tab)). Work backwards from the outcome: start from the decision the work is meant to support, list the inputs that decision needs, and only then look for the data. Palantir's Foundry guidance gives the same order, outcome first and data second (Palantir (opens in a new tab)). Sort what you find into hard constraints you cannot change, such as legislation, and soft constraints such as an existing process, which the GOV.UK guidance says should be changed rather than worked around.

6. Write requirements that can be tested

A requirement states what is needed, not how to provide it. NASA's requirements checklist fixes the vocabulary: shall marks a requirement, should a goal and will a statement of fact. It also suggests asking "why do you need the requirement?", because the answer may point to the real one (NASA, Appendix C (opens in a new tab)). The same checklist rules out words nobody can verify, among them fast, easy, user-friendly, robust, adequate and sufficient, and asks whether each requirement can be shown to be met by test, demonstration, inspection or analysis.

A light way to get that precision is EARS, the Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax, which fixes the order of the clauses (EARS (opens in a new tab)):

text
Ubiquitous:         The <system name> shall <system response>
State-driven:       While <precondition(s)>, the <system name> shall <system response>
Event-driven:       When <trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>
Optional feature:   Where <feature is included>, the <system name> shall <system response>
Unwanted behaviour: If <trigger>, then the <system name> shall <system response>

To make a statement testable, add three things: a threshold with its tolerance ("within 5 seconds in at least 95 of 100 cases"), the verification method, and a link back to the observation that justifies it. An acceptance test in Given, When, Then form then spells out one concrete case. Its Then step should check something observable from outside the system, such as a message or a screen, rather than internal state (Gherkin reference (opens in a new tab)).

AI features need the same discipline. "The assistant shall give accurate answers" cannot be tested. "For the 200-ticket labelled set, the classifier shall assign the correct category to at least 180 tickets" can, and it becomes the first case in an evaluation suite (see Evaluating LLM Applications). Pair every such threshold with an unwanted-behaviour requirement that says what happens when the model is wrong or unsure.

7. Play it back

Read the problem statement, the map and the requirements to the customer and ask what is wrong. Record assumptions and decisions in a one-page summary that both sides keep. A legitimate result of discovery is that nothing should be built, because the fix is a process change or the data does not exist. The GOV.UK guidance says plainly that stopping after discovery is not a failure. What you do build first is the subject of Technical Scoping and Sequencing.

Worked example: Tool-hire support desk: from a chatbot request to five testable requirements

This is a teaching scenario with invented data, built so that you can reproduce every number.

Stated request. The support lead at a tool-hire company asks: "We need an AI chatbot that answers customer emails." The team uses a helpdesk, an ERP that holds orders, and the web portals of three carriers.

Step 1: interview about past events

Three agents each talk through the last ticket they closed. Two describe an order-status email, and both mention "finding the order" before they mention writing anything. One says the hard ones are emails with no order number. Nobody mentions the carrier portal.

Step 2: observe and time the work

You sit with two agents for 12 consecutive tickets and time each step: reading the ticket, finding the order in the ERP, checking the carrier portal, writing the reply, logging and closing. The log, in seconds, is embedded in the script.

python
import csv, io, statistics

STEPS = ["read", "erp", "carrier", "write", "close"]

rows = list(csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(LOG)))
for row in rows:
    row.update({step: int(row[step]) for step in STEPS})
    row["total"] = sum(row[step] for step in STEPS)
grand_total = sum(row["total"] for row in rows)

def share(subset, steps):
    part = sum(row[step] for row in subset for step in steps)
    return part / sum(row["total"] for row in subset)

print(f"all tickets: n={len(rows)} seconds={grand_total} "
      f"write={share(rows, ['write']):.1%} lookup={share(rows, ['erp', 'carrier']):.1%}")
for category in ["order_status", "invoice", "damage_claim", "other"]:
    group = [row for row in rows if row["category"] == category]
    seconds = sum(row["total"] for row in group)

          f"time={seconds / grand_total:.1%} write={share(group, ['write']):.1%} "
          f"lookup={share(group, ['erp', 'carrier']):.1%}")

status = [row for row in rows if row["category"] == "order_status"]
print("order_status median handle time:", statistics.median(row["total"] for row in status))
for flag in ["yes", "no"]:

Order-status tickets are half the volume, and in them writing is 17.3% of the time while looking things up in two systems is 66.3%. Writing dominates only in damage claims, where the reply needs a judgement about liability.

What the result means. The request was a chatbot that writes replies. The first slice is a read-only lookup panel, because that is where the time goes for half the tickets. Drafting replies may come second, and it now has a baseline to beat. Twelve tickets is a pilot sample: before committing, check the category mix against a 90-day helpdesk export.

Edge cases and limitations

The sponsor is not the user and stands between you and the users
Managers often answer for their teams, and their account is second-hand. Ask for two hours of shadowing rather than a meeting, which is easier to grant. If access is refused, use system records as a proxy (ticket exports, audit logs, timestamps) and write the risk into the summary, naming the assumption you could not check.
A small observed sample that looks decisive
Twelve tickets from two agents on one day can miss a weekly or seasonal pattern, and people work more carefully when watched. Treat timings as a pilot. Cross-check the category mix and volumes against several weeks of system data before the numbers go into a business case.
Work you cannot watch on demand
Month-end close, incident response and rare exceptions do not happen while you are in the room, and some people, such as clinicians, cannot be interrupted. Use silent observation with questions afterwards, or ask the person to replay the last three real cases using the actual records, screen by screen.
Requirements for model output that cannot be met every time
A language model will not classify or extract correctly in every case, so a plain shall statement about its output will fail on day one. Write the requirement as a threshold over a fixed, labelled data set, and add a separate requirement for what the system does when the model is wrong or has low confidence.
The real problem is not a software problem
Discovery sometimes shows that the cause is a policy, a missing approval or a step nobody owns. Building around it hides the cause. Say so, show the evidence, and propose the process change even if it shrinks or ends the engagement.
Two stakeholders want requirements that cannot both hold
Compliance may require a person to approve every outgoing message while operations wants replies sent automatically. Do not average them into vague wording. Write both down, show the conflict, and record who decided and when, so the requirement that survives has an owner.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The request arrives as a solution (a chatbot, a dashboard, an integration) and nobody can say what number should change if it works.
  • The people paying for the work are not the people who will use it, so the request has passed through at least one retelling.
  • The solution depends on data in systems you have not seen, where access, quality or join keys could block the build.
  • Someone other than you will decide whether the work is accepted, so both sides need checks agreed in advance.

Think twice when

  • The problem and its cause are already evidenced, such as a reproducible defect with a known fix. Fix it and measure.
  • The change is cheap and reversible, so a one-day prototype shown to a user will answer the question faster than interviews.
  • Discovery is being used to postpone a decision that the existing evidence already supports.

A simpler alternative. One 30-minute call with one person who does the work, asking them to talk through the last time the problem happened, followed by a one-page note with the problem statement, the sources involved and three acceptance checks. This stops being enough when more than one team or system is involved, or when being wrong would cost more than a few days of work.

Key takeaways

  • A customer's request is usually a proposed solution. Discovery finds the event behind it and the problem that event reveals, before anything is built.
  • Ask about one specific past event and then watch the work, because people forget steps, leave out workarounds and cannot reliably predict what they would use.
  • Time the steps and split them by case type, since an average across every kind of case can point at the wrong bottleneck.
  • Map every source the workflow touches in the first days, with its owner, access path and join key, so that missing data or approvals surface before work is promised.
  • Write each requirement as a shall statement with a threshold, a verification method and a link to the observation behind it. Words such as fast or accurate cannot be tested.
  • Read the problem statement, the map and the requirements back to the customer, and accept that the right result may be a smaller change or no build at all.

Example 1 of 5

Basecamp narrows a permissions request and a calendar request to the real problem

37signals (Basecamp)Documented in a case study

Shape Up, written by Ryan Singer and published by Basecamp, describes how the Basecamp product team works. Chapter 3, Set Boundaries, covers what the team does with an incoming feature request before any build time is committed. [3]

The problem

Customers ask for features in solution terms: "more complex permission rules", "a calendar". Taken at face value, each is weeks or months of work. The chapter says the team does not put raw ideas into a backlog and does not answer yes or no on first contact, because it does not yet understand what is being asked for.

Where this topic fits

Discovery sits between receiving the request and designing anything. Shape Up calls the step narrowing down the problem. Its output is a specific situation in which the customer's current workflow breaks, which the team then uses as a baseline to design against.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Receive the request and give a soft, non-committal answer instead of adding it to a list of work.
  2. Contact a customer who asked, and ask about the moment the need arose (when they wanted it and what they were doing) rather than why they want it or what it should look like.
  3. Reconstruct the specific event. For the permissions request, a file had been archived by someone who did not know it would disappear for everyone else. For the calendar, a customer working from home had to drive to the office to read a wall calendar of meeting-room bookings.
  4. Restate the need in terms of that event, such as seeing free spaces rather than doing everything a calendar does.
  5. Compare the narrowed need with what the product already does. Basecamp's agenda view listed events but could not show empty slots.
  6. Design the smallest change that addresses the narrowed problem, such as a warning on the archive action.

Why this approach fits

A feature request compresses a situation into a solution. Asking when the need arose recovers the situation, which is concrete, can be checked with other customers, and is usually much smaller than the request. The cost comparison is stark: a conversation with the customer against six weeks of building the wrong thing.

Limitation or tradeoff

One customer's story is one data point; the chapter refers to this story "and others like it", so the team gathered several. The technique depends on the customer remembering a specific event. The narrowed fix can also leave part of the original wish unmet, which has to be explained to the person who asked. Shape Up adds that when no specific pain point can be found the team walks away, an option a paid engagement may not have.

What the sources document

  • A customer asked Basecamp for more complex permission rules, a change the author says could easily have taken six weeks to build. [3]
  • Digging deeper showed that someone had archived a file without knowing it would disappear for everyone else. The team realised it could put a warning on the archive action that explains the impact, which the author calls a one-day change instead of a six-week project. [3]
  • For the calendar request, the team called a customer and asked when she had wanted a calendar, rather than why she wanted one or what it should look like. [3]
  • Her account was of needing to check a wall calendar of meeting-room bookings while working from home. The team concluded that seeing free spaces was the need, not everything a calendar does. [3]
  • The chapter says the default response to a raw idea is a soft no that keeps options open, and that such ideas are not put in a backlog. [3]

Example 2 of 5

Intuit Follow-Me-Homes: watching customers work before writing a problem statement

IntuitDocumented in an engineering article

Intuit's company blog describes Design for Delight, the set of principles it says its employees use to innovate. The first principle, Deep Customer Empathy, starts with a visit the company calls a Follow-Me-Home. [4]

The problem

Teams building financial software for consumers and small businesses need to know which pains customers actually experience. The post says that second-hand information and conjecture might not be accurate, so teams are told to observe behaviour first-hand before agreeing on a problem to solve.

Where this topic fits

Observation is the first of four steps the post lists: Follow-Me-Homes, Deep Probing Interviews, a Customer Problem Statement, and an Ideal State. Watching is listed before interviewing. The post says the problem statement is most often written after Follow-Me-Homes or a customer debrief, although it can be written at any time.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Find real or potential customers who match the target. The post suggests getting help from Intuit Studios, using personal networks or simply asking people in the target group.
  2. Go to where the problem is experienced, which is the home for consumers and the office for small businesses.
  3. Set the context, then watch. Ask the customer to show rather than describe, and ask why they chose to do it that way.
  4. Keep the visit to between 30 and 90 minutes, and do not run it as an interview.
  5. Debrief as a team straight afterwards, sharing observations, pain points and surprises.
  6. Follow up with probing interviews held close in time to a behaviour of interest, such as a cancellation, expecting that the first answer may not be the root cause.
  7. Write several problem statements from what was observed, choose one, and keep solutions out of it.

Why this approach fits

Bookkeeping and invoicing happen in the middle of other work, surrounded by paper, other apps and interruptions that customers do not think to mention. Watching exposes those workarounds. Running observation, then probing, then the written statement keeps the statement tied to behaviour somebody saw.

Limitation or tradeoff

Visits cost time: up to 90 minutes each plus travel, for a handful of customers. An observer changes how people behave, and some work, such as annual tax filing, cannot be watched on demand. The post describes the method and gives no measured results, so it shows that Intuit uses the practice, not how much it improves outcomes.

What the sources document

  • Intuit describes Follow-Me-Homes as a way to observe people experiencing problems in their own environment, and says each should take 30 to 90 minutes per customer. [4]
  • The post states that a Follow-Me-Home is not an interview and gives an order of work, which is to observe first, ask the customer to show second, and inquire third. [4]
  • Teams are told to debrief as soon as possible after the visit, sharing observations, pain points and surprises. [4]
  • Deep Probing Interviews are described as best held close to the behaviour being studied, lasting 45 to 60 minutes, with a warning that first answers may not be the real reason. [4]
  • The Customer Problem Statement template has five parts (I am, I am trying to, But, Because, Which makes me feel), and the post says to avoid suggesting solutions in it. [4]

Microsoft ISE envisioning sessions and data exploration workshops before ML work

Microsoft Industry Solutions EngineeringDocumented in official docs

The ISE Engineering Fundamentals Playbook is published openly by Microsoft's Industry Solutions Engineering team, which describes itself as working side-by-side with customers. Its machine learning section documents the envisioning and data exploration stages that come before any data science investigation. [5][6][7]

The problem

Before a data science investigation the team needs a problem statement it can explore, and the playbook says that statement can strongly influence whether the project succeeds. Objectives are often presented loosely. Its example is "to improve sales" for a clothing retailer, which gives no way to tell a recommendation engine's effect apart from a seasonal sales surge.

Where this topic fits

Envisioning comes first and ends with a summary document. The team then confirms access to data and compute, and runs data exploration workshops, typically as part of a feasibility study. Modelling starts only after these.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Hold envisioning sessions with subject-matter experts to establish what the problem is, why it needs solving, whether it needs machine learning at all, and how a solution would be used.
  2. Talk to the actual end users about current practice, the performance of the current solution and their pain points, because stakeholders are often not the users.
  3. State a measurable objective in business terms, decide how performance will be measured, and ask what minimum performance would reach the business objective.
  4. Ask how an expert would solve the problem by hand and what data they would need. If an expert cannot solve it with the available data, more data is needed.
  5. Write hypothesis statements that can be proved or disproved, with a success criterion wherever one is possible.
  6. Summarise the proposed scenarios, assumptions, agreed decisions and next steps in an exit document, so that all parties share the same understanding of the scope.
  7. Confirm accounts, network access, legal approval and who may move data, then run the workshop on the data's features, location, size, quality, collection method and recent changes.

Why this approach fits

Machine learning projects fail on framing and data access at least as often as on modelling. These questions show early whether the objective can be measured, whether data to evaluate a solution exists (the playbook notes it may differ from the data needed to build one), and whether the team can reach the data at all.

Limitation or tradeoff

It is a checklist, so its value depends on who attends; the playbook itself lists making sure the subject-matter experts are present as a purpose of the workshop. Several questions assume Azure. Thresholds are not always possible: where no solution exists yet, the playbook accepts an expert's judgement as the measure of success, which is subjective. Answers given in a workshop are claims until someone has looked at the data.

What the sources document

  • The playbook lists the goals of envisioning as understanding the problem domain and business objective, defining how a solution would be used and measured, and determining what data is available. [5]
  • It notes that stakeholders are often not the end user of the solution and calls listening to the actual end users critical to the project's success. [5]
  • It advises defining hypothesis statements that can be proved or disproved, each with a clearly defined success criterion where possible, and gives an accuracy threshold as a sample phrasing. [5]
  • The data exploration page lists questions to settle before the workshop, including whether data access is approved from a legal and compliance perspective and who has permission to move on-premises data. [6]
  • Workshop objectives include documenting the features, location and availability of the data, its order of magnitude, its quality, how it was collected and any biases that result. [6]

Example 4 of 5

Palantir Foundry use case scoping: requirements written around user decisions

PalantirDocumented in official docs

Palantir's public Foundry documentation has a Use case lifecycle section. Its page on distilling functional requirements describes what to write down about a use case before development starts on the platform. [8][9][10]

The problem

Work is often requested as an implementation. The documentation says a use case is not "integrate source system X" or "apply ML technique Y", and its getting-started guide warns against starting from a need to build a sales dashboard. It asks instead whose working life improves and what can be measured to know the work succeeded.

Where this topic fits

Requirements are distilled after the use case is described and before solution design. The resulting statements feed decisions about interfaces, data enrichment and the data model that the documentation calls the ontology.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Write a short use case overview covering the motivation, the decisions being made and who makes them, the stakeholders, the outcomes and how they are measured, and how the work is done today with its pain points.
  2. Express each functional requirement in five parts, which are the user type, the interface, the decision, the decision inputs and the action.
  3. Read the decision inputs as a list of data needs, because each input must be sourced, enriched or computed from somewhere.
  4. Work backwards from the outcome to the data, and check which of it is already available before requesting new sources.
  5. Check the draft against the listed warning signs, such as requirements tied to specific screen elements or scoping that never reaches a user decision.
  6. Where a strict business requirement is costly, consider a functionally equivalent one that is cheaper to build.

Why this approach fits

Anchoring a requirement on a decision ties the screen to the data. The decision inputs become the data map and the action becomes the list of things the system must write back. It also keeps the requirement independent of any particular screen design, which the documentation says leaves room to choose an implementation.

Limitation or tradeoff

The format suits interactive, operational decisions. It is awkward for requirements with no user in them, such as batch pipelines, latency limits or security rules. It carries no threshold and no verification method, so acceptance criteria have to be added. The guidance is written for building on Foundry, so its implementation advice assumes that platform's tools.

What the sources document

  • The documentation defines a use case as a time-bound effort by a dedicated team to deliver new capabilities for a set of users, and says it is not integrating a source system or applying an ML technique. [9]
  • The use case overview template asks what decisions are being made and which users make them, and how the work is currently accomplished and what its limitations or pain points are. [8]
  • Functional requirements are suggested in the format user type, interface, decision, decision inputs and action, illustrated with a flight route alert triage example. [8]
  • Listed warning flags for the requirements phase include insufficient investigation into current pain points and requirements anchored on specific UI elements. [8]
  • The getting-started guide advises thinking about the outcome before the method and working backwards from the outcome to identify the necessary data. [10]

Example 5 of 5

Kiro specs: EARS requirements as the input an AI coding agent builds from

Kiro (Amazon Web Services)Documented in official docs

Kiro is an AI development tool whose website names Amazon Web Services as its parent organisation. Its documentation describes feature specs, a workflow in which a requirements.md file is produced and confirmed by a person before a design and a task list are generated. The pages cited were updated in August and September 2026. [11][12][13][14]

The problem

An AI coding agent given a loose prompt will implement one interpretation of it. The documentation describes the Requirements-First workflow as getting the "what" right before committing to the "how", and says that wording such as "large files" or "fast response times" would produce divergent implementations.

Where this topic fits

Requirements are the first of three phases: requirements, then design, then tasks. A person reviews and confirms the requirements before the design is generated, so this is the point where discovery findings enter the build.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Describe the feature in the opening prompt, covering who the user is, what they want to accomplish, why, and the success criteria.
  2. Kiro generates requirements.md with user stories, acceptance criteria and system behaviours in EARS form.
  3. A person reviews the file for completeness, adds missing scenarios and edge cases, and iterates.
  4. Optionally run Analyze Requirements, which reasons across the whole set and asks clarifying questions about inconsistencies, ambiguities and gaps. Answers update the file.
  5. Confirm the requirements. Kiro then generates the design document and the task list from them.
  6. For a customer-driven feature, validate the requirements with the customer before generating the design.

Why this approach fits

An agent cannot ask the customer what they meant, so anything ambiguous is decided silently in code. A fixed trigger-and-response sentence removes most of that room, and each statement converts directly into a test. The requirements file also becomes the thing a person reviews, which is cheaper than reviewing generated code.

Limitation or tradeoff

The requirements are generated from a prompt, so they are only as true as the discovery behind it. A well-formed EARS statement can specify the wrong behaviour precisely. The pattern shown in Kiro's pages is the event-driven one; EARS also defines state-driven, optional-feature and unwanted-behaviour forms. A testable requirement is also not a tested one: someone still has to write and run the test. The review step can also be skipped, because the same documentation describes a Quick Spec option that runs all three phases without approval gates between them.

What the sources document

  • Kiro's documentation says the requirements.md file uses EARS notation to provide structured, testable requirements, following a pattern of WHEN a condition or event, THE SYSTEM SHALL an expected behaviour. [11]
  • Feature specs offer two workflows. Requirements-First runs requirements, design, tasks. Design-First starts from a technical design and derives requirements from it. [11]
  • The Requirements-First page lists what the generated file includes (user stories with acceptance criteria, behaviours in EARS format, functional requirements, edge cases and error handling) and asks the person to review and confirm before design. [12]
  • Analyze Requirements is described as reasoning across the full requirement set to find logical inconsistencies, ambiguities, conflicting constraints, unstated assumptions and missing edge cases. [13]
  • EARS was developed at Rolls-Royce while analysing airworthiness regulations for a jet engine's control system, and was first published in 2009. [14]

4Common mistakes

  1. Asking users what they want, or whether they would use the thing you plan to build.

    Why it causes problems
    People answer politely and guess about a future they cannot picture. You leave with agreement that tells you nothing about what they do today, and build on it.
    How to recognise it
    Your notes are full of opinions and words like would, could and probably, and contain no account of a specific ticket, order or case.
    How to fix it
    Ask about the last time it happened and have the person walk through it. Ask to see the actual record, then watch the next one being handled.

  2. Interviewing only the sponsor and the managers.

    Why it causes problems
    The sponsor describes the process as designed. The workarounds, private spreadsheets and retyping that consume the time are known only to the people doing the work.
    How to recognise it
    Nobody you have spoken to has performed the task this month, and your process description has no exceptions in it.
    How to fix it
    Ask for shadowing time with at least two people who do the task, and with the person who owns each system the task touches.

  3. Writing requirements that name a screen or a technology instead of a capability.

    Why it causes problems
    A statement such as a dashboard with a region dropdown fixes the design before the need is understood, and it can be delivered in full without helping anyone decide anything.
    How to recognise it
    The requirement mentions a widget, a chart type or a model name, and you cannot say which decision it supports or what data the decision needs.
    How to fix it
    Rewrite it around who decides what, using which inputs, to take which action. Let the design follow from that.

  4. Using words that cannot be verified, such as fast, accurate, easy or user-friendly.

    Why it causes problems
    Each side reads its own threshold into the word. The disagreement surfaces at acceptance, when changing course is most expensive.
    How to recognise it
    You cannot describe the test that would fail the requirement, or two people give different pass marks.
    How to fix it
    Replace the word with a number, a tolerance and a verification method, taken from the baseline you measured.

  5. Leaving the data and systems map until the build has started.

    Why it causes problems
    Missing join keys, a system with no API, or a legal approval that takes weeks then stop work that is already scheduled and promised.
    How to recognise it
    The plan names data sources that nobody on your team has queried, and no named person has approved access.
    How to fix it
    In the first days, get one real record from every source in front of you and write down the owner, access path and join key for each.

  6. Trusting an average taken across every kind of case.

    Why it causes problems
    Different case types have different bottlenecks, so the overall average can point at a step that matters little for the most common type.
    How to recognise it
    Your time study has one row of totals and no breakdown by category, customer type or channel.
    How to fix it
    Split the observations by case type before drawing conclusions, and choose the first slice from the largest group.

Exercise 1

Build a linter that flags untestable requirement statements

CodingBeginnerAbout 40 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Write a Python script, using only the standard library, that checks requirement statements and reports the EARS pattern each one follows and any wording problems.

Use these six statements as input:

text
R1 The assistant should be fast and user-friendly.
R2 When an agent opens an order-status ticket, the lookup panel shall display the ERP order status within 5 seconds.
R3 The system shall handle large files and shall retry failed uploads as appropriate.
R4 If the carrier source returns an error, then the lookup panel shall display "tracking unavailable".
R5 While the ERP is in maintenance mode, when an agent opens a ticket, the lookup panel shall display the last cached status.
R6 The export shall include customer name, address, etc.

Treat these as unverifiable terms: flexible, easy, sufficient, safe, adequate, user-friendly, usable, appropriate, fast, small, large, robust, quickly, easily, clearly. Treat these as ambiguous phrases: "as appropriate", "etc.", "and/or", "but not limited to".

Requirements

  • Classify each statement as ubiquitous, state-driven, event-driven, optional-feature, unwanted-behaviour or complex, from its leading keyword.
  • Report a problem when a statement has no shall, and a different problem when it has more than one.
  • Report every unverifiable term and every ambiguous phrase found, without reporting the word appropriate twice when it is part of the phrase as appropriate.
  • Print one line per statement with its id, OK or FAIL, the pattern and the problems.

Expected behaviour

R2, R4 and R5 pass and are classified as event-driven, unwanted-behaviour and complex. R1 fails for having no shall and for two unverifiable terms. R3 fails for two shall clauses, the term large and the phrase as appropriate. R6 fails for the phrase etc.

Hint 1

Lower-case the statement once and test the leading keyword with startswith. Check for the complex pattern before the state-driven one.

Hint 2

A plain substring test finds fast inside breakfast. Use a regular expression that requires a non-word character on each side.

Hint 3

Remove the ambiguous phrases from the text before searching for the single unverifiable terms.

Show solution
python
import re

VAGUE = ["flexible", "easy", "sufficient", "safe", "adequate", "user-friendly",
         "usable", "appropriate", "fast", "small", "large", "robust",
         "quickly", "easily", "clearly"]
AMBIGUOUS = ["as appropriate", "etc.", "and/or", "but not limited to"]


def ears_pattern(text):
    low = text.lower().strip()
    if low.startswith("while ") and ", when " in low:
        return "complex"
    if low.startswith("while "):
        return "state-driven"
    if low.startswith("when "):
        return "event-driven"
    if low.startswith("where "):
        return "optional-feature"
    if low.startswith("if ") and ", then " in low:
        return "unwanted-behaviour"
    return "ubiquitous"


def lint(text):
    low = text.lower()
    problems = []
    shall_count = len(re.findall(r"\bshall\b", low))
    if shall_count == 0:
        problems.append("no 'shall'")
    elif shall_count > 1:
        problems.append(f"{shall_count} 'shall' clauses: split it")
    ambiguous = [term for term in AMBIGUOUS if term in low]
    remaining = low
    for term in ambiguous:
        remaining = remaining.replace(term, " ")
    vague = [term for term in VAGUE
             if re.search(rf"(?<![\w-]){re.escape(term)}(?![\w-])", remaining)]
    if vague:
        problems.append("unverifiable: " + ", ".join(vague))
    if ambiguous:
        problems.append("ambiguous: " + ", ".join(ambiguous))
    return ears_pattern(text), problems


REQUIREMENTS = [
    "The assistant should be fast and user-friendly.",
    "When an agent opens an order-status ticket, the lookup panel shall display the ERP order status within 5 seconds.",
    "The system shall handle large files and shall retry failed uploads as appropriate.",
    'If the carrier source returns an error, then the lookup panel shall display "tracking unavailable".',
    "While the ERP is in maintenance mode, when an agent opens a ticket, the lookup panel shall display the last cached status.",
    "The export shall include customer name, address, etc.",
]

for number, requirement in enumerate(REQUIREMENTS, start=1):
    pattern, problems = lint(requirement)
    verdict = "FAIL" if problems else "OK"
    print(f"R{number} {verdict:<4} {pattern:<19}{'; '.join(problems)}".rstrip())

Output:

text
R1 FAIL ubiquitous         no 'shall'; unverifiable: user-friendly, fast
R2 OK   event-driven
R3 FAIL ubiquitous         2 'shall' clauses: split it; unverifiable: large; ambiguous: as appropriate
R4 OK   unwanted-behaviour
R5 OK   complex
R6 FAIL ubiquitous         ambiguous: etc.

The lookbehind and lookahead in the regular expression stop fast matching inside a longer word and stop easy matching inside a hyphenated term. Removing the ambiguous phrases first is what prevents appropriate being reported twice for R3.

A linter of this kind catches wording, not truth. R2 passes because it is well formed, but nothing here checks that 5 seconds is the right threshold or that the panel is the right thing to build. Those answers come from the baseline and the customer.

Check your answer

  • Does your output mark exactly R1, R3 and R6 as FAIL?
  • Is R5 classified as complex rather than state-driven?
  • Does R3 report as appropriate once, without also listing appropriate as an unverifiable term?
  • If you add the statement "The breakfast menu shall list prices", does it pass without flagging fast?
