Statistics for ML

Statistics is the set of methods for summarising data and for judging how far a number measured on a sample can be trusted for the wider population the sample came from. In machine learning it supplies the summaries, error bars and tests used to decide whether a measured difference between models is real or only noise.

1Overview

Why it matters

Every number in a model evaluation is computed from a sample: a test set of a few hundred or a few thousand examples, a batch of benchmark questions, a week of traffic in an A/B test. Draw a different sample and the number moves. A team that does not know how far it moves will ship a "2-point improvement" that was noise, discard a real gain because one run looked bad, or rank models on a leaderboard by differences smaller than the measurement error.

The same tools guard against a quieter mistake. A model learns whatever patterns predict the target in the data it was given, and a pattern can predict well without being a cause. Reading a correlation, a coefficient or a feature importance as "what will happen if we change this" has produced models that scored well and recommended harmful actions. ML engineers comparing models, analysts reading experiment dashboards and anyone who signs off a launch need enough statistics to ask three questions of any result: how was the sample drawn, how large is the uncertainty, and does this show a cause or only an association?

Key terms

Population
The complete set of cases you want to make a statement about, such as every question a model will be asked after launch. It is usually too large to observe in full.
Sample
The subset of the population you actually measured, such as the 300 questions in a test set. Conclusions carry over only if it was drawn fairly, ideally at random.
Standard deviation
A measure of spread, roughly the typical distance of a value from the mean, in the same units as the data.
Standard error
The standard deviation of a statistic across repeated samples. For a mean it is the standard deviation of the data divided by the square root of the sample size.
Sampling distribution
The distribution of values a statistic, such as a sample mean, would take if the sampling were repeated many times.
Central limit theorem
The result that the mean of many independent observations is approximately normally distributed, whatever the shape of the data it was computed from.
Confidence interval
A range computed from a sample by a procedure that captures the true population value in a stated share of repeated samples, commonly 95 %.
Null hypothesis
The default claim a test tries to discredit, usually that there is no difference or no effect.
p-value
The probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
Confounder
A variable that influences both of two other variables and so makes them correlated even when neither affects the other.

2Explanation

Introduction

You use statistics every time you read a score. "Model B is 83 % accurate" squeezes several hundred right-or-wrong answers into one number. This lesson is about what such a number hides and how much weight it can bear.

Take a small case. Two classifiers are run on the same 300 test questions. Model A gets 231 right (77 %) and model B gets 249 right (83 %). B looks six points better. But those 300 questions are only a sample: a few hundred examples drawn from the much larger population of questions the model will meet after launch. A different draw of 300 would have given both models slightly different scores, in the same way that two handfuls from one bag of mixed sweets never hold exactly the same colours. The question is whether six points is more than that handful-to-handful wobble.

Statistics answers in four moves, and the lesson follows them in order.

  1. Describe the sample with a few numbers: where the values sit (mean, median) and how spread out they are (standard deviation, percentiles).
  2. Quantify the sampling error. The standard error says how much a mean would vary from sample to sample, and the central limit theorem says that this variation follows a bell curve once the sample is reasonably large.
  3. Infer. A confidence interval turns the standard error into a range of plausible values. A hypothesis test asks how surprising the result would be if there were no real difference, and reports the answer as a p-value.
  4. Interpret. A difference can be real and still not mean what you hoped. The last part explains why correlation is not causation and what a randomised experiment changes.

The lesson builds on probability for ML: you need the normal distribution, expectation and variance from there. The worked example returns to models A and B and shows the same 300 answers giving a p-value of 0.065 or 0.005, depending on whether the analysis uses the fact that both models answered the same questions. These tools are put to work next in train/test splits and cross-validation and later in A/B testing.

Samples vary: the standard error and the central limit theorem

A statistic computed from a sample is itself random, because another sample would give another value. Reasoning about that randomness is valid only if the sample represents the population, which in practice means it was drawn at random. A biased sample gives a confidently wrong answer however large it is. Given random sampling, the spread of a sample mean across repeated samples is its standard error:

SE(xˉ)=sn\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}SE(xˉ)=n​s​

Less variable data shrinks it, and so does more data, but only by the square root: halving the error takes four times the sample. Accuracy is the mean of 0/1 values with standard deviation p(1−p)\sqrt{p(1-p)}p(1−p)​, so

SE(p^)=p^ (1−p^)n\text{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\,(1-\hat{p})}{n}}SE(p^​)=np^​(1−p^​)​​

The central limit theorem (CLT) adds the shape. As OpenStax puts it (opens in a new tab), the distribution of sample means approaches a normal distribution as nnn grows, regardless of the shape of the original distribution. The plot shows it for a strongly skewed population, an exponential distribution with mean 1, where the mean of nnn independent draws has the exact density fn(x)=nnxn−1e−nx/(n−1)!f_n(x) = n^n x^{n-1} e^{-nx} / (n-1)!fn​(x)=nnxn−1e−nx/(n−1)!.

The CLT needs independent observations with finite variance. It takes hold quickly for symmetric data and slowly for skewed or heavy-tailed data.

Confidence intervals

A confidence interval is the estimate plus and minus a critical value times the standard error. For a large sample the 95 % critical value comes from the normal distribution: 1.96. For a small sample, sss is itself a shaky estimate, so the t distribution (opens in a new tab) with n−1n - 1n−1 degrees of freedom supplies a larger one:

xˉ±t0.975, n−1⋅sn\bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}xˉ±t0.975,n−1​⋅n​s​

The "95 %" describes the procedure: NIST's wording is that if many samples were collected and the interval computed each time, about 95 % of those intervals would contain the true mean. It is not a 95 % probability that this one interval does. It also covers sampling error only, so a biased sample or a buggy metric sits outside it. When no formula fits the statistic (a median, an F1 score), the bootstrap builds an interval by resampling the data with replacement, which is what scipy.stats.bootstrap (opens in a new tab) does.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

A test turns "is this difference real?" into a calculation.

  1. State a null hypothesis H0H_0H0​, the dull explanation: the two models are equally accurate.
  2. Compute a test statistic: the observed difference divided by its standard error.
  3. Compute the p-value: the probability, if H0H_0H0​ were true, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
  4. Compare it with a threshold α\alphaα chosen in advance, often 0.05. If p<αp < \alphap<α, reject H0H_0H0​ and call the result statistically significant.

The grid shows the two ways a test can be wrong. In the terms of the NIST handbook (opens in a new tab), α\alphaα is the risk of rejecting a null hypothesis that is true, and β\betaβ is the risk of failing to reject one that is false. Power, 1−β1 - \beta1−β, grows with the sample size and with the size of the real effect.

When there is truly no difference, p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1, so about 5 % of them fall below 0.05. Microsoft's experimentation team checks exactly this (opens in a new tab) by simulating experiments in which both groups get the same product.

The American Statistical Association's statement on p-values (opens in a new tab) rules out three common readings. A p-value is not the probability that H0H_0H0​ is true. It does not measure the size or importance of an effect: with millions of rows a trivial difference gets a tiny p-value, and with thirty rows a large one may not reach 0.05. And a decision should not rest on the threshold alone. "Not significant" is also not "no difference"; it often means the sample was too small to tell. Report the estimate and its interval beside the p-value. A two-sided test at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05 rejects exactly when the 95 % interval for the difference excludes zero, so the interval gives the verdict and the size of the effect together.

When the two samples are paired, with the same questions answered by both models or the same users measured before and after, test the per-item differences and not the two averages. NIST's t-test page (opens in a new tab) describes paired data as a one-to-one correspondence between the samples, and the worked example shows how much it matters.

Many tests inflate false positives. With 20 independent comparisons and no real effects, the chance that at least one has p<0.05p < 0.05p<0.05 is 1−0.9520=0.641 - 0.95^{20} = 0.641−0.9520=0.64.

Correlation is not causation

The correlation coefficient rrr measures how closely two variables follow a straight line, from −1 through 0 to +1:

r=∑(xi−xˉ)(yi−yˉ)∑(xi−xˉ)2  ∑(yi−yˉ)2r = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\;\sqrt{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}r=∑(xi​−xˉ)2​∑(yi​−yˉ​)2​∑(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​)​

A correlation between xxx and yyy has four possible explanations: xxx causes yyy; yyy causes xxx; a third variable, a confounder, drives both; or the pattern is chance or an artefact of how the rows were selected. The data alone cannot separate them. In this simulation, using a feature has no effect on spending, yet the two are strongly correlated because long-tenured customers do more of both:

python
import random
from statistics import correlation

rng = random.Random(1)
tenure = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(5000)]        # hidden common cause
uses_feature = [t + rng.gauss(0, 1) for t in tenure]
spend = [2 * t + rng.gauss(0, 1) for t in tenure]
print(round(correlation(uses_feature, spend), 2))      # 0.63

assigned = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(5000)]      # set by chance, not by the customer
print(round(correlation(assigned, spend), 2))          # -0.01

The last two lines are the remedy. When the experimenter assigns the treatment at random, nothing about the customer can influence who receives it, so the confounder's path is cut and a difference larger than sampling noise is caused by the treatment. That is what an A/B test does.

This matters in ML because a model is fitted to associations. scikit-learn's example on causal interpretation (opens in a new tab) fits a wage model that keeps an R2R^2R2 of 0.968 after an unobserved confounder, ability, is left out, while its coefficient for a college degree is inflated. Predictions can be accurate while the implied effect is wrong.

Step 5: check it in code

The script needs only the Python standard library (version 3.10 or later). Its last block is a permutation test, which finds a p-value without a formula. If the models were interchangeable, each question's difference would be as likely to be −di-d_i−di​ as +di+d_i+di​. So flip the signs at random 20,000 times and count how often the total lands at least as far from zero as the observed one.

python
import math
import random
from statistics import NormalDist, correlation, fmean, stdev

# 300 test questions: (A correct, B correct) -> number of questions
counts = {(1, 1): 219, (1, 0): 12, (0, 1): 30, (0, 0): 39}
pairs = [pair for pair, k in counts.items() for _ in range(k)]
n = len(pairs)
a = [p[0] for p in pairs]
b = [p[1] for p in pairs]
z95 = NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.975)                      # 1.96

def p_two_sided(z):
    return 2 * (1 - NormalDist().cdf(abs(z)))

se = {}
for name, x in (("A", a), ("B", b)):
    acc = fmean(x)
    se[name] = math.sqrt(acc * (1 - acc) / n)
    print(f"{name}: accuracy {acc:.3f}  SE {se[name]:.4f}  "
          f"95% CI ({acc - z95 * se[name]:.3f}, {acc + z95 * se[name]:.3f})")

diff = fmean(b) - fmean(a)
se_unpaired = math.sqrt(se["A"] ** 2 + se["B"] ** 2)
print(f"unpaired: diff {diff:.3f}  SE {se_unpaired:.4f}  z {diff / se_unpaired:.2f}  "
      f"p {p_two_sided(diff / se_unpaired):.3f}")

d = [bi - ai for ai, bi in pairs]                      # +1, 0 or -1 per question
se_paired = stdev(d) / math.sqrt(n)
print(f"paired:   diff {fmean(d):.3f}  SE {se_paired:.4f}  z {fmean(d) / se_paired:.2f}  "
      f"p {p_two_sided(fmean(d) / se_paired):.3f}  "
      f"95% CI ({fmean(d) - z95 * se_paired:.3f}, {fmean(d) + z95 * se_paired:.3f})")
print(f"correlation between the two models' scores: {correlation(a, b):.2f}")

rng = random.Random(0)
observed = abs(sum(d))
extreme = sum(
    abs(sum(x if rng.random() < 0.5 else -x for x in d)) >= observed
    for _ in range(20_000)
)
print(f"permutation p-value: {(extreme + 1) / (20_000 + 1):.4f}")

Step 6: read the result

  • B is better on questions like these, by somewhere between 2 and 10 points. The interval is the result. Six points is only its centre.
  • The paired analysis is the valid one, not merely the favourable one. The unpaired formula assumes independent samples, which is false here. The 0.58 correlation between the models' per-question scores is what pairing exploits.
  • The permutation p-value (0.008) and the normal approximation (0.005) agree closely enough that the conclusion does not hang on the formula.
  • The claim stops at the population the questions were drawn from. If the 300 questions are not a fair sample of production traffic, or were used to tune B, the interval is honest arithmetic about the wrong quantity.

Edge cases and limitations

Small samples and proportions near 0 or 1
The normal-based interval assumes the sampling distribution is already bell-shaped. With 19 correct answers out of 20, the formula gives 0.95 ± 0.096, an interval from 0.854 to 1.046 that runs past 100 %. The Wilson score interval for the same data is 0.764 to 0.991. As a working rule, use the plain formula only when the successes and the failures both number at least 10. Otherwise use a Wilson or exact interval, or a bootstrap.
Observations that are not independent
Every formula in this lesson divides by √n on the assumption that the n observations are independent. Ten questions about one passage, forty rows from one user, or cross-validation folds that share training data carry less information than their count suggests, so the standard error comes out too small and results look more significant than they are. Anthropic reports clustered standard errors on popular evals more than three times the naive ones. Compute the standard error at the level of the independent unit, such as the passage or the user, or use a clustered formula.
Very large samples make every difference significant
As n grows the standard error shrinks toward zero, so a test eventually detects differences too small to matter. Google's data-validation team altered 0.01 % of 100 million values and found that a chi-squared test at the 1 % level would have raised an alert in 7 of 10 trials. With data at that scale a p-value answers "is there any difference at all?", which is rarely the question. Decide in advance the smallest difference that would change a decision and compare the confidence interval with it.
Many comparisons, peeking and reused test sets
A 5 % false-positive rate applies to one test planned in advance. Check 20 metrics, 20 segments or 20 model variants and the chance of at least one false positive is 64 %. Looking at a running experiment every day and stopping the first time p drops below 0.05 inflates the rate in the same way, and so does choosing the best of many models on a test set and then reporting its score on that same set. Fix the primary metric and sample size beforehand, tighten the threshold when many tests are run (dividing α by the number of tests is the simplest correction), and keep a final test set that no decision has touched.
Heavy tails and single outliers
For metrics such as revenue per user or clicks per session, one extreme value can dominate the mean and its standard deviation, and the central limit theorem takes far longer to apply. Microsoft's simulated A/A tests exposed metrics whose p-values were not evenly spread because a single user had over a million duplicated clicks. Look at the maximum and the top percentiles before testing a mean, and consider capping extreme values, bootstrapping a median or percentile, or analysing the logarithm.
A correlation of zero does not mean no relationship
The correlation coefficient measures straight-line association only. If y equals x squared and x is spread symmetrically around zero, y is completely determined by x and yet r is 0. A single outlier can also create a strong r between unrelated variables or hide a real one. Plot the two variables before quoting r, and use a rank correlation such as Spearman's, which Python offers as correlation(x, y, method='ranked'), when the relationship is monotonic but curved.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • You are comparing two models, prompts or configurations on a finite test set and the difference between them is a few points or less.
  • You are reporting a metric to someone who will act on it, and they need to know its margin of error.
  • You are planning an evaluation or experiment and must decide how many examples or users are enough to detect the difference you care about.
  • You are reading an A/B test, a benchmark table or a paper and need to judge whether a claimed gain is larger than the noise.
  • You are checking whether a feature's distribution has shifted between two batches of data.

Think twice when

  • The sample is biased or was not drawn from the population you care about. Error bars measure sampling error only and will sit reassuringly around the wrong number.
  • You need a causal answer from observational data. A significant correlation is still a correlation, so randomise if you can and otherwise treat the result as a hypothesis.
  • You measured the whole population, such as every transaction last month, and only want to describe it. There is no sampling error to estimate.
  • The dataset is so large that every difference is significant. A test adds nothing there, so judge the size of the effect against a threshold that matters.

A simpler alternative. For a quick check, use the rule that the 95 % margin of error of an accuracy is at most about 1/√n: roughly ±10 points on 100 examples, ±3 on 1,000 and ±1 on 10,000. If the difference you see is several times that, no formal test is needed, and if it is smaller, collect more data before arguing about it. The rule stops being enough when the comparison is paired, the observations come in groups, or the metric is not a simple proportion. Then compute the standard error properly or bootstrap it.

Key takeaways

  • Any score computed on a sample is an estimate, so report it with its sample size and a standard error or confidence interval.
  • The standard error of a mean is the standard deviation divided by √n, which means halving the uncertainty takes four times the data.
  • The central limit theorem makes sample means approximately normal, so the estimate ± 1.96 standard errors is a 95 % interval for large samples, and small samples need the wider t value.
  • A p-value is the probability of a result at least this extreme if there were no real difference. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it says nothing about the size of the effect.
  • When two models are scored on the same items, analyse the per-item differences, because ignoring the pairing throws away precision and can change the conclusion.
  • Correlation shows that two things move together, not that one causes the other. Only random assignment, or a design that rules out confounders, supports a causal claim.

Anthropic: standard errors and paired differences for language-model evals

AnthropicDocumented in a paper

Anthropic's research post "A statistical approach to model evaluations" (November 2024) summarises the paper "Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations" by Evan Miller. Both set out how eval scores should be analysed and reported, and the paper includes standard errors computed on real evals with Anthropic models. [13][14]

The problem

A language-model evaluation ("eval") is a list of questions with a score for each answer, usually reported as one average per model. Models are then ranked on differences of a few points. Without a measure of uncertainty there is no way to tell whether such a difference reflects the models or the particular questions that happened to be in the eval. The paper opens with two fictional models whose ranking flips between three evals and asks whether anything can be concluded from the table.

Where this topic fits

The statistics sit between the eval harness, which produces a score per question, and the published comparison table. The key modelling step is to treat the eval's questions as a sample drawn from a much larger unseen population of possible questions. The mean score then becomes an estimate with a standard error, and everything in this lesson applies: the central limit theorem, confidence intervals, paired tests and sample-size planning.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Treat the eval's questions as a random draw from a larger population of possible questions, so the observed mean score is an estimate of an underlying skill and has sampling error.
  2. Compute the standard error of the mean from the per-question scores with the central limit theorem formula, the square root of the sample variance divided by the number of questions, and print it in parentheses beside the score together with the question count.
  3. Form a 95 % confidence interval as the mean score plus and minus 1.96 standard errors.
  4. Where questions come in related groups, such as several questions about one text passage, compute clustered standard errors so that correlated questions are not counted as independent evidence.
  5. To compare two models that answered the same questions, take the difference in score on each question and compute the standard error of those differences, instead of combining two separate standard errors.
  6. Before running or commissioning an eval, use the sample-size formula to find how many questions are needed to detect a difference of a chosen size at a chosen false-positive rate and power.

Why this approach fits

An eval is close to the textbook setting for the central limit theorem: hundreds or thousands of questions, each scored on a bounded scale, averaged into one number. That makes the standard error cheap to compute from data the harness already has.

The paired analysis is the part that changes conclusions. Two models tend to find the same questions easy and the same questions hard, so their per-question scores are positively correlated, and taking differences question by question cancels that shared difficulty. It is the same effect as in this lesson's worked example, where pairing cut the standard error from 0.0326 to 0.0214 at no extra cost.

Limitation or tradeoff

The framework rests on the assumption that the questions are a random sample from the population of interest. Real evals are written or collected by people, and an eval can be precise about a skill nobody needs. A standard error also says nothing about test questions that leaked into training data, about sensitivity to prompt wording, or about errors in the grader.

Clustering has to be handled explicitly. If grouped questions are treated as independent, the standard error is understated, by a factor of about three in the paper's worst reported case. Small evals remain a problem that analysis cannot fix: with a few hundred questions, intervals several points wide are simply what the data support.

Microsoft ExP: a chi-squared test every A/B test must pass before it is analysed

Microsoft Experimentation PlatformDocumented in an engineering article

Microsoft's Experimentation Platform (ExP) team describes its Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) check in a 2020 article. A KDD 2019 paper by authors from Microsoft, Booking.com and Outreach.io gives the test, a numeric example, case studies and a taxonomy of causes. [15][16]

The problem

An A/B test configured to split users 50/50 should finish with nearly equal numbers of users in each group. If the counts differ by more than chance allows, some users are missing from one group for a reason, and the reason is usually related to the change being tested. The groups are then no longer comparable and every metric computed from them is suspect.

The sources describe an MSN experiment that increased the number of cards in an image carousel from 12 to 16. The scorecard showed engagement falling. An SRM warning had fired: the treatment group contained fewer users than configured.

Where this topic fits

This is a hypothesis test used as a gate. It runs on the user counts before any outcome metric is examined. The null hypothesis is that the observed counts were produced by the configured split. A chi-squared goodness-of-fit test turns the gap between observed and expected counts into a p-value, and a failing experiment is sent to diagnosis and not to a ship decision.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Before looking at any metric, count the users that reached the analysis in each variant.
  2. State the null hypothesis: the counts come from the configured allocation, for example half of the users in each variant.
  3. Compute the chi-squared statistic from the observed and expected counts, and from it the p-value.
  4. Compare the p-value with a deliberately strict threshold of 0.0005. The check runs on every experiment, so a conventional 0.05 would raise false alarms on one healthy experiment in twenty.
  5. If the check fails, do not read the scorecard. Look for where users were lost by examining segments, the triggering condition for the analysis and the time at which the mismatch began.
  6. Fix the cause and analyse again. In the carousel case the most engaged treatment users had been classified as bots and removed, and after correcting for that the result reversed.

Why this approach fits

A ratio alone cannot be judged without the sample size, and the test supplies it. A split of 50.2 to 49.8 on 1,000 users (502 against 498) gives a chi-squared statistic of 0.016 and a p-value of 0.90, which is ordinary noise. The same split on 1.6 million users gives a p-value below two in a million. That is the square-root law from this lesson working in the experimenter's favour: with large counts, even a small systematic loss of users is detectable.

The strict threshold reflects the cost structure. The check is applied to every experiment, and each alarm costs an investigation, so the team accepts lower sensitivity to keep false alarms rare.

Limitation or tradeoff

The test detects a mismatch and says nothing about its cause. The sources list causes in assignment, execution, log processing and analysis, and diagnosing one can take longer than the experiment.

Passing the check does not prove the experiment is sound. A fault that removes the same kind of users from both groups leaves the ratio intact. On a small experiment, a threshold of 0.0005 gives little power to detect a moderate mismatch. And the expected counts depend on knowing the configured ratio throughout, which becomes harder when an experiment's allocation is ramped up in stages.

What the sources document

  • The article says that at Microsoft every A/B test must pass the SRM test before its effects are analysed, and that a recent analysis found about 6 % of A/B tests have an SRM. [15]
  • The article says looking at the ratio of users is not sufficient, that a chi-squared test is used, and that the threshold is p-value below 0.0005, chosen conservatively to reduce the likelihood of false positives. [15]
  • The paper gives the example of 821,588 against 815,482 users, a ratio of 50.2 to 49.8, and says the probability of this happening by chance under an expected 50/50 split is less than 1 in 500,000. [16]
  • In the carousel experiment that raised the number of cards from 12 to 16, the treatment group had fewer users than configured because the most engaged treatment users were classified as bots and removed during data processing. Pneumonia risk models that learned asthma lowers the risk of death

Documented in a paper

The problem

The models were meant to support an admission decision: patients at high risk of dying would be admitted to hospital and patients at low risk treated as outpatients. One of the models, a rule-based learner, produced the rule that a history of asthma means lower risk. The rule matched the training data. It was also the opposite of the medical truth, and a model applying it would have sent some of the most vulnerable patients home.

Where this topic fits

This is correlation versus causation at the exact point where it bites: when a predictive model is turned into a decision rule. As a description of patients who were treated as usual, the association is correct. The intended use changes how patients are treated, and treatment is the hidden variable that produced the association.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Train models on records of past pneumonia patients, with death as the target and history, vital signs, lab tests and x-ray findings as features.
  2. Notice in an inspectable model that a history of asthma is associated with a lower probability of death.
  3. Trace the pattern to hospital practice. Asthmatic patients who arrived with pneumonia were usually admitted directly to intensive care, and that care was effective enough to bring their death rate below that of other patients.
  4. Recognise the structure. Asthma led to more aggressive care, the care lowered mortality, and the care is not a feature in the model, so asthma stands in for it.
  5. Follow the consequence of the intended use. Scoring asthmatic patients as low risk would lead to treating them as outpatients, which withdraws the very care that created the low risk in the data.
  6. Prefer models whose terms can be read and corrected. In the 2015 re-analysis the asthma term is found again and can be removed or redrawn, and two more terms with the same suspicious direction are flagged.

Why this approach fits

The data were observational, and the paper points out that it is usually not ethical to randomise the care patients receive in order to collect unbiased data. With randomisation unavailable, the remaining defence is to look at what the model has learned and ask, for each association, what else could have produced it.

The case also shows why accuracy on held-out data offers no protection. The asthma pattern is real in the test set as well, because test patients received the same care, so a model that exploits it scores well. Only the change from predicting to deciding exposes the error.

Limitation or tradeoff

Inspection finds only the associations a human recognises as wrong. The authors say plainly that their models are still based on correlation and that they cannot yet tell which details of the learned shapes come from overfitting, from correlation with other variables, from unmeasured variables or from real effects.

Removing a term is also not a complete repair. Other features correlated with asthma can carry part of the same signal, and a less transparent model may have learned similar patterns that nobody has noticed, which is the reason the original project gave for not deploying its most accurate model.

U.S. Census Bureau: a margin of error published with every survey estimate

U.S. Census BureauDocumented in official docs

The American Community Survey (ACS) is an ongoing sample survey of the United States. Chapter 7 of the Census Bureau's handbook "Understanding and Using American Community Survey Data: What All Data Users Need to Know" explains the error measures published with every estimate and how to test whether two estimates differ. [18][19]

The problem

ACS figures are estimates of what would have been obtained if the entire population, and not only the chosen sample, had been interviewed. Each one differs from the full-population value and from what a different sample would have given. People use the figures to compare places, years and groups, and the Bureau's overview page says the data help inform how trillions of dollars in federal funds are distributed each year. Users need to know which differences between estimates are real.

Where this topic fits

The whole chain from this lesson is delivered as a routine feature of a data product. Sampling produces an estimate. Its standard error is scaled into a margin of error at a fixed confidence level. Users add and subtract the margin to get a confidence interval, and they combine two standard errors into a test statistic to decide whether a difference is statistically significant.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Estimate each figure from the survey sample and publish it with a margin of error at the 90 % confidence level.
  2. Add and subtract the margin of error to get a 90 % confidence interval for the population value.
  3. Recover the standard error by dividing the margin of error by 1.645, the normal critical value for 90 %.
  4. To compare two estimates, divide their difference by the square root of the sum of their squared standard errors.
  5. Compare the absolute value of that statistic with 1.645 for 90 % confidence, 1.960 for 95 % or 2.576 for 99 %. A larger statistic means the difference is statistically significant at that level.
  6. Judge reliability with the coefficient of variation, the standard error as a share of the estimate. For small areas the Bureau combines several years of data, because a larger sample means a smaller standard error.

Why this approach fits

A survey that reaches a fraction of households cannot avoid sampling error, so the honest design is to publish the size of that error next to every number. Scaling the standard error by a fixed multiplier means any user can rebuild the interval or run the test with a calculator, with no access to the underlying responses.

The Indiana and Arizona comparison shows the value of the habit. A gap of 0.3 points looks like a difference in a table, and the test shows it is within what sampling alone would produce.

Limitation or tradeoff

The margin of error covers sampling error only. The handbook says that nonsampling error, from questionnaire problems, processing mistakes or nonresponse, is very difficult to measure directly and is not reflected in these measures.

A 90 % level is more lenient than the 95 % common elsewhere, so one interval in ten is expected to miss. The handbook warns that checking whether two intervals overlap is not a reliable test. Its notes add that the Bureau's testing spreadsheet makes no adjustment for multiple comparisons, and that interval bounds may need truncating at natural limits such as zero.

What the sources document

  • The handbook defines sampling error as the difference between an estimate based on a sample and the value that would be obtained from the entire population, and says the margin of error is the measure of sampling error provided with all published ACS estimates. [18]
  • Margins of error for published ACS estimates are provided at a 90 percent confidence level. The handbook's example is 564,757 one-person households in Colorado with a margin of error of 10,127, giving an interval from 554,630 to 574,884. [18]
  • The standard error is obtained by dividing the margin of error by 1.645, and two estimates are compared by dividing their difference by the square root of the sum of the squared standard errors and comparing the result with 1.645, 1.960 or 2.576. [18]
  • For householders aged 65 or older living alone, Florida (12.6, margin 0.2) against Arizona (10.5, margin 0.3) gives a test value of 9.581, which is significant, while Indiana (10.8, margin 0.2) against Arizona gives 1.369, which is not. [18]

Google TFX data validation: summary statistics on every batch, and a significance test set aside

GoogleDocumented in a paper

The SysML 2019 paper "Data Validation for Machine Learning" by Breck and co-authors describes the data-validation system deployed in TFX, Google's machine learning platform. The open-source library TensorFlow Data Validation (TFDV) documents the same workflow of statistics, schema and anomaly checks. [20][21]

The problem

A machine learning pipeline keeps running when its input data go wrong. The paper's opening example, which it says reflects a real production outage, is a code change that pins one feature to −1 for a slice of serving data. The model keeps predicting, less accurately, and the faulty data are logged and become the next day's training data. The team needed automatic checks that catch such errors in each batch, and that detect when training data and serving data drift apart, without flooding the on-call engineer with alerts.

Where this topic fits

Descriptive statistics are the foundation: a component computes summary statistics for every batch, and all validation runs on those summaries and not on the raw rows. For comparing two distributions the team considered a hypothesis test and set it aside, because at their data volumes a test flags differences that do not matter. They replaced the p-value with a direct measure of how much the distribution moved.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. For each batch of training or serving data, compute per-feature descriptive statistics that show which features are present and the shape of their value distributions.
  2. Derive a schema from the statistics, recording expected types, presence and allowed values, and have the owning team review it.
  3. Validate the statistics of each new batch against the schema and alert on anomalies such as a missing feature or an unexpected value.
  4. To detect skew or drift, compare a feature's distribution in the training data with its distribution in the serving data, or in the previous day's batch.
  5. Measure the change with the L-infinity distance, the largest change in probability for any single value, and alert when it exceeds a threshold the team has set, such as one percentage point.
  6. Start the investigation from the value that changed most, which the alert reports.

Why this approach fits

Summary statistics are small and cheap to compare, so a batch can be re-validated against a revised schema almost instantly, and the same summaries serve both the single-batch checks and the comparisons between batches.

Setting the test aside follows from the standard-error formula. With a hundred million rows the standard error of any proportion is tiny, so a goodness-of-fit test answers "do these distributions differ at all?", and for two days of real traffic the answer is always yes. The operational question is "do they differ enough to act on?", which a distance and a threshold answer directly, in units a team can reason about.

Limitation or tradeoff

The threshold is a human judgement with no statistical guarantee attached, so it has to be tuned per feature. The TFDV guide notes that the L-infinity comparison detects skew for categorical features only, and that numeric features need a Jensen-Shannon divergence threshold.

Summaries of one feature at a time can miss an error confined to a small slice of the data or a change in the relationship between features. And the reasoning reverses on small batches: there a distance estimate is noisy, and the sampling error that the large-data argument dismissed has to be accounted for again.

  • The paper says the system is deployed in production as an integral part of TFX at Google and that hundreds of product teams use it to monitor and validate several petabytes of production data per day. [20]
  • The system's Data Analyzer computes a predefined set of data statistics sufficient for validation, and the Data Validator relies on these per-batch statistics so that validation is lightweight and a batch can be revalidated against an updated schema almost instantly. [20]
  • The authors found statistical tests such as the chi-squared test too sensitive for the typical scale of data. In their experiment, replacing 10 thousand of 100 million points drawn from N(0,1) with points from N(0,2) led a chi-squared test at a 1 % threshold to fire in 7 of 10 trials. [20]

4Common mistakes

  1. Declaring a winner from two scores without a sample size or an interval.

    Why it causes problems
    A difference smaller than the sampling error is as likely to reverse as to hold on the next test set. On 300 questions an accuracy near 80 % has a 95 % margin of about ±4.5 points, so a 2-point lead between two separately scored models means almost nothing, and work gets prioritised around noise.
    How to recognise it
    A results table with scores to one decimal place and no n, no standard error and no interval. Rerunning with a different random seed or a different test split changes the ranking.
    How to fix it
    Report n and the standard error with every score. For a comparison, compute the interval of the difference, paired where possible, and name a winner only if the interval excludes zero by an amount that matters.

  2. Reading a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true, or that the result is due to chance.

    Why it causes problems
    A p-value is calculated on the assumption that the null hypothesis holds, so it cannot also be the probability of that assumption. Treating p = 0.03 as "97 % sure the new model is better" overstates the evidence, most of all when real improvements are rare among the ideas being tried, because then a large share of the significant results are false positives.
    How to recognise it
    Sentences such as "there is only a 3 % chance this is a fluke" or "we are 97 % confident it works" in a write-up. The American Statistical Association's second principle says p-values measure neither quantity.
    How to fix it
    Word it as "if there were no real difference, a result this large would turn up about 3 % of the time". Then give the estimated effect and its confidence interval, and weigh them against what was known beforehand.

  3. Treating a non-significant result as proof that there is no difference.

    Why it causes problems
    Failing to reject the null hypothesis may only mean the sample was too small. A regression that costs two points of accuracy passes unnoticed through a 100-example check whose margin of error is about ±8 points, and a real improvement gets abandoned for the same reason.
    How to recognise it
    A conclusion of "no effect" or "no regression" next to a wide confidence interval that contains both zero and differences large enough to matter.
    How to fix it
    Read the interval and not only the verdict. If it still contains effects you would care about, the result is inconclusive and more data are needed. Before running a check, work out the smallest difference it is able to detect.

  4. Using a formula for independent observations on data that are paired or grouped.

    Why it causes problems
    Ignoring pairing throws away precision: in the worked example it turns p = 0.005 into p = 0.065. Ignoring grouping fails in the dangerous direction. Treating many rows per user, several questions per passage or overlapping cross-validation folds as independent makes the standard error too small and produces confident claims that do not replicate.
    How to recognise it
    The n in the formula counts rows, while the things that were sampled independently are users, documents or passages. Or two models were scored on the same items and compared with a two-sample test.
    How to fix it
    Identify the independent unit first. For paired data, test the per-item differences. For grouped data, reduce to one value per group or use clustered standard errors, and when bootstrapping resample whole groups.

  5. Running many comparisons, or checking results repeatedly, and reporting the one that reached significance.

    Why it causes problems
    Each extra look or comparison is another chance for noise to cross the threshold. With 20 independent tests and no real effects there is a 64 % chance that at least one shows p below 0.05, so a significant result picked from many is weak evidence.
    How to recognise it
    A reported win on a segment, metric or variant that was not named before the analysis, an experiment stopped on the day it first looked significant, or a "best" model chosen and scored on the same test set.
    How to fix it
    Name the primary metric, the comparison and the sample size before looking. Count every test that was run and tighten the threshold, for example by dividing α by the number of tests. Confirm surprising findings on fresh data.

  6. Interpreting a correlation, coefficient or feature importance as the effect of changing that feature.

    Why it causes problems
    The association may come from a confounder or from reverse causation, so acting on it can achieve nothing or do harm. The pneumonia models learned that asthma predicts survival because asthmatic patients received intensive care, and using that pattern to send them home would have removed the cause of their survival.
    How to recognise it
    A recommendation of the form "users who do X have higher Y, so make everyone do X" drawn from observational data. Ask who chose X, and whether that choice could be related to Y.
    How to fix it
    List the alternative explanations: reverse causation, a common cause and selection. Run a randomised experiment where you can. Where you cannot, say "associated with", adjust for known confounders and treat the result as a hypothesis.

The interval is 3.5±2.201×0.8123.5 \pm 2.201 \times 0.8123.5±2.201×0.812, from 1.71 to 5.29. With only 12 values the t value 2.201 is the right multiplier; 1.96 would give an interval that is too narrow.

Part 3. t=3.5/0.812=4.31t = 3.5 / 0.812 = 4.31t=3.5/0.812=4.31, which exceeds 2.201, so the difference is significant at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05. The t distribution with 11 degrees of freedom puts the two-sided p-value at about 0.001.

Part 4. If fine-tuning made no difference, each document's difference would be as likely to carry either sign, so all 4,096 sign patterns are equally likely. The sizes of the differences add up to 46, which is the largest total any pattern can reach. A pattern totals 42 or more only if the differences it counts as negative have sizes adding up to at most 2: none of them (1 pattern), one of the three differences of size 1 (3 patterns), two of those (3 patterns), or one of the two differences of size 2 (2 patterns). That makes 9 patterns, and their 9 mirror images total −42 or less. The exact p-value is 18/4096=0.004418 / 4096 = 0.004418/4096=0.0044. It needs no assumption that the differences are normally distributed, and it leads to the same conclusion as the t test.

Part 5. The unpaired standard error is 12.472/12+11.482/12=4.89\sqrt{12.47^2 / 12 + 11.48^2 / 12} = 4.8912.472/12+11.482/12​=4.89, six times the paired one, and t=3.5/4.89=0.72t = 3.5 / 4.89 = 0.72t=3.5/4.89=0.72. That analysis mixes the spread between documents (about 12 points) into the noise. The pairing removes it: the columns have a correlation of 0.98, so almost all of the variation is shared and cancels in the differences. The unpaired formula is also simply wrong here, because it assumes two independent samples.

Part 6. On documents like these and with this reviewer, the fine-tuned model scores higher by about 3.5 points, plausibly between 1.7 and 5.3. Twelve documents and one reviewer cannot show that the gain holds for other kinds of document, that another reviewer would agree, or that 3.5 points is large enough to matter to readers.

Exercise 2

Separate a correlation from an effect, then size and read the experiment

AnalysisIntermediateAbout 45 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

The numbers are invented for practice. A product team adds an AI summary feature to a note-taking app. A month later the dashboard shows:

GroupUsersRetained after 30 days
Used the summary feature8,00062 %
Did not use it32,00045 %

The product manager wants to report that the feature raises retention by 17 points.

  1. Compute the standard error of the 17-point difference and its 95 % confidence interval, treating the two groups as independent samples. Is the difference statistically significant?
  2. Explain why the answer to part 1 does not justify the claim. Give three specific alternative explanations for the gap.
  3. Design a randomised experiment to measure the feature's effect. Name the unit of randomisation, the two groups and the metric. Then work out how many users each group needs for the 95 % margin of error on the difference in retention to be ±1 percentage point, assuming retention near 50 %.
  4. The experiment runs with 20,000 users per group. Retention is 9,960 of 20,000 in the control group and 10,180 of 20,000 in the group that was given the feature. Compute the difference, its standard error, the z statistic and the 95 % interval, and say what the team should report. A z of 2.20 corresponds to a two-sided p-value of 0.028.
  5. The analyst also compared 20 secondary metrics and found one with p = 0.03. How should that result be treated?

A calculator or a spreadsheet is enough.

Requirements

  • Use the standard error of a difference between two independent proportions, the square root of p1(1 − p1)/n1 + p2(1 − p2)/n2.
  • Give three alternative explanations that are specific to this scenario and cover a common cause, reverse causation and selection.
  • Show the sample-size calculation and not only its answer.
  • Report the experiment's result as an estimate with an interval and compare it with the 17 points on the dashboard.
  • Put a number on the multiple-comparison problem in part 5.

Expected behaviour

Part 1: the standard error is about 0.0061, the interval is 15.8 to 18.2 points and z is about 28, so the gap is far beyond sampling noise. Part 2: significance rules out noise and nothing else. Users chose whether to use the feature, so engagement, tenure, plan or platform can explain the gap, and users who stay longer have more time to try the feature. Part 3: about 19,200 users per group. Part 4: a difference of 1.1 points with a standard error of 0.0050, z = 2.20, p = 0.028 and an interval from 0.1 to 2.1 points. The feature probably helps a little, and nearly all of the 17-point gap was not caused by it. Part 5: with 20 tests about one false positive is expected and the chance of at least one is 64 %, so the finding is a lead to confirm and not a result.

Hint 1

Significance tells you the gap is not sampling noise. Ask a different question. Who decided which users ended up in each row of the dashboard?

Hint 2

For the sample size, set 1.96 × √(2 × 0.25 / n) equal to 0.01 and solve for n.

Hint 3

Compare the experiment's whole interval, not only its centre, with the 17 points from the observational data.

Part 2. A test only answers "could sampling variation alone produce this gap?" It does not say why the groups differ. Here the users sorted themselves into the groups, so the comparison is observational:

  • Common cause. Heavy, engaged or long-tenured users are more likely to try a new feature and more likely to stay regardless of it. Engagement drives both columns.
  • Reverse causation. A user who stays for 30 days has 30 days in which to try the feature, while one who leaves in week one has seven. Staying causes usage as much as usage causes staying.
  • Selection. If the feature is shown only on some platforms or plans, or only once a user has enough notes, the "used it" row is a different kind of customer from the start.

Part 3. Randomise by user, because retention is measured per user and one person should not see both versions. Treatment: the feature is available. Control: it is not. Metric: the share of users active 30 days after assignment, chosen before the experiment starts. Random assignment makes the groups alike on engagement, tenure, platform and everything else, measured or not.

so about 19,200 users per group. A ±2 point margin would need a quarter of that, about 4,800.

Part 4. Control retention is 9960/20000=0.4989960 / 20000 = 0.4989960/20000=0.498 and treatment is 10180/20000=0.50910180 / 20000 = 0.50910180/20000=0.509, a difference of 0.011.

The report should say that the feature raises 30-day retention by about 1.1 points, with an interval from 0.1 to 2.1 points. That is a real but small effect, estimated imprecisely. It is one fifteenth of the 17 points on the dashboard, and the remaining gap came from who chooses to use the feature. Whether one point justifies the feature's cost is a product decision that the p-value does not make.

Part 5. At a threshold of 0.05, 20 comparisons with no real effects produce one false positive on average, and the chance of at least one is 1−0.9520=0.641 - 0.95^{20} = 0.641−0.9520=0.64.
