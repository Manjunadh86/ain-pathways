Hierarchical and Density-Based Clustering

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of nested groups by repeatedly merging the two closest clusters, and you choose how many groups to keep by cutting the tree. Density-based clustering (DBSCAN, HDBSCAN) instead grows clusters through crowded regions of space and labels points in sparse regions as noise.

1Overview

Why it matters

k-means assumes you know the number of groups, that each group is a compact blob around one centre, and that every point belongs to some group. Real data breaks all three. GPS pings follow roads, customer segments nest inside larger segments, and a pile of text embeddings contains many one-off documents that belong to no theme. Hierarchical clustering gives you the whole family of answers from one run, from every point alone to everything together, so you can look before deciding how many groups there are. Density-based methods find groups of any shape and say "noise" about points that fit nowhere, which matters whenever a forced assignment would pollute the groups.

The price is a different set of choices. You pick a linkage rule and a cut height, or a radius and a neighbour count, and a wrong value quietly produces one giant cluster or a result that is mostly noise. All of these methods work from distances, so unscaled features distort them as they distort k-means. Analysts building segments, engineers deduplicating records or mapping spatial data, and anyone preparing embeddings for topic discovery need to read these outputs critically rather than accept whatever a library returns.

Key terms

Agglomerative clustering
The bottom-up form of hierarchical clustering. Every point starts as its own cluster and the two closest clusters are merged repeatedly until one cluster remains.
Linkage
The rule that turns distances between points into a distance between two clusters, such as the smallest (single), the largest (complete), the average, or Ward's variance criterion.
Dendrogram
A tree diagram of the merge history in which the height of each join is the distance at which the two clusters were merged.
Cut height
A distance threshold applied to a dendrogram. Cutting there keeps every merge below it and yields the flat clusters that exist at that level.
eps (epsilon)
The radius of the neighbourhood that DBSCAN examines around each point, in the same units as the features.
min_samples
The number of points, counting the point itself, that must lie within eps of a point for scikit-learn to call it a core point.
Core point
A point with at least min_samples points (itself included) within eps. DBSCAN clusters grow outward from core points.
Border point
A point that is not core but lies within eps of a core point. It joins that core point's cluster and cannot extend it.
Noise point
A point that is neither core nor border. DBSCAN and HDBSCAN in scikit-learn give it the label -1.
Mutual reachability distance
HDBSCAN's distance between two points, the largest of their two core distances and their ordinary distance. A core distance is the distance to a point's min_samples-th nearest neighbour, counting the point itself.

2Explanation

Introduction

You already know the k-means recipe from the previous lesson: choose k, place k centres, assign every point to its nearest centre, move the centres, and repeat. It is fast and works well when the groups are compact blobs of similar size. It also carries three assumptions that are easy to forget. You know k in advance. A group is whatever lies nearest to one centre, so groups are convex blobs. Every point belongs to some group.

The picture below breaks all three at once. Forty points lie on two interlocking half circles. Anyone looking at them sees two groups, but k-means with k = 2 can only cut the plane with a straight line, and that line slices through both crescents. The points are coloured by what DBSCAN finds instead, which is exactly the two crescents.

This lesson covers two families of methods that drop those assumptions in different ways. Hierarchical clustering drops "you know k". In its common bottom-up form, agglomerative clustering, every point starts as its own cluster and the two closest clusters are merged, again and again, until one cluster remains. The record of merges is a tree called a dendrogram, and choosing the number of clusters becomes choosing where to cut the tree. Density-based clustering drops "groups are convex" and "everything belongs somewhere". It treats a cluster as a crowded region separated from other crowded regions by emptier space, and it calls the points in emptier space noise. DBSCAN does this with one radius and one count. HDBSCAN repeats the idea at every radius and keeps the clusters that persist.

Both families work from distances between points, so features need comparable scales first (see feature engineering and scaling). The rest of the lesson explains how merges are scored, how a dendrogram is read, how DBSCAN decides which points are core, border or noise, what HDBSCAN adds, and when each method beats k-means. A worked example then traces all three by hand on eight points.

How it works

Agglomerative clustering: merge the closest pair, record the height

The algorithm has no randomness and no k.

  1. Start with nnn clusters, one per point.
  2. Find the two clusters with the smallest distance between them and merge them. Record which two clusters joined, the distance (the height of the merge) and the new size.
  3. Compute the distance from the new cluster to every other cluster.
  4. Repeat from step 2 until one cluster is left, after n−1n-1n−1 merges.

The one real design decision is step 3. A linkage rule turns distances between points, written d(a,b)d(a,b)d(a,b), into a distance between clusters AAA and BBB:

dsingle=min⁡a∈A, b∈Bd(a,b),dcomplete=max⁡a∈A, b∈Bd(a,b),daverage=1∣A∣ ∣B∣∑a∈A∑b∈Bd(a,b)d_{\text{single}} = \min_{a \in A,\, b \in B} d(a,b), \qquad d_{\text{complete}} = \max_{a \in A,\, b \in B} d(a,b), \qquad d_{\text{average}} = \frac{1}{|A|\,|B|} \sum_{a \in A} \sum_{b \in B} d(a,b)dsingle​=a∈A,b∈Bmin​d(a,b),dcomplete​=a∈A,b∈Bmax​d(a,b),daverage​=∣A∣∣B∣1​a∈A∑​b∈B∑​d(a,b)

Ward linkage instead merges the pair whose union raises the total within-cluster sum of squared distances to the centroids by the least. For Euclidean distance that rise is Δ=∣A∣ ∣B∣∣A∣+∣B∣∥aˉ−bˉ∥2\Delta = \frac{|A|\,|B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert \bar{a}-\bar{b}\rVert^2Δ=∣A∣+∣B∣∣A∣∣B∣​∥aˉ−bˉ∥2, with aˉ\bar aaˉ and bˉ\bar bbˉ the centroids, and the merge height SciPy reports is 2Δ\sqrt{2\Delta}2Δ​. The scikit-learn guide (opens in a new tab) calls Ward a variance-minimising approach similar to the k-means objective. The linkage decides which cluster shapes the method prefers:

  • Single follows chains of nearby points, so it can trace elongated clusters, but the guide notes it is not robust to noisy data. In practice one stray point can bridge two clusters or end up as a cluster of its own. The guide also names it the worst strategy for the "rich get richer" effect that gives uneven cluster sizes.
  • Complete prefers compact clusters of similar diameter, because the farthest pair decides. A single distant point can stop two otherwise close groups from merging.
  • Average sits between the two.
  • Ward gives the most regular cluster sizes according to the guide, and scikit-learn accepts only Euclidean distance with it. SciPy likewise defines ward, centroid and median only for Euclidean distance.

Reading a dendrogram and cutting it

A dendrogram draws every merge as a U-shaped link. The horizontal bar of the U sits at the merge height, and in SciPy's words the length of the two legs represents the distance between the child clusters. Leaves are the points. A long vertical stretch with no merges means the clusters stayed far apart over that range of distances, which makes it a natural place to cut. Cutting at height ttt keeps every merge at or below ttt and gives flat cluster labels:

python
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster

Z = linkage(X, method="average")                   # (n-1) x 4: child a, child b, height, size
labels = fcluster(Z, t=3.0, criterion="distance")  # cut the tree at height 3.0

Row iii of Z merges the clusters named in its first two columns into a new cluster numbered n+in+in+i; the third column is the height and the fourth the number of original points. In scikit-learn the same cut is AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=None, distance_threshold=3.0, linkage="average"), where n_clusters must be None when a threshold is given and the default linkage is Ward. The two cuts differ only for a merge exactly at the threshold, which fcluster keeps and scikit-learn does not perform. Without a connectivity constraint every step considers every possible merge, which the guide calls computationally expensive. Dendrograms are also most useful for small samples; with thousands of leaves they stop being readable.

DBSCAN: clusters as connected crowds

DBSCAN takes two parameters, a radius eps and a count min_samples. The neighbourhood of a point ppp is every point within eps of it, including ppp itself:

Nε(p)={ q:d(p,q)≤ε }N_\varepsilon(p) = \{\, q : d(p,q) \le \varepsilon \,\}Nε​(p)={q:d(p,q)≤ε}

Each point then gets one of three roles. It is a core point if ∣Nε(p)∣≥|N_\varepsilon(p)| \ge∣Nε​(p)∣≥ min_samples. It is a border point if it is not core but lies in the neighbourhood of a core point. Otherwise it is noise. A cluster grows from an unvisited core point: give it a label, add its neighbours, and for every core point added, add its neighbours too. Border points are absorbed but never expanded from. Points never reached keep the label -1. Two core points land in the same cluster exactly when a chain of core points, each within eps of the next, joins them. That chain is why a cluster can bend around a crescent, and why the number of clusters is an output of the algorithm instead of an input.

Two caveats come from the scikit-learn guide. A border point that two clusters can both reach may end up in either one, so those labels can change with the order of the data. And eps is "crucial to choose appropriately": too small and most points become noise, too large and clusters merge until one remains. min_samples sets the tolerance for noise and is worth raising on large, noisy data.

The original paper proposes a heuristic for eps called the sorted k-distance graph: compute each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour, sort the values, and look for the knee. The paper sorts them in descending order and takes the first "valley" as the threshold, with the larger values to its left counted as noise. Sorted ascending, as in the code below, that is the knee where the curve starts to climb steeply. The authors say the valley is hard to find automatically and propose picking it by eye. With scikit-learn's convention that the point counts itself, a point is core exactly when its (min_samples - 1)-th nearest other point lies within eps, so that is the distance to plot. The paper suggests min_samples of 4 for two-dimensional data and reports that k-distance graphs for larger k look much the same.

python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN

min_samples = 4
dists, _ = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=min_samples).fit(X).kneighbors(X)
k_dist = np.sort(dists[:, -1])       # column 0 is the point itself, at distance 0
eps = 0.5                            # replace with the knee of k_dist
labels = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=min_samples).fit_predict(X)   # -1 marks noise

HDBSCAN: DBSCAN over every radius

DBSCAN applies one density threshold to the whole dataset. The scikit-learn guide says that assumption makes it struggle when clusters have different densities. HDBSCAN removes eps in five steps.

  1. Core distance. dc(x)d_c(x)dc​(x) is the distance from xxx to its min_samples-th nearest neighbour, counting xxx itself. It is small in dense regions and large in sparse ones.
  2. Mutual reachability distance. dm(a,b)=max⁡{dc(a), dc(b), d(a,b)}d_m(a,b)=\max\{d_c(a),\,d_c(b),\,d(a,b)\}dm​(a,b)=max{dc​(a),dc​(b),d(a,b)}. Dense points keep their mutual distances, while sparse points are pushed away from everything by at least their own core distance, so a stray point cannot act as a bridge.
  3. Hierarchy. Build the minimum spanning tree on dmd_mdm​ and remove its edges from heaviest to lightest, which is single linkage on dmd_mdm​. Every level is a DBSCAN-style clustering for one radius, so the tree holds the results for all radii at once.
  4. Condense. When removing an edge splits a cluster and one side has fewer than min_cluster_size points, those points are treated as falling out of the parent, not as a new cluster.
  5. Select. With λ=1/d\lambda = 1/dλ=1/d as the density level, a cluster's stability is ∑p(λp−λbirth)\sum_p (\lambda_p - \lambda_{\text{birth}})∑p​(λp​−λbirth​) over its points, where λbirth\lambda_{\text{birth}}λbirth​ is the level at which the cluster appeared and λp\lambda_pλp​ the level at which point ppp left it. The default eom method keeps the non-overlapping clusters with the greatest total stability, which favours clusters that persist over a wide range of radii. The leaf method keeps the finest clusters instead. Points outside every selected cluster are noise, and each clustered point gets a membership strength in [0,1][0,1][0,1].

The main parameters are min_cluster_size (the smallest group worth calling a cluster) and min_samples (how conservative the density estimate is: larger values declare more points noise). HDBSCAN has been in scikit-learn since version 1.3. Its min_samples counts the point itself, unlike the separate hdbscan package, so add 1 when copying settings from that package.

python
from sklearn.cluster import HDBSCAN

hdb = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=15).fit(X)
hdb.labels_          # -1 marks noise
hdb.probabilities_   # membership strength, 0 for noise

Choosing between them

The diagram condenses the decision. Whichever method you pick, judge the result with plots, cluster sizes, the share of noise, and whether the clusters survive moving the parameters through a range. Internal scores such as the silhouette coefficient lean toward convex shapes: the scikit-learn guide notes the silhouette is higher for convex clusters than for density-based ones, so a correct density-based result can score lower than a wrong k-means one (the edge cases give numbers for the crescents).

Reproduce it

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, HDBSCAN

names = "ABCDEFGH"
X = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [2.0, 1.2], [1.4, 2.1], [2.9, 2.6],
              [6.0, 5.0], [7.1, 5.2], [6.3, 6.1], [8.6, 0.6]])

def groups(labels):
    found = {}
    for name, lab in zip(names, labels):
        found.setdefault(int(lab), []).append(name)
    return sorted(found.values())

Z = linkage(X, method="average")
print([round(float(h), 4) for h in Z[:, 2]])
print(groups(fcluster(Z, t=3.0, criterion="distance")))
print(groups(DBSCAN(eps=1.6, min_samples=3).fit(X).labels_))
print(groups(HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=3, min_samples=3).fit(X).labels_))

Edge cases and limitations

Single linkage chases outliers and builds bridges
Because single linkage uses the closest pair, one stray point decides the top of the tree. In the worked example it makes the two-cluster cut {A..G} and {H}. A line of points spaced just under the gap between two groups can fuse them. Adding the point (4.4, 3.7) to the worked example lowers the height at which single linkage joins the groups from 3.92 to 2.06. Use complete, average or Ward when the data are noisy, and keep single linkage for clean, elongated structures.
Scale and dimension decide what eps means
eps is a distance in feature units, so unscaled features make it meaningless: a column in thousands swamps a column in fractions. In hundreds of dimensions the nearest and farthest neighbours of a point end up at similar distances, which leaves no radius that separates crowded from empty regions. Scale first, and reduce dimensions before clustering, as BERTopic does by projecting embeddings with UMAP before HDBSCAN.
min_samples counts the point itself
In scikit-learn the neighbourhood includes the point, so min_samples=2 means one other point is enough. The class documentation says so, and its example DBSCAN(eps=3, min_samples=2) on [[1,2],[2,2],[2,3],[8,7],[8,8],[25,80]] labels the two points near (8, 7.5) as a cluster, which would be impossible if the point did not count. (The user guide's prose says "other samples", so trust the class documentation.) The separate hdbscan package counts differently, so add 1 when moving its settings to scikit-learn. Border points that two clusters can reach take the label of the cluster built first, so shuffling the rows can move them.
One eps cannot fit clusters of different density
In the worked example point D sits farther from its neighbours than any other point in its group. With eps = 1.5 it is noise, with eps = 1.6 it is a border point, and at 3.92 the groups merge. A real dataset with a tight cluster and a loose one has no eps that serves both: a value that keeps the loose cluster whole fuses the tight cluster with its neighbours, and a value that separates the tight ones turns the loose cluster into noise. That is the case for HDBSCAN.
Noise is a label, not a cluster, and it can be large
DBSCAN and HDBSCAN return -1 for noise, and a cautious min_samples can make that a large share of the data. Code that treats -1 as one more cluster, or drops those rows silently, gives wrong counts and biased segments. Report the noise share, decide what happens to those points, and note that BERTopic offers reduce_outliers to reassign them when coverage matters more than purity.
Internal scores favour convex clusters
The silhouette coefficient is higher for convex clusters than for density-based ones, as the scikit-learn guide warns. On the two crescents the correct DBSCAN clustering scores about 0.28, while the k-means split that cuts through both crescents scores about 0.47. Do not rank methods by silhouette. Compare with plots, domain checks, and stability when parameters or samples change.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The groups are not round blobs (rings, crescents, bands along roads or rivers) and you do not know how many there are, which is where DBSCAN and HDBSCAN replace k-means.
  • Some points should stay unassigned, such as GPS jitter, one-off documents or sensor glitches, instead of being forced into the nearest group and distorting it.
  • You want to see nested structure or let a domain expert choose the granularity. Run agglomerative clustering once, draw the dendrogram and try several cut heights.
  • Clusters differ in density, such as a tight cluster beside a loose one, and a single eps would fail. Use HDBSCAN.
  • The question is about similarity of whole profiles, as with genes or customers, and an ordered tree beside a heat map is the deliverable.

Think twice when

  • You need exactly k groups of similar size, for example five sales territories. Use k-means or a constrained variant.
  • Features are high-dimensional and unreduced, so distances barely differ between near and far points. Reduce dimensions first, or use embeddings designed for similarity.
  • The data are an even cloud with no density structure. DBSCAN then returns one cluster or all noise, and agglomerative clustering returns an arbitrary cut.
  • You must label new points quickly with a fitted model. scikit-learn's DBSCAN and AgglomerativeClustering have no predict method for unseen points.

A simpler alternative. k-means with a sensible k, or a plain scatter plot and a sort on one feature, is often enough when the groups are blob-like and well separated. Move to hierarchical or density-based methods when a plot shows non-convex shapes, when you need noise labels, or when the number of groups is the unknown you are trying to learn.

Key takeaways

  • Agglomerative clustering merges the two closest clusters again and again and records every merge; the linkage rule (single, complete, average or Ward) defines "closest", so it also decides which cluster shapes the method prefers.
  • A dendrogram's heights are merge distances, so a long vertical gap with no merges marks a natural place to cut, and the cut height replaces k as the parameter.
  • DBSCAN labels each point core, border or noise from two numbers, eps and min_samples, and grows clusters along chains of core points, so clusters can take any shape and their number is an output.
  • One global eps cannot suit clusters of different density; HDBSCAN replaces it by core distances and a hierarchy over all radii, and keeps the clusters that persist, steered mainly by min_cluster_size and min_samples.
  • Choose these methods over k-means when shapes are not convex, k is unknown or outliers must stay out, and always check the noise share and the stability of the result, because a poor parameter can give one giant cluster or mostly noise.

Eisen et al. (1998): hierarchical clustering of yeast gene expression

Eisen, Spellman, Brown and Botstein (PNAS, 1998)Documented in a paper

The 1998 PNAS paper "Cluster analysis and display of genome-wide expression patterns" describes a system that clusters genes by the similarity of their expression across DNA microarray experiments and draws the result as a colour table ordered by the tree. The full text is available on PubMed Central. [11]

The problem

A microarray measures thousands of genes at once, so across an experiment series every gene is a long profile of numbers. Biologists want to know which genes behave alike, because genes working in the same process tend to be switched on and off together. A flat list of thousands of profiles cannot be read, and nobody knows in advance how many groups of co-expressed genes exist or how they nest. The paper's yeast analysis covers 2,467 genes with a functional annotation, measured over several time courses.

Where this topic fits

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering is the whole method. The input is a table of log expression ratios, one row per gene. The similarity measure is a correlation between profiles (in the paper's examples the uncentred form, with no change from the reference as the baseline), the merge rule is average linkage, and the output is a tree. The tree is used twice: to read clusters at any granularity, and to reorder the rows of the table so that similar genes sit next to each other.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Arrange the data as a matrix with one row per gene and one column per measurement, holding log ratios of expression against a reference.
  2. Score every pair of genes with a correlation coefficient of their profiles. The authors say it captures similarity in shape and puts no emphasis on the magnitude of the changes, and in their examples the baseline is no change from the reference.
  3. Find the most similar pair, join them in a node, and give the node a profile computed by averaging its members, weighted by how many genes each side contains, which is the average-linkage idea.
  4. Replace the pair by the node, update the similarities, and repeat until one node remains, which takes n - 1 joins for n genes and yields the full tree.
  5. Use the tree to order the rows so that genes with similar patterns are adjacent, then draw the table with colour cells (red for increased, green for decreased, black for unchanged) beside the tree.
  6. Read clusters as contiguous blocks and compare the known functions of the genes in each block.

Why this approach fits

The number of groups is unknown and the groups nest: a block of translation-related genes contains smaller blocks. A tree shows all those levels at once, where a flat partition would force one choice. Correlation matches the biologist's notion of "co-expressed", and the tree order turns a table of thousands of rows into something a person can scan.

Limitation or tradeoff

A hierarchy can be built from any data, so a block that looks tight needs support beyond the picture. The authors checked this by randomizing the human data in three ways and clustering again, and no similar structure appeared. Neighbouring leaves from different branches are not necessarily similar, because many leaf orderings are consistent with the same tree. A full hierarchy over tens of thousands of genes also needs all pairwise similarities, so it grows quickly with the number of genes.

What the sources document

  • The authors cluster genome-wide expression data from DNA microarrays with a hierarchical method based closely on average linkage, in which each joined pair becomes a node whose profile is the average of its members. [11]
  • The authors say the standard correlation coefficient, the dot product of two normalised vectors, conforms well to the intuitive biological notion of co-expression because it captures similarity in shape but places no emphasis on magnitude. Their Methods set the offset to 0 in all examples, with 0 meaning no change from the reference. [11]
  • The computed trees are used to order genes in the data table so that genes with similar expression patterns are adjacent, and the table is colour coded with red for increased ratios, green for decreased and black for unchanged. [11]
  • Figure 2 clusters 2,467 yeast genes that have a functional annotation in the Saccharomyces Genome Database, and the text singles out a cluster of 126 genes strongly down-regulated in response to stress, dominated by 112 ribosomal-protein genes plus other proteins involved in translation. [11]
  • To check that the structure is biological and not an artefact of the clustering procedure, the authors randomized the human growth-response data in three different ways and clustered it with the same procedure, and no similar structure resulted. [11]

dedupe: grouping duplicate records with hierarchical clustering

dedupe (open-source Python library)Documented in official docs

The documentation of the dedupe library explains how it finds records that describe the same real-world entity. A classifier scores pairs of records, and a hierarchical clustering step then turns those scores into groups. The pages used here are the "Grouping duplicates" chapter and the API entry for `partition`. [12][13]

The problem

A table can hold the same customer, company or address many times with typos and abbreviations. A model can estimate the probability that two records are duplicates, but those probabilities do not combine consistently: record A may look 60 percent like B and B 60 percent like C while A and C look far less alike. Someone still has to decide which records form one entity, without knowing how many entities there are.

Where this topic fits

Agglomerative clustering is the last stage of the pipeline. It takes the pairwise scores, merges the most similar records first, and a threshold on the resulting tree decides which merges are accepted. The threshold is the knob that trades precision against recall.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Score pairs of records with the trained model, which gives each pair a probability of being duplicates.
  2. Treat the scores as similarities between records, because probabilities alone cannot say which records belong together.
  3. Run hierarchical clustering with centroid linkage on the scored records, so the most similar records and clusters merge first.
  4. Accept a merge into a cluster only while the cluster's cophenetic similarity stays above the threshold. The default is 0.5.
  5. Return each cluster as a tuple of record ids plus a confidence score per record.
  6. Tune the threshold, lowering it to find more duplicates (recall) or raising it to make groups stricter (precision).

Why this approach fits

The number of real entities is large and unknown, so a method that needs k is a poor fit, while a cut height maps directly to a precision and recall trade-off that a data team can choose. A hierarchy also gives a repeatable rule for turning non-transitive pairwise scores into one grouping.

Limitation or tradeoff

One threshold applies to the whole dataset, so a setting that suits common names may be too loose or too strict for rare ones. The groups can be no better than the pairwise scores, and a wrong early merge cannot be undone later in the tree. The API page also says partition should only be used for small to moderately sized datasets, and that larger data needs you to generate your own pairs and score them.

What the sources document

  • Dedupe first estimates the probability that pairs of records are duplicates and then clusters the records, because those pairwise probabilities are not transitive; the page's example has two 60 percent matches whose third pair is only 36 percent. [12]
  • The grouping uses hierarchical clustering with centroid linkage, and the outcome depends on a threshold on group membership; different thresholds give very different groups. [12]
  • `partition(data, threshold=0.5)` puts records in a cluster only if the cluster's cophenetic similarity is greater than the threshold; lowering it increases recall and raising it increases precision. [13]
  • The result is a list of tuples, each holding record ids and a confidence score between 0 and 1 per record that expresses how similar the record is to the others in its cluster. [13]
  • The API page says `partition` should only be used for small to moderately sized datasets, and that for larger data you may need to generate your own pairs of records and feed them to `score()`. [13]

PostGIS ST_ClusterDBSCAN: clustering map features inside the database

PostGISDocumented in official docs

The PostGIS manual documents `ST_ClusterDBSCAN`, a window function available since version 2.3.0 that runs DBSCAN over a column of geometries and returns a cluster id for every row. [14]

The problem

Spatial teams keep building footprints, parcels and points in a PostGIS database and want to group features that lie near each other into zones, while leaving isolated features alone. They do not know how many zones exist, the zones are not round, and moving the rows to a separate tool just to cluster them adds a step and a second copy of the data.

Where this topic fits

DBSCAN is the function itself. The distance threshold eps is in the units of the geometry's spatial reference system and minpoints is the neighbour count. The output column feeds ordinary SQL, such as GROUP BY with ST_Collect, to turn cluster ids into zone geometries.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Choose the geometry column and a distance threshold in the units of its spatial reference system, using a projected system when distances should mean metres or feet.
  2. Call `ST_ClusterDBSCAN(geom, eps, minpoints) OVER ()` so that the window covers the whole result set, which gives each row an integer cluster id.
  3. The function treats a geometry within eps of at least minpoints geometries as core and one within eps of a core geometry as border. Everything else gets NULL.
  4. Aggregate by cluster id, for example collecting the geometries of each cluster and the ids of its parcels.
  5. Handle the NULL group as the unclustered features, for example by excluding or reviewing them.

Why this approach fits

Map features form bands and rings that k-means would cut, and the number of zones is unknown, so a density rule suits them. The eps parameter has a physical meaning in map units, and the NULL label is useful in geography, where an isolated building really does belong to no neighbourhood. Running it as a window function keeps the data where it already lives.

Limitation or tradeoff

One eps applies to the whole table, so a dense city centre and a sparse rural area cannot both be right. Distances are in the geometry's coordinate units, so unprojected longitude and latitude would make eps mean different ground distances at different latitudes. A border geometry that two clusters can reach is assigned to one of them arbitrarily unless the window has an ORDER BY. The manual gives no guidance for choosing eps, so it has to come from domain judgement or a k-distance check.

What the sources document

  • ST_ClusterDBSCAN is a window function that implements DBSCAN and returns a cluster identifier for each input geometry, given a distance eps and a minimum number of geometries minpoints. [14]
  • A geometry within eps of at least minpoints geometries, itself included, is classed as core and one within eps of a core geometry as border; geometries that are neither receive NULL as their cluster id. [14]
  • The function is available from PostGIS 2.3.0. [14]
  • The manual's first example clusters building polygons within 50 meters of each other with at least 2 polygons per cluster, and singletons get NULL; its second aggregates parcels by cluster id with eps 0.5 and minpoints 5 using ST_Collect and array_agg. [14]
  • A border geometry within eps of core geometries in more than one cluster is arbitrarily assigned to one of them, an ORDER BY in the window definition makes repeated calls deterministic, and ambiguous assignments may differ from other DBSCAN implementations. [14]

BERTopic: UMAP and HDBSCAN find topics and set outliers aside

BERTopic (open-source library)Documented in official docs

The BERTopic documentation (the algorithm overview, the parameter-tuning page and the outlier-reduction page) describes a topic-modelling pipeline in which HDBSCAN groups document embeddings and documents that fit no group are labelled topic -1. [15][16][17]

The problem

A corpus of thousands of reviews, tickets or papers needs a list of themes. The number of themes is unknown, themes differ in size and tightness, and many documents are one-offs that belong to no theme. Forcing every document into some theme would add noise to each theme's keyword list.

Where this topic fits

HDBSCAN is the clustering step in the middle of the pipeline. Before it, documents are embedded and the embeddings are reduced to a few dimensions with UMAP. After it, the documents of each cluster are combined into a bag of words and weighted with c-TF-IDF to name the topic.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Embed every document with a sentence-embedding model. The default is all-MiniLM-L6-v2.
  2. Reduce the embeddings with UMAP, which has 5 output components by default, so that HDBSCAN works in a low-dimensional space.
  3. Cluster the reduced vectors with HDBSCAN. The `min_cluster_size` parameter (default 10) sets the smallest possible topic.
  4. Documents that HDBSCAN cannot place get the label -1 instead of being forced into a cluster, so they add no noise to the other topics' keywords.
  5. Build a bag of words per cluster and weight words by c-TF-IDF to produce each topic's representation.
  6. Optionally reassign outliers with `reduce_outliers`, using probabilities, topic distributions, c-TF-IDF or embeddings, then refresh the topics.

Why this approach fits

Topics have no fixed number and no common size, which rules out k-means, and the long tail of one-off documents is exactly what a noise label is for. Reducing dimensions first keeps HDBSCAN in a regime where distances still mean something, and the outlier label keeps odd documents from blurring the keywords.

Limitation or tradeoff

The share of outliers depends on min_cluster_size and min_samples and can be large, and reassigning them with reduce_outliers trades topic purity for coverage. Topics also depend on the embedding model and on the UMAP settings that come before the clustering, so a clustering parameter cannot be judged apart from them.

What the sources document

  • BERTopic embeds documents (default model all-MiniLM-L6-v2), reduces dimensionality with UMAP, clusters with HDBSCAN, builds a bag of words per cluster and represents topics with class-based TF-IDF. [15]
  • The documentation says HDBSCAN can find clusters of different shapes and identify outliers where possible, so documents are not forced into clusters where they might not belong, which reduces noise in the topic representations. [15]
  • The default min_cluster_size is 10, min_samples defaults to the same value and controls how many outliers are generated, and the page advises increasing min_cluster_size rather than decreasing it. [16]
  • Documents that fail to cluster are labelled -1 as outliers, and the outlier-reduction page offers strategies based on probabilities, topic distributions, c-TF-IDF and embeddings, applied with reduce_outliers followed by update_topics. [17]
  • UMAP's n_components defaults to 5, and the tuning page warns that higher settings risk HDBSCAN struggling with the dimensionality. [16]

Hunt and Reffert (2023): HDBSCAN on Gaia DR3 to catalogue open star clusters

The paper "Improving the open cluster census. II. We read the arXiv abstract page and the arXiv HTML text of the accepted manuscript. [18][19]

The problem

Open clusters show up as overdensities among hundreds of millions of stars. They differ widely in size, density and distance, the number of clusters is unknown, and nearly every star belongs to none of them. Each new Gaia data release makes the previous census out of date, so the search has to be repeatable and must not assume how many clusters there are or what shape they have.

Where this topic fits

HDBSCAN is the detection step. It runs on many overlapping patches of the sky and returns candidate groups of stars. The authors then filter and validate the candidates, so the clustering output is a long list of candidates, not the final catalogue.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Select the Gaia DR3 sources to search. The abstract gives 729 million sources down to a magnitude of about G=20, and the text reports 729.7 million sources that pass a quality flag.
  2. Describe each star by five Gaia quantities, its sky position, proper motion and parallax, used directly. Within each patch of sky they are recentred on the patch and each axis is rescaled to the same median and interquartile range, which removes the units.
  3. Split the sky into overlapping HEALPix fields, because 729.7 million sources cannot be clustered at once. The authors aim for no more than 20 million sources per field.
  4. Run HDBSCAN in each field with minimum cluster sizes of 10, 20, 40 and 80 and a minimum neighbourhood count of 10, so that groups of stars that are dense in those five dimensions become candidate clusters and the other stars stay unclustered.
  5. Merge the detections from all fields and filter them with a statistical density test, the cluster significance test, which gives each candidate an astrometric signal-to-noise, and validate clusters with a Bayesian convolutional neural network that classifies colour-magnitude diagrams.
  6. Infer ages, extinctions and distances for the accepted clusters, and crossmatch them with the literature to separate known objects from candidate new ones.

Why this approach fits

The authors' own reasoning is that Gaia has different densities at different distances and locations, which one global radius handles badly. A star survey also has an unknown number of groups and a huge background of stars that belong to no group, which k-means would force into clusters. Using all radii at once lets one run find both dense and faint, sparse clusters.

Limitation or tradeoff

A density algorithm finds overdensities, not physical objects. The authors report that HDBSCAN is over sensitive: of the 7.1 million detections, duplicates included, almost all were astrometric false positives, so they filter with a significance test and a neural-network check and choose a strict threshold that gives up some completeness. The abstract also suggests that many clusters from older catalogues that the search did not detect may not be real, including 1,152 from the MWSC catalogue, and says that as many as a few thousand of the new catalogue's clusters fit unbound moving groups better than bound clusters.

4Common mistakes

  1. Running DBSCAN with the default eps on features that have not been scaled.

    Why it causes problems
    The scikit-learn documentation says eps usually cannot be left at its default, because it is a distance in feature units. When one column is in thousands and another in fractions, the large column decides every distance and eps is either far too small, so everything is noise, or far too large, so everything is one cluster.
    How to recognise it
    The labels are almost all -1, or one cluster holds nearly every point, and changing eps by ten percent changes the result drastically.
    How to fix it
    Scale the features, plot the sorted k-distance curve and set eps near its knee, then sweep eps around that value and prefer a plateau where the number of clusters and the noise share stay put.

  2. Treating the label -1 as an ordinary cluster, or dropping those rows without saying so.

    Why it causes problems
    Noise points are by definition unlike each other, so a "noise cluster" is a meaningless segment. Dropping them silently makes the population counts wrong and biases any statistic computed per cluster.
    How to recognise it
    One group has label -1 and its points are scattered across the whole space, cluster counts are off by one, or the noise share was never reported.
    How to fix it
    Handle -1 explicitly. Report the noise share, then decide whether to review those points, keep them as "unclustered", or reassign them with a stated rule such as BERTopic's reduce_outliers.

  3. Choosing the cut height of a dendrogram by eye, or reusing a height from a different linkage.

    Why it causes problems
    Heights are not comparable across linkages. In the worked example the last merge is at 4.84 for single, 6.21 for average and 11.49 for Ward. A cut taken from the wrong scale gives either dozens of tiny clusters or a single one.
    How to recognise it
    Many clusters of one or two points, or a cut that falls inside a stretch where merges are packed closely together.
    How to fix it
    Sort the merge heights (the third column of the SciPy linkage matrix), look for the largest gap, and cut inside it. Recompute the height whenever the linkage or the scaling changes.

  4. Using Ward or centroid linkage with a non-Euclidean distance such as cosine.

    Why it causes problems
    The Ward criterion is defined through sums of squared Euclidean distances. scikit-learn rejects other metrics for Ward, and SciPy says ward, centroid and median are correctly defined only for Euclidean distance, so a precomputed cosine matrix gives a result with no clear meaning.
    How to recognise it
    scikit-learn raises an error for Ward with a non-Euclidean metric. A precomputed distance matrix passed to SciPy cannot be checked by the library, so confirm by hand that the distances are Euclidean.
    How to fix it
    For embeddings, normalise each vector to unit length and use Euclidean distance, which orders pairs like cosine distance, or choose average or complete linkage with the cosine metric.

  5. Setting min_samples to 1 or 2 so that almost every point becomes a core point.

    Why it causes problems
    With min_samples of 1 every point is core, so there is no noise and clusters are the connected components at distance eps, which is single-linkage chaining again. Values of 2 behave almost the same.
    How to recognise it
    No noise at all, and long snaking clusters that join groups through a thin line of points.
    How to fix it
    Start near 4 for two-dimensional data, as the original paper suggests, and raise it on large or noisy data. Check that isolated points are labelled -1 on your data.

from itertools import combinations
from math import dist

points = [(1.0, 1.0), (2.0, 1.2), (1.4, 2.1), (2.9, 2.6),
          (6.0, 5.0), (7.1, 5.2), (6.3, 6.1), (8.6, 0.6)]
with_x = points + [(4.4, 3.7)]


def dbscan(pts, eps, min_samples):
    n = len(pts)
    nbrs = [[j for j in range(n) if dist(pts[i], pts[j]) <= eps] for i in range(n)]
    core = [len(nb) >= min_samples for nb in nbrs]
    labels, cid = [-1] * n, 0
    for i in range(n):
        if labels[i] != -1 or not core[i]:
            continue
        labels[i], stack = cid, [i]
        while stack:
            p = stack.pop()
            if core[p]:
                for q in nbrs[p]:
                    if labels[q] == -1:
                        labels[q] = cid
                        stack.append(q)
        cid += 1
    return labels


def link(pts, a, b, how):
    ds = [dist(pts[i], pts[j]) for i in a for j in b]
    return min(ds) if how == "single" else sum(ds) / len(ds)


def agglomerate(pts, how, k=None, height=None):
    clusters = [(i,) for i in range(len(pts))]
    while len(clusters) > 1:
        h, a, b = min((link(pts, a, b, how), a, b) for a, b in combinations(clusters, 2))
        if len(clusters) == k or (height is not None and h > height):
            break
        clusters = [c for c in clusters if c not in (a, b)] + [a + b]
    return sorted("".join("ABCDEFGHX"[i] for i in sorted(c)) for c in clusters)


def groups(labels):
    found = {}
    for name, lab in zip("ABCDEFGHX", labels):
        found.setdefault(lab, []).append(name)
    return " ".join("".join(v) if k != -1 else "noise:" + "".join(v)
                    for k, v in sorted(found.items()))


def plateaus(pts):
    previous = None
    for eps in sorted({dist(p, q) for p, q in combinations(pts, 2)}):
        outcome = groups(dbscan(pts, eps, 3))
        if outcome != previous:
            print(f"  eps >= {eps:.4f}  {outcome}")
            previous = outcome


print("DBSCAN, min_samples=3, eight points")
plateaus(points)
print("single k=2:", agglomerate(points, "single", k=2))
print("average k=2:", agglomerate(points, "average", k=2))
print("DBSCAN, nine points (X added)")
plateaus(with_x)
print("single cut at 3.0:", agglomerate(with_x, "single", height=3.0))
print("average cut at 3.0:", agglomerate(with_x, "average", height=3.0))

Reading the result for step 4: before X the answer "two clusters plus H as noise" holds for eps from 1.5811 to just under 3.9205. After X it holds only from 1.5811 to just under 2.0616, because X is within 2.06 of both groups. Once eps reaches 2.0616, X has D and E within reach and becomes a core point, and the chain of core points D, X, E fuses the groups. Single linkage uses only the closest pair, so X lets the two groups merge at 2.0616 and the cut at 3.0 sees one big cluster. Average linkage averages all pairs between the groups, and most of those pairs are far apart, so X barely moves the average and the cut at 3.0 still separates the groups.

Compare k-means, linkages and DBSCAN on two crescents

CodingIntermediateAbout 50 min (estimate)Hypothetical scenario

Forty points lie on two interlocking half circles with no noise. Generate them with NumPy:

Use scikit-learn, which is free, in a local environment or a hosted notebook.

  1. Compute, for every point, the distance to its second-nearest other point (the k-distance for min_samples = 3). Report the smallest and largest value.
  2. Run DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=3) for eps from 0.10 to 0.60 in steps of 0.01. Report the smallest and largest eps that give exactly two clusters and no noise.
  3. Cluster into two groups with KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0) and with AgglomerativeClustering using single, average and Ward linkage. For each, count how many points match the true crescents. Cluster ids are arbitrary, so use the better of the count and 40 minus the count.
  4. Compute the silhouette score of the k-means labels and of the DBSCAN labels at eps = 0.25.
  5. In two sentences, explain why the silhouette ranks the methods in the opposite order from the truth.

Requirements

  • Build the k-distance values with NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=3), remembering that the first neighbour of each point is the point itself.
  • Use min_samples = 3 for every DBSCAN run.
  • Report an agreement count between 0 and 40 for every method, using the order-independent rule given in step 3.
  • Report the two silhouette scores to two decimals.

Expected behaviour

The k-distances run from about 0.165 to about 0.329. DBSCAN finds exactly two clusters and no noise for eps from 0.17 to 0.50 and merges the crescents at 0.51, because the closest points on different crescents are 0.503 apart. DBSCAN and single linkage match all 40 points. K-means matches 29 or 30 of 40, because it ends in a straight-line split. In the better of its two local optima each cluster holds 15 points of one crescent and 5 of the other. Average and Ward linkage do not recover the crescents. In our plain-Python checks they matched between 28 and 35 points, depending on how ties in this symmetric data were broken. The silhouette is about 0.47 for the k-means labels and about 0.28 for the correct DBSCAN labels.

Hint 1

Pairs in the same crescent are 0.165 apart, while the crescents are 0.503 apart at their closest, so a wide range of eps works.

Hint 2

Cluster ids are arbitrary, so compare with truth in both orientations and keep the larger agreement.

Hint 3

For the sweep, collect eps values where set(labels) == {0, 1}, then print the first and last.

Show solution
python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, KMeans, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

t = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 20)
upper = np.column_stack([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)])
lower = np.column_stack([1 - np.cos(t), 0.5 - np.sin(t)])
X = np.vstack([upper, lower])
truth = np.array([0] * 20 + [1] * 20)


def agreement(labels):
    same = int((labels == truth).sum())
    return max(same, len(truth) - same)


# 1. the first neighbour of each point is the point itself, so the last of three is the second-nearest other point
dists, _ = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=3).fit(X).kneighbors(X)
k_dist = dists[:, -1]
print("k-distance min and max:", k_dist.min().round(3), k_dist.max().round(3))

# 2. sweep eps
good = [round(float(e), 2) for e in np.arange(0.10, 0.601, 0.01)
        if set(DBSCAN(eps=e, min_samples=3).fit_predict(X)) == {0, 1}]
print("two clusters, no noise for eps from", good[0], "to", good[-1])

# 3. compare methods
dbs = DBSCAN(eps=0.25, min_samples=3).fit_predict(X)
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
print("DBSCAN", agreement(dbs), "k-means", agreement(km))
for linkage in ("single", "average", "ward"):
    labels = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage=linkage).fit_predict(X)
    print(linkage, agreement(labels))

# 4. silhouette
print("silhouette DBSCAN", round(silhouette_score(X, dbs), 2),
      "k-means", round(silhouette_score(X, km), 2))

Expected output, apart from the average and Ward counts, which depend on tie-breaking and fall below 40:

text
k-distance min and max: 0.165 0.329
two clusters, no noise for eps from 0.17 to 0.5
DBSCAN 40 k-means 30
single 40
silhouette DBSCAN 0.28 k-means 0.47

The k-means line may show 29 instead of 30, because the data have two local optima, and the silhouette of the k-means labels stays near 0.47 either way. The silhouette ranks k-means higher because it rewards compact clusters, as the scikit-learn guide warns. It compares each point's average distance to its own cluster with its average distance to the nearest other cluster. A long crescent has a large average internal distance, so its points score low even though the grouping is correct.
