Technical Scoping and Sequencing

Technical scoping is deciding what the first deliverable version of a solution will include and what it will leave out, so that it fits a fixed amount of time. Sequencing is deciding the order of the work, so that the biggest unknowns and the most valuable pieces are tackled first.

1Overview

Why it matters

Discovery ends with a problem statement and a list of requirements, and that list is usually larger than the time available. A forward deployed engineer often has a few weeks, one or two people, and a customer who expects to see something working. If nobody decides what to leave out, the calendar decides: the last items in the plan are dropped whether or not they mattered, and testing is squeezed because it comes last. The inspector general's review of the 2013 HealthCare.gov launch describes this pattern: scope was cut in the final weeks, testing was never completed, and the cuts came too late and went too shallow to change the outcome.

Scoping and sequencing settle four things before the build starts: which thin version will run end to end first, which unknown could sink the plan and so must be tested in the first days, how long the work is likely to take and how sure you are, and which of scope, speed and quality gives way when something slips. Anyone who commits to a date on behalf of a team needs this skill: forward deployed engineers, solutions engineers, tech leads, and product engineers planning their own work. It matters most for AI features, where the model is often the least certain part and a simpler first version is frequently enough.

Before you start

What you will be able to do

  • Cut a requirements list down to a first slice that runs end to end for one narrow case and can be demonstrated.
  • Order work by value and by risk, and explain why each item is first, later, a time-boxed spike or out of scope.
  • Give each work item a three-point estimate and turn a set of them into a probability of fitting a time box.
  • State which of time, scope and quality is fixed for an engagement and which will give way first.
  • Write a one-page scope note with a first slice, an explicit out-of-scope list and a stated confidence level.
  • Spot late unplanned scope cuts, single-number estimates and plans where everything is a must-have, and correct them.

Key terms

Scope
The set of things a piece of work will deliver, together with the explicit list of things it will not deliver.
First slice
The smallest version that runs from a user's action to a real result for one narrow case, touching every layer it needs. Also called a vertical slice.
Appetite
The amount of time a team is willing to spend on a problem, set before the solution is designed. It is a budget, not a prediction.
Time box
A fixed period for a piece of work, after which the work stops or is reviewed whatever state it is in.
Spike
A short, time-boxed investigation whose output is an answer to a question, not production code.
Riskiest assumption
The belief that the plan depends on and that is most likely to be wrong, such as the belief that a system has an API you are allowed to use.
Three-point estimate
An estimate given as three numbers (optimistic, most likely and pessimistic) instead of one.
Confidence level
The probability that work will finish within a stated time or cost, for example an 80 percent chance of fitting in 15 days.
Must-have
An item without which the delivery has no point. Everything else is, to some degree, contingency that can be dropped to protect the date.
Scope hammering
Basecamp's term for repeatedly cutting and reshaping scope during the work so that it fits the time box.

2Explanation

Introduction

Anyone who has packed for a flight with one cabin bag has done scoping. The bag does not get bigger, so you decide what goes in, and the decision is better made at home than at the gate. Software work for a customer has the same shape. The previous lesson, Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering, ends with a problem statement and a numbered list of testable requirements. This lesson is about what happens next: the list is bigger than the time you have, and you must choose.

Scope is what a piece of work will deliver and, just as importantly, what it will not. Sequencing is the order in which you do it. A documented case shows both. Around 2010 the Swedish Police started building a system that gave officers access to police systems from laptops in their cars. Henrik Kniberg, who coached the 60-person project, writes that an earlier attempt had taken seven years to reach its first release. This time the team cut the problem along two lines, region and crime type. Version 1.0 supported only a couple of crime types and was field-tested by a handful of officers in one region, Östergötland. Other crime types were still handled the old way, with paperwork at the station. Feedback from those officers shaped each later release, and by the nationwide release to 12,000 police the team had already released many times (Kniberg, Making sense of MVP (opens in a new tab)).

That first version is a first slice: narrow in who and what it covers, but complete from the user's action to a real result. The rest of this lesson covers the four decisions that produce one:

  1. Cut the problem to a first slice that fits a fixed amount of time.
  2. Sequence the work so that the riskiest unknowns and the most valuable pieces come first.
  3. Estimate with ranges and a stated confidence level, not a single number.
  4. Trade off scope, speed and quality on purpose, and write the choice down.

The loop in the diagram runs more than once. An estimate that does not fit sends you back to cut scope, and a spike that fails sends you back to the slice, because the slice may rest on an assumption that turned out to be false. In a forward deployed engagement this is the scoping phase of the engagement lifecycle. Its output is short: a one-page scope note that the customer has agreed to.

How it works

Scoping turns a requirements list into a plan that both sides believe. It has five moves. They are written in order, but in practice you go round them two or three times.

1. Fix the time first, then fit the scope

Ask how much time the problem is worth before designing anything. Basecamp calls that number the appetite and separates it from an estimate: an estimate begins from a design and produces a number, whereas an appetite begins from a number and produces a design (Shape Up, chapter 3 (opens in a new tab)). In an engagement the appetite is often handed to you, for example three weeks before a steering meeting.

Every project balances four demands: time, cost, features and quality. The DSDM handbook advises deciding at the start which of them you will protect and which you can negotiate if you hit a problem, because trying to fix all four leaves no contingency (DSDM handbook (opens in a new tab), section 3.3). Its answer is the same as Basecamp's: fix time, cost and quality, and let the features vary.

2. Cut a first slice

A first slice is thin but complete. It takes one kind of user through one kind of case, from their action to a real result, and touches every layer on the way: interface, logic and data access. Building layer by layer instead (all the pipelines, then all the logic, then the screens) leaves nothing to show until the end and hides integration problems until then. Shape Up's guidance is to have something demoable in the first week or so, and to pick a first piece that is core (the project is pointless without it), small (finished in a few days) and novel (not done before, so finishing it removes uncertainty) (chapter 10 (opens in a new tab)).

To make a slice thin, cut along one or more of these lines:

Cut byFirst slice coversLeft for later
User group or siteOne team or one regionEveryone else
Case typeThe most common caseRare and awkward cases
Data sourceThe source you can already reachSources that need approvals or new integrations
Degree of automationThe tool suggests, a person decidesActing without review
Engineering depthHard-coded settings and manual steps behind the scenesAdmin screens and self-service setup

Do not cut what makes the slice trustworthy: the acceptance checks agreed in discovery, access controls, and the measurement that shows whether the number moved.

For AI work, the thinnest slice often has no model in it, or the simplest one. The first of Google's Rules of Machine Learning tells teams not to be afraid of launching a product without machine learning, and the fourth advises keeping the first model simple while getting the infrastructure right (Rules of ML (opens in a new tab)). Anthropic gives the same advice for LLM applications: find the simplest solution possible and add complexity only when needed (Building effective agents (opens in a new tab)). A lookup, a rule or a single prompt that runs end to end also gives you a baseline to beat (see Evaluating LLM Applications).

Finish this move by writing the out-of-scope list. DSDM calls these items "Won't Have this time" and records them so that they are not informally reintroduced later.

3. Sequence by risk and by value

Two questions order the work. Value: if this item worked, how many people would it help, and by how much? Risk: what do we believe that could turn out to be false and break the plan?

For value, a simple score makes unlike items comparable. Intercom's RICE score multiplies reach (how many people in a period), impact (how much each) and confidence (how well supported the estimates are), then divides by effort, giving value per unit of work (Intercom (opens in a new tab)). In an engagement, reach and impact come from the baseline you measured in discovery, such as tickets per week and seconds saved per ticket.

Find the risks by asking for them. Microsoft's ISE team holds a pre-mortem, with the customer if possible: imagine the project has failed, and list what caused it (ISE playbook (opens in a new tab)). The UK government's service manual calls identifying and testing the riskiest assumptions a crucial part of the alpha phase. Its example is the Register to vote team, which focused its alpha phase on whether it could build an API that integrated with the registration systems of over 400 local councils, because without that the service would not have worked (GOV.UK (opens in a new tab)). The tool for a risky item is a spike: a time-boxed investigation that produces an answer, using code you expect to throw away.

The same rule applies inside the build. Shape Up's test is to ask which piece you could still throw together at the end if you ran out of time, and which might prove harder than you think. The second kind goes first (chapter 13 (opens in a new tab)). Dependencies and lead times override any ranking: an access request that takes a week to approve is filed on day one.

4. Estimate with ranges and a confidence level

A single number hides how sure you are, and early numbers are wide. Construx reports that estimates made at the initial-concept stage can be wrong by a factor of four in either direction, and that the range narrows only as decisions are made (Cone of Uncertainty (opens in a new tab)). So give each item three numbers: optimistic OOO, most likely MMM and pessimistic PPP. The PERT formulas turn them into an expected value and a spread (three-point estimation (opens in a new tab)):

E=O+4M+P6SD=P−O6E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6} \qquad SD = \frac{P - O}{6}E=6O+4M+P​SD=6P−O​

For a set of items, add the expected values and combine the spreads as the square root of the sum of squares. This assumes that one item running late does not make the others late.

Etotal=∑EiSDtotal=∑SDi2E_{total} = \sum E_i \qquad SD_{total} = \sqrt{\sum SD_i^2}Etotal​=∑Ei​SDtotal​=∑SDi2​​

The chance of fitting a time box of TTT days is then roughly Φ(T−EtotalSDtotal)\Phi\left(\frac{T - E_{total}}{SD_{total}}\right)Φ(SDtotal​T−Etotal​​), where Φ\PhiΦ is the standard normal cumulative distribution: 0.5 at zero, about 0.84 at 1 and about 0.98 at 2. A simulation that draws every item at random many times gives the answer without the bell-curve assumption. Fog Creek built this into FogBugz as Evidence Based Scheduling, which divides each estimate by a ratio picked at random from that developer's own history of estimate against actual (Spolsky (opens in a new tab)).

Two habits keep the numbers honest. Say the confidence level aloud, because "about four chances in five" is a different promise from "expected". And check your inside view against an outside one. Reference class forecasting bases a forecast on actual performance in a class of comparable past projects (Flyvbjerg (opens in a new tab)). For you that may be as simple as "the last three integrations at this customer took twice what we guessed".

5. Trade off scope, speed and quality on purpose

When the estimate does not fit, something gives. Decide which before the pressure arrives.

  • Scope gives first. Label every item. DSDM's guide is that Must Haves take no more than about 60% of the effort, so that the rest is contingency (handbook chapter 10). To test a must-have, Shape Up asks whether the feature could ship without it, what happens if it is left out, and whether it is a new problem or one customers already live with (chapter 14 (opens in a new tab)). Compare with the customer's current workaround, not with the ideal.
  • Quality is a stated bar, not a leftover. Fix it as acceptance criteria before building. Where some failure is acceptable, make it a budget. Google's SRE teams agree a reliability target; while the measured service stays above it, releases continue, and when the error budget is spent, releases stop (SRE book (opens in a new tab)).
  • Time moves only by decision. Basecamp extends a project only when the remaining work is all must-have and contains no open questions.

Write the result on one page: appetite, first slice, out-of-scope list, sequence, estimate with its confidence level, and what gives first. That page feeds the solution design document and your status updates.

Worked example: Tool-hire support desk: fitting the order lookup panel into a 15-day box

This continues the teaching scenario from the discovery lesson. The data is invented so that you can reproduce every number.

Starting point. Discovery found that agents spend about 230 of 347 seconds per order-status ticket looking things up in the ERP and in three carrier portals, and it produced five requirements for a read-only lookup panel. You have 15 working days before the customer's operations review, and you are the only engineer.

Step 1: fix what will not move

Time is fixed at 15 days. Quality is fixed as the acceptance checks from discovery: the replay test on 100 past tickets, and read-only access to the ERP. Scope is what can change.

Step 2: list the work with value and three-point estimates

Value is the lookup time removed per order-status ticket. It comes from the discovery log plus one new fact from an ERP export: carrier A ships 60% of orders. O, M and P are in days.

def pert(o, m, p):
    return (o + 4 * m + p) / 6, (p - o) / 6

def draw(o, m, p):  # one random duration from the PERT (beta) distribution
    return o + (p - o) * random.betavariate(1 + 4 * (m - o) / (p - o), 1 + 4 * (p - m) / (p - o))

def normal_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

random.seed(7)
TRIALS = 20000
for label, scope in SCOPES.items():

    sd = math.sqrt(sum(pert(*ITEMS[n])[1] ** 2 for n in scope))
    p_formula = normal_cdf((BOX - mean) / sd)

    p_sim = sum(t <= BOX for t in totals) / TRIALS
    p80 = totals[int(0.8 * TRIALS) - 1]

          f"P(fit) formula={p_formula:5.1%} simulated={p_sim:5.1%} 80% by day {p80:4.1f}")

Step 4: choose and sequence

  • Committed (S1). ERP status for tickets that contain an order number, tested and piloted: 9.17 expected days, 61% of the box. This is the first slice: one case type and one data source, end to end.
  • Target (S2). Carrier A tracking is the largest single saving and the largest unknown. With it, the chance of fitting is about four in five.
  • Not this time. Sender matching on top of S2 fits about three times in ten, and carriers B and C do not fit at all. Sender matching is requirement R3 from discovery. It is deferred, not deleted: until a later phase, tickets without an order number keep today's manual lookup.

Order of work: on day 1, file the ERP access request (its 5-day pessimistic case is waiting, not work) and start a one-day spike on carrier A's API. Build the panel against one hard-coded order, so that there is something to demo in week one. Then order-number extraction, the replay test and the pilot. If the spike finds no usable API, sender matching takes carrier A's place: S1 plus sender matching is 11.33 expected days and fits with near certainty.

Step 5: say it in one paragraph

By day 15 the panel will show ERP status for order-status tickets that include an order number, tested on 100 past tickets and piloted with two agents. Carrier A tracking is likely, at about four chances in five, and is the first thing we drop if ERP access is approved late. Tickets without an order number, and carriers B and C, are not in this phase.

What the result means. Adding up the most likely values gives 19.5 days for the full list, which invites a promise of "about four weeks" against a three-week box. The ranges show that the full list has almost no chance of fitting (0.2%), and the explicit cut turns the plan into a commitment the customer can rely on plus a clearly labelled stretch.

Edge cases and limitations

The riskiest item is a wait, not a task
Access approvals, vendor credentials and security reviews take elapsed days while needing almost no effort, and you cannot speed them up by working harder. Estimate them in elapsed days, start them on day one, and build against a stub or an exported sample in the meantime. Put a date in the scope note: if access has not arrived by day 5, name the item that drops.
Items that run late together
Combining spreads as the square root of the sum of squares assumes that items slip independently. A shared cause, such as one undocumented ERP behind three tasks or one person everyone waits on, makes them slip together, and the real spread is wider. As an upper bound, add the spreads directly. For scope S2 in the worked example that gives 3.42 days instead of 1.54, and the chance of fitting falls from about 79% to about 64%.
An accuracy target has no reliable upper estimate
You can estimate building a pipeline and a labelled evaluation set. You cannot estimate how many rounds of prompt or model changes it takes to reach 95% on that set. Scope the iteration as a fixed time box with a decision at the end, and put the fallback in the first slice (for example, low-confidence cases go to a person), so that the slice ships even if the target is missed.
A workflow with no usable partial version
Some work cannot be done half in the new tool, such as a filing that needs every section or a replacement for a system of record. Slice by audience instead: run the new path in parallel for one team, compare its output with the old one, and call it a test release, not a usable one. Kniberg's terms help here, because an earliest testable product comes before an earliest usable one.
Scope is fixed by a contract
When a statement of work lists deliverables, you cannot cut them on your own. Find out which items are named in the contract and treat only those as must-haves. Agree a written change procedure before starting, and raise a likely miss as soon as the estimate shows it, while the customer still has options other than a missed date or untested work.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The requirements list is larger than the time or people available, and someone will be held to a date.
  • The plan depends on something nobody has tried, such as an API you have not called, a join you have not run, or a model that has not been evaluated on the customer's data.
  • More than one person or team must agree what is in and what is out before the work starts.
  • The work is longer than about a week, so that a slip found at the end would be expensive.

Think twice when

  • The whole job is a day or two of well-understood work. Do it, show it and move on, because a scope note would cost more than a mistake.
  • You do not yet know which problem you are solving. Go back to discovery, because estimating a guess only produces a confident wrong number.
  • The numbers are being adjusted to justify a date that has already been promised. Say that the date is fixed and scope to it.

A simpler alternative. For a job of a few days, write three lines: what will work at the end, what will not be included, and the one thing you are least sure about, which you do first. Agree them in a message to the customer. This stops being enough when the work runs past a week, touches a system you have not used before, or is built by more than one person.

Key takeaways

  • Fix the time first and fit the scope to it. An appetite is a budget you set, while an estimate is a prediction about a design.
  • A first slice is narrow but complete, covering one user group and one case type end to end, with the acceptance checks left in.
  • Do the item that is both valuable and uncertain first, as a time-boxed spike or inside the first slice, and file slow external requests on day one.
  • Estimate each item with three numbers, add the expected values, combine the spreads, and state the chance of fitting the time box.
  • Decide before you start what gives way when something slips, keep must-haves to roughly 60% of the box, and write down what is out of scope.
  • Scope that is cut early and deliberately is a plan. Scope that is cut in the last weeks, as at the 2013 HealthCare.gov launch, is a symptom.

Example 1 of 5

Basecamp sets an appetite, integrates one slice early and hammers scope to fit a six-week cycle

37signals (Basecamp)Documented in a case study

Shape Up, written by Ryan Singer and published by Basecamp (37signals), describes how the Basecamp product team plans and builds. Chapters 3, 10, 13 and 14 cover setting a time budget, building a first integrated piece, ordering work by its unknowns and deciding what to cut. [1][2][3][4]

The problem

Product work has no natural end. The book observes that without a time limit a better version can always be imagined. It also describes two ways a plan misleads. Task lists grow as the team discovers work, so counting completed tasks says little about how far along a project is. And a team can complete many tasks while having no single working thing to click on and try, so nobody can tell whether the project is on track.

Where this topic fits

Scoping happens at three points. Before any work is committed, the appetite is set and a solution is shaped to fit it. In the first days of building, the team picks one piece and integrates it end to end. Then, through the whole cycle, the team keeps separating must-haves from nice-to-haves until what remains fits the time box.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Set the appetite before designing. A small batch is one or two weeks and a big batch is six weeks, for a team of one designer and one or two programmers.
  2. Shape a solution to fit the appetite. If a six-week version cannot be imagined, narrow the problem definition or break off a meaningful part that can be shaped to six weeks.
  3. In the first week or so, integrate one slice vertically. In the Clients in Projects feature the team chose the visibility toggle. The designer tried rough versions in the HTML templates, and the programmer wired the toggle to appear on every supported content type, change state and save to the database, before it changed any real visibility.
  4. Choose that first piece by three tests. It is core to the concept, small enough to finish in a few days, and novel enough that finishing it removes uncertainty.
  5. Order the remaining scopes by their unknowns. Push the scariest work uphill first, such as geocoding the team has never done, and leave routine work such as an email template for the final week.
  6. As work is discovered, record must-haves as tasks and mark nice-to-haves with a tilde. Test each must-have with a fixed set of questions, comparing against what customers do today instead of against an ideal.
  7. At the deadline, let the circuit breaker act, so that the project is not extended by default. An extension of a couple of weeks is considered only when the remaining tasks are true must-haves and contain no open questions.

Why this approach fits

A fixed time box turns "is this good enough?", which has no answer, into "is there time for this?", which does. With time set first, scope is the only thing that can move, so trade-offs are made daily and in small pieces by the people who know the work, instead of once at the end by someone who does not. Integrating one slice in the first week converts the most dangerous unknown, whether the parts fit together and the idea makes sense, into something a manager can click on.

Limitation or tradeoff

The method assumes a product team that owns its roadmap. The circuit breaker, cancelling work that does not ship, is rarely available in a paid engagement with named deliverables. It also depends on skilled shaping before the cycle starts: the book says that uphill work at the end of a cycle points to an oversight in shaping. Shape Up describes one company's practice, with about a dozen people on the product team at the time of writing, and reports no measured outcomes. Six weeks is that company's choice, not a rule.

What the sources document

  • Shape Up defines the appetite as a time budget for a standard team size, set in two sizes. A small batch is a project that one designer and one or two programmers can build in one or two weeks, and a big batch takes the same-size team a full six weeks. [1]
  • The book contrasts the two terms. An estimate starts from a design and ends with a number, while an appetite starts from a number and ends with a design. It names the principle fixed time, variable scope. [1]
  • In the Clients in Projects case study, a team of one designer and one programmer chose the visibility toggle as the first piece to integrate. About three days after the project started the designer demoed the working toggle to a manager, at a point when it did not yet change the visibility of content. [2]
  • The chapter on showing progress says effective teams choose the most important problems with the most unknowns first. It contrasts a geocoding scope, which the team has never done before, with an email template that could be worked out in a day during the final week. [3]
  • The book says nice-to-haves are marked with a tilde and usually never get built, that tasks found by QA are nice-to-haves by default, and that a project is extended only if the outstanding tasks are true must-haves and the outstanding work is all downhill. [4]

The facts above come from the cited sources. The steps and analysis in this example are our explanation of how they fit together.

Example 2 of 5

Microsoft ISE runs a pre-mortem and time-boxed feasibility spikes before engineering sprints

Microsoft Industry Solutions EngineeringDocumented in official docs

The ISE Engineering Fundamentals Playbook is published by Microsoft's Industry Solutions Engineering team and describes how it runs customer engagements. Its design-review section covers Engineering Feasibility Spikes, a phase added after envisioning and architecture sessions when an engagement still has substantial technical unknowns. [8]

The problem

Some customer engagements begin with technical questions that the design sessions did not settle, such as whether a service can do what the design assumes and with which permissions. The playbook says that these engagements warrant an exploratory or validation phase. Without one, the plan for the sprints rests on guesses, and the first evidence that a design is not viable arrives after work has been built on top of it.

Where this topic fits

The spikes sit between envisioning (or the Architectural Design Session) and the first engineering sprint. A pre-mortem supplies the list of risks, the risks chosen from it become spikes, and what the spikes find feeds the decision log, the architecture and the order of the remaining work.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Hold a pre-mortem, a 90-minute meeting of the entire team that can include the customer. It answers one question, which is to imagine the project has failed and say what problems and challenges caused the failure.
  2. Use the risks that people raise to decide which ones to pursue as engineering spikes.
  3. Run each spike as a time-boxed investigation. Write only enough code to reach the technical learning, knowing that it will not serve as the code of the final solution.
  4. Share findings while the work is in flight. Everyone on the dev crew joins a 30-minute call at least weekly, including people whose spikes are unfinished.
  5. Plan again after each sharing call. Re-prioritise the next spikes in the light of what was learned, and change direction when a spike's original acceptance criteria are no longer valid.
  6. Track outcomes in a decision log, and take concrete findings to product groups and subject-matter experts to show why something is or is not a viable way forward.
  7. When the spikes are finished, keep looking for emerging risks in the normal rhythm, for example with a risk column on the retrospective board.

Why this approach fits

A team working in a customer's environment depends on services whose limits it does not control, and the risks that matter are specific enough to settle by trying. The playbook's own illustration is a console app written only to learn whether a Graph client could call the Graph API with the minimal number of permissions. Putting these questions before the sprints means the order of the build is set by evidence. The weekly sharing call makes the loop shorter than a sprint, and including the customer means they watch the plan change for reasons they have seen.

Limitation or tradeoff

A spike phase delays visible features, which is hard to defend to a sponsor who wants a demo, so the time box and the decision log have to be shown. The page gives no method for ranking risks beyond the pre-mortem discussion, and a pre-mortem surfaces only the risks that someone in the room can imagine. Spike code that works is tempting to ship, and the page warns teams to be intentional about not aiming for production-level code. It describes the process and the benefits the team sees, such as increased customer trust, without measured results.

  • It describes a pre-mortem as a 90-minute meeting that includes the entire team and can also include the customer, which asks what problems and challenges caused the imagined failure of the project. It says this input is used to decide which risks to pursue as engineering spikes. [8]
  • Learnings are shared weekly, or more often if needed, on a 30-minute call that everyone on the dev crew joins. The page says this feedback loop is significantly tighter than in a sprint-based process. [8]

Example 3 of 5

Intercom scores roadmap ideas with RICE to decide what to build first

IntercomDocumented in an engineering article

Intercom's blog post "RICE: Simple prioritization for product managers", written by Sean McBride and published in January 2018, describes the scoring system the company developed for ordering project ideas on its product roadmap. [9]

The problem

The post lists why ordering a roadmap is hard. It is satisfying to work on pet ideas instead of projects with broad reach, tempting to pick clever ideas over ones that affect the team's goals, exciting to start new ideas instead of ones you are already confident about, and easy to discount the extra effort one project needs over another. Even someone who avoids all four still has to combine and compare these factors consistently across every idea, and the author's answer is a scoring system instead of doing it in your head.

Where this topic fits

Scoring sits between collecting ideas and committing to an order. It takes product metrics for reach, research or judgement for impact, and a rough effort figure covering product, design and engineering. It produces a sorted list, which the team then re-examines and adjusts for dependencies.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Estimate reach, the number of people or events affected in a set period, from product metrics where possible. The author's team counts customers per quarter.
  2. Estimate impact on each person using a fixed multiple-choice scale, which is 3 for massive, 2 for high, 1 for medium, 0.5 for low and 0.25 for minimal.
  3. State confidence in those estimates as 100%, 80% or 50%, so that an exciting idea with no supporting data is scaled down.
  4. Estimate effort in person-months across product, design and engineering, in whole numbers, or 0.5 for anything well under a month.
  5. Multiply reach, impact and confidence and divide by effort. The post describes the result as total impact per time worked.
  6. Sort the list, then look again at scores that seem too high or too low, and either correct the estimates or accept that instinct was wrong.
  7. Move an item out of score order when there is a reason, such as one project being a dependency of another, and be clear that a trade-off is being made.

Why this approach fits

The value side of sequencing is where opinion dominates. Fixed scales force each factor to be stated separately, so a disagreement becomes an argument about one number ("is reach really 2,000?") instead of about the whole idea. Dividing by effort turns the score into value per unit of work, which is what a team with a fixed time box wants to maximise. Confidence as a multiplier puts a visible price on guesswork.

Limitation or tradeoff

The score is only as good as its inputs. The impact scale is a judgement, and multiplying four rough numbers makes the result look more precise than it is, as the sensitivity check above shows. RICE measures expected value, not delivery risk: its confidence factor covers trust in the estimates, so a high-scoring item can still hide a technical unknown that should be spiked first. The post itself says scores should not be a hard and fast rule, and it reports no measured outcome. Reach also needs product metrics, which a new engagement may not have, so use counts from discovery instead.

What the sources document

  • The post says Intercom began developing its own scoring system for prioritisation from first principles and, after testing and iteration, settled on four factors, which are reach, impact, confidence and effort. [9]
  • Reach is measured in people or events per time period. Impact uses a scale of 3, 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.25. Confidence uses 100%, 80% and 50%. Effort is estimated in person-months, in whole numbers or 0.5 for anything well under a month. [9]
  • The post's three example projects have reach of 450, 2,000 and 800 customers per quarter, impact scores of 3, 1 and 2, confidence of 100%, 80% and 50%, and effort of 2, 4 and 1 person-months. [9]
  • The author writes that RICE scores should not be used as a hard and fast rule, because one project may be a dependency for another or a feature may be table stakes for selling to certain customers. [9]

Example 4 of 5

NASA budgets major projects at a 70 percent joint cost and schedule confidence level

NASADocumented in official docs

NASA's procedural requirements for space flight projects (NPR 7120.5F) require a joint cost and schedule confidence level, or JCL, for larger projects. A NASA primer and Appendix J of the NASA Cost Estimating Handbook explain how the analysis is done. [17][18][19]

The problem

Appendix J traces the policy to reports by the US General Accounting Office from 2002 onwards, which identified incomplete cost-risk assessment and flawed initial programme planning among the major causes of cost growth. NASA's first probabilistic guidance, in 2006, was an instruction to budget at a stated confidence level, first 65 percent for the Constellation programme and then 70 percent as standard practice. The appendix lists three problems with it: no formally documented policy, schedule risk left out of the confidence statement, and a tedious reconciliation between project and independent estimates. The January 2009 policy update, which introduced the joint cost and schedule level, was written to address them.

Where this topic fits

The analysis is required at a commitment point. Under NPR 7120.5F, projects with an estimated life-cycle cost above $250 million produce a JCL at Key Decision Point C. Appendix J describes this as the point where the decision authority determines the confidence level at which the agency commits to deliver the project. The result is used to set the budget and the reserves, and it is calculated again if the project is rebaselined.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Build a summary schedule as a logic network, in which every activity has predecessors and successors and the end milestone is linked in.
  2. Load cost onto the schedule activities, separating time-dependent cost, which grows with duration, from time-independent cost.
  3. Add the project's risk list. Each discrete risk has a probability of occurring and an impact, and is mapped to the activities it affects.
  4. Apply uncertainty to durations and costs, typically as a three-point estimate of low, most likely and high values, often modelled as a triangular distribution.
  5. Run a simulation. Each run gives one cost and schedule pair, and the JCL for a target is the percentage of runs that fall at or below both the cost target and the schedule target.
  6. Plan and budget the project at a 70 percent JCL unless the decision authority approves and documents another level, with funding at no less than the equivalent of a 50 percent JCL.

Why this approach fits

Cost and schedule are coupled, because a late project keeps paying its team, so two separate confidence figures overstate the chance of meeting both. A joint figure with a stated percentage makes the commitment explicit: budgeting at 70 percent accepts in advance that some projects will exceed their plan and sizes the reserves for that. The toy model shows why a plan built from most-likely values is almost never met. Parallel streams finish when the later one does, the ranges stretch further on the late side than on the early side, and risks only add.

Limitation or tradeoff

The result is only as good as the schedule logic, the three-point inputs and the risk list. Appendix J warns about double counting between listed risks and general uncertainty, and says estimates from subject-matter experts are generally not considered a best-practice method. A risk nobody listed is not in the model, so a 70 percent JCL is not a 70 percent guarantee. The analysis is also heavy. For a three-week engagement the proportionate version is a three-point table and a short simulation like the one in this lesson's worked example. The thresholds quoted are from NPR 7120.5F as accessed and may differ in later versions.

What the sources document

  • NASA's JCL primer defines a joint confidence level as an integrated uncertainty analysis of cost and schedule, whose result indicates the probability that a project's cost will be equal to or less than the targeted cost and that its schedule will be equal to or less than the targeted finish. [17]
  • The primer lists four key inputs (schedule, cost, risk and uncertainty) and a five-step process, which is to develop a summary analysis schedule, load cost onto the schedule activities, incorporate the risk list, conduct uncertainty analysis, and calculate and view results. [17][19]
  • NPR 7120.5F requires single-project programs and projects with an estimated life-cycle cost greater than 250 million US dollars to develop a cost-loaded schedule and perform a risk-informed probabilistic analysis that produces a JCL at Key Decision Point C. [18]
  • The same section says mission directorates shall plan and budget these projects based on a 70 percent JCL or as approved by the decision authority, that a JCL approved at less than 70 percent shall be justified and documented, and that funding shall be no less than the equivalent of a 50 percent JCL or as approved by the decision authority.   • Appendix J says uncertainty is typically modelled using a three-point estimate of low, most likely and high values. It defines a risk separately, as an event not in the baseline plan that is characterised by a probability of occurring and an expected impact. [19]

The problem

HealthCare.gov had to open on 1 October 2013. The report says that changes to policy and business requirements continued well into 2013 and compressed the time for the technical build, and that the nine months from January to September 2013 provided very little time for what remained, including testing. Through most of that year CMS kept the same plans for a full launch. The report calls this path dependency: continuing to follow the same plan and schedule instead of changing course as circumstances warranted.

Where this topic fits

The report documents two rounds of scoping by the same organisation. The first was a set of "reprioritization" meetings from mid-August 2013, less than two months before launch. The second was a three-day "ruthless prioritization" session in April 2014, part of planning that began the day after the first enrollment period closed, seven months before the second one opened.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Through most of 2013, keep the plan for a full launch on 1 October while business requirements and technical specifications are still changing.
  2. From mid-August 2013, hold reprioritization meetings to find the minimum that can be delivered. Reduce the site to accepting information, determining eligibility and selecting a plan, and delay the Spanish-language site, the small-business site and the anonymous shopper tool.
  3. Launch on the fixed date without finishing functionality testing or completing a full end-to-end test. Outages begin within two hours.
  4. After the recovery, start planning the next period the day after the first one closes. Managers list their technology needs, technical and business staff evaluate the people, time and other resources each one needs, and the group debates and cuts about half.
  5. Lock the decisions and measure progress against them. Set a "pens down" date of 7 October 2014, after which the only system changes allowed are fixes for problems found in testing.
  6. Release new functionality only when it is ready, testing the new consumer application through a soft launch before open enrollment, and accept that some items arrive later than planned.

Why this approach fits

The case isolates the timing and deliberateness of scope cuts, because one organisation did it both ways on one system. In 2013 the launch date was fixed and the plan for a full launch was kept for most of the year, so testing absorbed the slip, and the cuts that finally came were rushed and too shallow to matter. In 2014 the report says the timeframe and resources were fixed, so scope was the variable: it was reduced by people who had costed each item, and quality was protected by a freeze date and a rule of releasing only what was ready.

Limitation or tradeoff

Cutting has costs that last. The automated financial management system was cut in both rounds, and the report notes that the manual process used in its place was later found by an OIG audit to lack effective internal controls. The readiness rule also meant CMS did not always deliver on schedule; a new account creation system was not launched as planned. The report was written afterwards from interviews and documents, and it credits the recovery to several factors together, including a "badgeless" culture in which CMS staff and contractors worked as one team, so the effect of prioritisation alone cannot be separated out.

4Common mistakes

  1. Calling every requirement a must-have.

    Why it causes problems
    With no item that can be dropped there is no contingency, so the first slip lands on the date or on testing. The customer also loses the chance to choose what matters most while there is still time to choose.
    How to recognise it
    The expected effort of the must-haves is well over 60% of the time box, and nobody can name the first thing that would be dropped.
    How to fix it
    Ask of each item what happens if it is not delivered, and whether a manual workaround exists. Relabel until the must-haves fit in roughly 60% of the box, and list the first three things to drop.

  2. Building layer by layer, with all the data work first, then the logic, then the screens.

    Why it causes problems
    Nothing can be demonstrated until the last layer exists, integration problems surface at the end, and the customer cannot correct a misunderstanding while it is still cheap.
    How to recognise it
    At the end of the first week there is nothing a user could click or run, and progress is reported as a percentage of tasks finished.
    How to fix it
    Pick one case and wire it through every layer with stubs and hard-coded values where needed. Demo it in the first week, then widen it.

  3. Quoting the sum of the most likely values as the estimate.

    Why it causes problems
    Ranges stretch further on the late side, so the total of the most likely values sits below the expected total. In the worked example the full list sums to 19.5 days, and the chance of finishing it by then is under one in five.
    How to recognise it
    The plan shows single figures with no ranges, and nobody can say what the pessimistic case is or how likely the date is.
    How to fix it
    Give three points per item and report the expected total, the chance of fitting the box, and the day by which you are 80% likely to finish.

  4. Leaving the item with the biggest unknown until the end.

    Why it causes problems
    The fact that could invalidate the plan is discovered when no time is left to change course, and the comfortable early progress turns out to have been on work that was never at risk.
    How to recognise it
    Every task in the first week is something the team has done before, and a risk has been marked as to be confirmed for more than a few days.
    How to fix it
    Spike the unknown in the first days with a time box and a yes-or-no question, and file slow external requests such as access approvals on day one.

  5. Cutting quality quietly instead of cutting scope.

    Why it causes problems
    Skipped tests, a missing evaluation set or loosened permissions are invisible until acceptance or production, when they cost the most. The 2013 HealthCare.gov launch went ahead without a completed end-to-end test.
    How to recognise it
    Test, evaluation and security tasks are scheduled last and get shorter each week, and someone says the system will be hardened later.
    How to fix it
    Put the acceptance checks inside the first slice and set a freeze date after which only fixes are allowed. When time runs short, drop a feature and say so.

  6. Writing what is in scope and leaving what is out unstated.

    Why it causes problems
    The customer assumes the unstated cases are covered. They come back as defects or late additions, and each one is argued about separately under time pressure.
    How to recognise it
    The scope note has no section for what is not included, and a stakeholder says they assumed it would also do something.
    How to fix it
    List the items that are not in this phase, with the manual fallback for each, and read the list aloud when you play the scope back to the customer.

CANDIDATES = {
    "live policy lookup from the claims system": (250, 2, 0.8, 3, 6, 15),
    "answer inside the team chat tool": (300, 1, 0.8, 2, 4, 9),
    "endorsement documents (25 more PDFs)": (120, 1, 0.8, 2, 3, 6),
    "thumbs up/down feedback log": (300, 0.5, 1.0, 0.5, 1, 2),
    "answers in Welsh": (15, 2, 0.5, 2, 4, 8),
}


def pert(o, m, p):
    """Expected days and standard deviation of one three-point estimate."""
    return (o + 4 * m + p) / 6, (p - o) / 6


def fit_probability(estimates):
    """Expected total and chance that the estimates fit in the box (normal approximation)."""
    mean = sum(pert(*e)[0] for e in estimates)
    sd = math.sqrt(sum(pert(*e)[1] ** 2 for e in estimates))
    z = (BOX_DAYS - mean) / sd
    return mean, 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))


def rice(reach, impact, confidence, o, m, p):
    return reach * impact * confidence / pert(o, m, p)[0]


plan = list(MUST.values())
mean, prob = fit_probability(plan)
print(f"must-haves: {mean:.2f} days = {mean / BOX_DAYS:.0%} of the box, fit {prob:.1%}")

ranked = sorted(CANDIDATES.items(), key=lambda item: rice(*item[1]), reverse=True)
for name, (reach, impact, confidence, o, m, p) in ranked:
    score = rice(reach, impact, confidence, o, m, p)
    mean, prob = fit_probability(plan + [(o, m, p)])
    if prob >= THRESHOLD:
        plan = plan + [(o, m, p)]
        verdict = "ADD "
    else:
        verdict = "SKIP"
    print(f"{verdict} score={score:6.1f} fit-if-added={prob:5.1%} total={mean:5.2f}d  {name}")

mean, prob = fit_probability(plan)
print(f"plan: {len(plan)} items, {mean:.2f} expected days, fit {prob:.1%}")

Output:

text
must-haves: 12.00 days = 60% of the box, fit 100.0%
ADD  score= 138.5 fit-if-added=100.0% total=13.08d  thumbs up/down feedback log
SKIP score=  57.1 fit-if-added=48.7% total=20.08d  live policy lookup from the claims system
ADD  score=  53.3 fit-if-added=89.5% total=17.58d  answer inside the team chat tool
SKIP score=  28.8 fit-if-added=32.6% total=20.92d  endorsement documents (25 more PDFs)
SKIP score=   3.5 fit-if-added=18.8% total=21.92d  answers in Welsh
plan: 5 items, 17.58 expected days, fit 89.5%

Reading the result. The script's verdict is the start of a conversation, not the end of one. The live policy lookup is worth slightly more per day than the chat tool (57.1 against 53.3) and is skipped only because of its size and its range of 3 to 15 days. That width is an unknown, most likely API access to the claims system. A one-day spike could narrow the estimate. If it came back as 4, 6, 8, the lookup would give 19.08 expected days and a 70.9% chance of fitting on top of the must-haves and the feedback log: better, but still under the threshold, and there would be no room left for the chat tool. So the real choice to put to the customer is "chat tool or live lookup, not both in this box", and the spike is what makes that choice an informed one.

Notice also what the script cannot see. It treats items as independent, it knows nothing about dependencies between them, and its scores inherit every weakness of the reach and impact guesses. Use it to prepare the discussion, then adjust by hand and write down why.

Check your answer

  • Do your must-haves come to exactly 12.00 expected days and 60% of the box?
  • Is the live policy lookup ranked second by score and still skipped, with a fit-if-added of 48.7%?
  • Is the chat tool added after the lookup was skipped, giving a final fit of 89.5%?
  • If you change the threshold to 0.45, does the lookup get added and the chat tool get skipped?
  • Does your script leave the MUST and CANDIDATES data unchanged after it has run?
