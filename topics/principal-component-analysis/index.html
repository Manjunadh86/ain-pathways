Dimensionality Reduction and PCA

Principal component analysis (PCA) rotates a table of numeric features onto new, uncorrelated axes ordered by how much of the data's variance each one carries, so that the first few axes can stand in for the original columns. Libraries usually compute it from the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the centred data.

1Overview

Why it matters

Real feature tables are rarely as wide as they look. Pixels, sensor channels, gene counts and yields at neighbouring maturities move together, so a table with hundreds of columns often has only a handful of independent directions in it. PCA finds those directions, which makes it the usual first step before clustering, nearest-neighbour search or plotting, and a standard way to compress or de-noise numeric data.

Most bad PCA results come from three mistakes. The columns are not scaled, so the column with the biggest units wins. The PCA is fitted before the train/test split, so test rows shape the axes. Or variance is treated as importance, when a direction that explains little of the spread can still carry the signal. Separately, t-SNE and UMAP plots are routinely over-read as if distances and cluster sizes were measurements. Data scientists, engineers who build vector-search or feature pipelines, and analysts who read embedding plots all need to know where each tool is reliable.

Key terms

Principal component
One of the new axes PCA finds, a unit-length direction in feature space along which the centred data vary most once the earlier components are accounted for. The axes are perpendicular to each other.
Variance
The average squared distance of values from their mean (the sample version divides by n−1). PCA treats variance as the information worth keeping.
Covariance matrix
A square table with the variance of each feature on the diagonal and the covariance of each pair of features elsewhere. Its eigenvectors are the principal axes.
Scores
The coordinates of each row along the principal axes, found by projecting the centred row onto them. Scores on different components are uncorrelated.
Loadings
The entries of a principal axis, showing how much each original feature contributes to that component. Their signs are arbitrary, because an axis and its negative are the same line.
Explained variance ratio
The fraction of the total variance carried by one component, which is its eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues. The ratios of all components add up to 1.
Singular value decomposition (SVD)
A factorisation of any matrix X into U, Σ and Vᵀ. For centred data the columns of V are the principal axes, and the squared singular values divided by n−1 are the variances.
Scree (elbow) plot
A plot of the explained variance of each component in order. The bend after which components add little is a common but subjective guide to how many to keep.
Reconstruction error
The squared distance between a row and the row rebuilt from only its first k scores. For PCA the total over all rows equals the sum of the squared singular values that were dropped.
Whitening
Rescaling every score to unit variance so that all kept components have the same spread. It discards the information about how large each component's variance was.

2Explanation

Introduction

You already know that two columns can carry the same information. A table that holds a height in centimetres and the same height in inches has one real dimension and one redundant column. Real tables contain a softer version of this: the length and width of leaves rise and fall together, neighbouring pixels in a photograph are almost the same shade, and two temperature sensors in one room differ mainly by noise. Principal component analysis (PCA) is the standard way to find that redundancy and remove it.

The method looks for a new set of axes. The first axis, the first principal component, points in the direction along which the data are most spread out. The second is the most spread-out direction that is perpendicular to the first, and so on. Spread is measured as variance. Each row is then described by its position along the new axes, its scores, instead of by its original columns. If most of the variance sits on the first few axes, keeping only those gives a shorter description of every row that still reproduces most of the differences between rows.

Picture six leaves measured for length and width. The six points form a diagonal band. An axis drawn along the band captures most of the differences between leaves, and the short axis across the band captures the rest. Dropping the short axis turns two numbers per leaf into one, at a cost that the method reports exactly.

PCA does four jobs in practice: it shrinks or de-noises a model's input, it compresses vectors before they are stored or searched, it gives a quick two-dimensional look at a dataset, and it removes correlation between inputs. This lesson covers the geometry, the computation through the SVD, how to choose how many components to keep, the preparation that decides whether the result means anything, and the cases where PCA is the wrong tool. A last section covers t-SNE and UMAP, two non-linear methods that make good pictures of high-dimensional data and poor measurements of it. It builds on linear algebra for ML for eigenvectors and the SVD, and on feature engineering and scaling for standardisation.

How it works

Keep the directions where the data vary

Put the data in a matrix XXX with nnn rows (samples) and ddd columns (features), and subtract each column's mean to get the centred matrix XcX_cXc​. For a unit-length direction w\mathbf{w}w, the numbers XcwX_c\mathbf{w}Xc​w are the positions of the rows along that direction, and their variance is

Var⁡(Xcw)=w⊤S w,S=1n−1 Xc⊤Xc\operatorname{Var}(X_c\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\top S\,\mathbf{w}, \qquad S = \frac{1}{n-1}\,X_c^\top X_cVar(Xc​w)=w⊤Sw,S=n−11​Xc⊤​Xc​

SSS is the covariance matrix, with variances on its diagonal and covariances between pairs of features elsewhere. The first principal component is the unit vector w1\mathbf{w}_1w1​ that makes w⊤S w\mathbf{w}^\top S\,\mathbf{w}w⊤Sw as large as possible. Jolliffe and Cadima (opens in a new tab) show that this vector is the eigenvector of SSS with the largest eigenvalue λ1\lambda_1λ1​, and that λ1\lambda_1λ1​ is the variance along it. The second component maximises the same quantity among directions perpendicular to the first, which gives the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue, and so on. The eigenvalues add up to the total variance, the scores on different components are uncorrelated, and the entries of each eigenvector are the loadings of the original features.

Computing it with the SVD

Forming SSS squares the data, which can lose precision when columns are nearly dependent, so libraries usually factorise XcX_cXc​ directly: Xc=UΣV⊤X_c = U\Sigma V^\topXc​=UΣV⊤. The columns of VVV are the principal axes, the scores are XcV=UΣX_c V = U\SigmaXc​V=UΣ, and the variance of component kkk is λk=σk2/(n−1)\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2/(n-1)λk​=σk2​/(n−1). The review states the same equivalence, and sklearn.decomposition.PCA (opens in a new tab) implements it: it centres but does not scale the data, applies an SVD, and exposes components_, explained_variance_, explained_variance_ratio_ and singular_values_.

The solver depends on the shape of the data. In scikit-learn 1.9.1 the default auto policy picks covariance_eigh (an eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix) when there are fewer than 1000 features and more than ten times as many samples as features, a randomized truncated SVD when the data exceed 500 by 500 and fewer than 80% of the components are requested, and the exact SVD otherwise. The user guide (opens in a new tab) gives the cost as about O(nmax⁡2 ncomponents)O(n_{\max}^2\,n_{\text{components}})O(nmax2​ncomponents​) for the randomized solver against O(nmax⁡2 nmin⁡)O(n_{\max}^2\,n_{\min})O(nmax2​nmin​) for the exact one, and adds IncrementalPCA for data that do not fit in memory and TruncatedSVD, which skips centring (the PCA documentation points to it for sparse data).

Explained variance and how many components to keep

The explained variance ratio of component kkk is λk/∑jλj\lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_jλk​/∑j​λj​, and its running total says how much of the spread the first kkk components keep. Rebuilding each row from its first kkk scores leaves a total squared error equal to the sum of the squared singular values that were dropped, so the ratios also measure what compression costs. Three ways to pick kkk:

  • Cumulative variance. Keep enough components to pass a target such as 90% or 95%. Jolliffe and Cadima call 70% a common but subjective cut-off. In scikit-learn, a float n_components between 0 and 1 does this with the exact solver (svd_solver='full').
  • Elbow plot. Plot the ratios in order and look for the bend. The Seurat tutorial (opens in a new tab) in the examples picks 10 components from an elbow and encourages readers to repeat the analysis with 10, 15 or even 50.
  • Downstream score. Treat kkk as a hyperparameter and cross-validate it. scikit-learn's digits example (opens in a new tab) searches n_components over 5, 15, 30, 45 and 60 in front of a logistic regression. Only this rule knows what the components are for.

Prepare the data: centre, scale, split

PCA maximises variance, so units decide the answer. A column that varies more only because it is measured in larger units takes over the first component. In scikit-learn's wine example (opens in a new tab) the proline column dominates the first component of the raw data, and a logistic regression after PCA scores 35.19% on the test set, against 96.30% after the columns are standardised. Standardising turns the covariance matrix into the correlation matrix, whose components do not change when units change.

Fit the scaler and the PCA on training rows only, then apply them to validation, test and new rows. Putting both in a scikit-learn Pipeline makes cross-validation refit them inside every fold. Fitting on all rows lets the test rows shape the axes, a form of the leakage described in train/test splits and cross-validation.

Using it in code

python
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

reducer = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=0.95, svd_solver="full"))
reducer.fit(X_train)                    # scaler and axes are learned from training rows only
Z_train = reducer.transform(X_train)    # scores, shape (n_train, k)
Z_test = reducer.transform(X_test)      # the same scaler and axes applied to unseen rows
print(reducer[-1].n_components_, reducer[-1].explained_variance_ratio_.sum())

# Rebuild approximate rows in the original units to see what was lost
X_back = reducer[0].inverse_transform(reducer[-1].inverse_transform(Z_test))

PCA(whiten=True) additionally rescales each score to unit variance. The user guide notes this can help models such as RBF-kernel SVMs and k-means, at the price of discarding how large each component's variance was.

What PCA cannot see

  • Variance is not relevance. PCA never looks at the target. In scikit-learn's PCR versus PLS example (opens in a new tab) the target follows a low-variance direction, so a regression on one principal component scores R2=−0.026R^2 = -0.026R2=−0.026, while a method that uses the target scores 0.658.
  • Only straight-line structure. PCA finds a flat subspace. Data on a curved surface need more components than the surface's true dimension.
  • Outliers pull the axes. Jolliffe and Cadima note that PCA is by its nature sensitive to outliers.

t-SNE and UMAP: for looking, not for measuring

t-SNE and UMAP place each row on a two- or three-dimensional map so that near neighbours stay near. They are non-linear, and they keep neighbourhoods at the expense of everything else.

  • scikit-learn's TSNE (opens in a new tab) recommends reducing very wide data with PCA (TruncatedSVD for sparse data) to about 50 dimensions first, suggests a perplexity between 5 and 50, and offers fit_transform but no transform, so new rows cannot be placed on an existing map.
  • The Distill article on t-SNE (opens in a new tab) shows that cluster sizes carry no meaning, that gaps between clusters may not either, and that a low perplexity can make clumps from random noise.
  • UMAP is stochastic (opens in a new tab): runs differ unless random_state is set, which switches off some multithreading. It has a transform method (opens in a new tab), provided the new data resemble the training data. Its FAQ (opens in a new tab) lists data visualisation, clustering and classification as downstream tasks, warns that its uniform-density assumption means density is not preserved well, and prefers HDBSCAN-style methods to k-means if you cluster its output at all.

Use these maps to generate hypotheses, then check them with PCA scores or the original features. The Seurat tutorial draws the same line: it clusters on the principal components, draws the cells with UMAP, and warns against drawing conclusions from the visualisation alone.

The first script uses only the standard library, so it runs anywhere.

python
import math

length = [9, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18]   # cm
width = [5, 4, 4, 7, 9, 7]         # cm
n = len(length)

mx, my = sum(length) / n, sum(width) / n
dx = [v - mx for v in length]
dy = [v - my for v in width]

sxx = sum(a * a for a in dx) / (n - 1)
syy = sum(b * b for b in dy) / (n - 1)
sxy = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(dx, dy)) / (n - 1)

# Eigenvalues of [[sxx, sxy], [sxy, syy]] from the quadratic formula
half_sum = (sxx + syy) / 2
radius = math.sqrt(((sxx - syy) / 2) ** 2 + sxy ** 2)
lam1, lam2 = half_sum + radius, half_sum - radius

# Unit eigenvector for lam1: (sxx - lam1) * vx + sxy * vy = 0, so (vx, vy) is proportional to (sxy, lam1 - sxx)
vx, vy = sxy, lam1 - sxx
norm = math.hypot(vx, vy)
vx, vy = vx / norm, vy / norm

scores = [a * vx + b * vy for a, b in zip(dx, dy)]
print(f"means: {mx}, {my}")
print(f"covariance: [[{sxx}, {sxy}], [{sxy}, {syy}]]")
print(f"eigenvalues: {lam1:.3f}, {lam2:.3f}; PC1 share: {lam1 / (lam1 + lam2):.4f}")
print(f"PC1 direction: ({vx:.3f}, {vy:.3f})")
print("PC1 scores:", [round(s, 3) for s in scores])

Edge cases and limitations

Axis signs are arbitrary, and tied variances make axes unstable
An axis and its negative describe the same line, so two libraries or two runs can return opposite signs for the same component, and a sign should never be interpreted. When two eigenvalues are almost equal, the individual axes are not well determined and only the plane they span is stable. NCAR's guide to EOF analysis notes that eigenvalues are not necessarily distinct because of sampling issues. Compare components only when their variances are clearly separated.
More columns than rows gives at most n-1 useful components
After centring, n rows span at most n-1 dimensions, so a table with 100 samples and 10,000 features has at most 99 components with any variance, and the rest are exactly zero. scikit-learn's default keeps min(n_samples, n_features) components. A component count close to n usually means the fit is describing the sample rather than the population, so check the elbow on held-out data.
Refitting changes the axes and the scores
NCAR warns that each time more data are used to compute the leading EOF, the individual PC values will probably change. In production, a model trained on one PCA's scores breaks silently if the PCA is refitted and the axes rotate or flip. Persist the fitted scaler and PCA with the model, and refit them only together with the downstream model.
Centring destroys sparsity
Subtracting column means turns a sparse matrix of word counts into a dense one that may not fit in memory. scikit-learn's PCA supports sparse input only with some solvers, and its documentation points to TruncatedSVD, which skips centring and is how latent semantic analysis is computed. On data with very different column means the first TruncatedSVD component can mostly encode the mean rather than a direction of variation.
One outlier can rotate the first axis
Variance is a sum of squared distances, so a single extreme row contributes far more than a typical one and can pull the first component toward itself. A telltale sign is a PC1 whose explained variance changes sharply when one row is removed, or a single score far larger than the rest. Inspect or winsorise extreme rows, or use a robust variant, before reading the components.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • Many numeric features are correlated, and a downstream model, distance calculation or index suffers from the redundancy or from the cost of the extra columns.
  • You need a quick two- or three-dimensional look at the structure of numeric data, and a linear view is an honest first step before trying t-SNE or UMAP.
  • You want to compress or de-noise vectors, such as embeddings or image pixels, and can measure the loss as reconstruction error or as the recall of a downstream search.
  • You need uncorrelated inputs, for example for a regression whose features are nearly collinear, and can cross-validate the number of components.

Think twice when

  • The columns are mostly independent, so the explained variance falls off slowly and no small set of axes keeps the structure.
  • You must explain each model input in the units of the original features, or the signal sits in a rare, low-variance direction that PCA would discard.
  • The data lie on a strongly curved surface, or are mostly categorical or sparse counts, where PCA's linear, mean-centred geometry fits poorly.

A simpler alternative. Dropping redundant columns, or letting an L1 or L2 penalty handle collinearity (see regularization), keeps the features interpretable and is often enough. Reach for PCA when the redundancy is spread across many columns, or when the goal is compression rather than selection.

Key takeaways

  • Principal components are the perpendicular directions of greatest variance in the centred data, and the scores on them are uncorrelated.
  • Libraries usually compute PCA from the SVD of the centred matrix, and each component's variance is its squared singular value divided by n-1.
  • Standardise columns that have different units, and fit the scaler and the PCA on training rows only, or the first component will follow the units and the test set will leak into the axes.
  • Choose the number of components from a cumulative-variance target, an elbow plot and above all a cross-validated downstream score, because variance is not the same as relevance to the target.
  • t-SNE and UMAP make pictures, not measurements, so cluster sizes and gaps should not be trusted, and clustering or modelling is better done on PCA scores or the original features.

Example 1 of 5

Seurat guided clustering: PCA before clustering, UMAP only to draw 2,700 blood cells

Seurat (Satija Lab)Documented in official docs

The Seurat R toolkit's guided clustering tutorial analyses about 2,700 peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 10X Genomics, from raw counts to a UMAP plot. The tutorial page documents each step and the commands used. [12]

The problem

A single-cell RNA-seq experiment measures thousands of genes in each of thousands of cells, and the count of any one gene is noisy. The analyst needs to group cells with similar expression profiles into cell types, find the genes that distinguish the groups, and draw the result in two dimensions.

Where this topic fits

PCA sits between the gene-level preprocessing (filtering cells, normalising, choosing variable genes, scaling) and everything that compares cells. The neighbour graph that defines the clusters and the UMAP picture are both computed from the principal-component scores, not from the 2,000 gene values.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Read the 10X counts into a Seurat object (13,714 genes detected in at least three of the 2,700 cells), then keep cells with more than 200 and fewer than 2,500 detected genes and under 5% mitochondrial counts.
  2. Normalise, then select the 2,000 most variable genes, which the tutorial says helps highlight biological signal.
  3. Scale every gene to mean 0 and variance 1, so that highly expressed genes do not dominate (the tutorial passes all genes to ScaleData), then run PCA on the 2,000 variable genes, so that each cell is described by PC scores instead of 2,000 noisy gene values.
  4. Read an elbow plot of variance explained per component to decide how many PCs to keep. The tutorial settles on 10 and encourages checking other values.
  5. Build the neighbour graph and cluster on those 10 PCs. At resolution 0.5 the tutorial's output reports nine communities among the 2,638 cells that passed filtering.
  6. Run UMAP on the same 10 PCs to draw the cells in two dimensions and check that the clusters co-localise, without drawing conclusions from the picture alone.

Why this approach fits

Genes that mark the same cell type are correlated across cells, so a few principal axes collect that shared signal while the independent noise of each gene is averaged out. Distances between cells are then measured in a ten-dimensional space that is cheaper and less noisy than the 2,000-dimensional gene space. PCA is also fast, and its elbow plot gives the analyst a visible way to set how many dimensions to keep.

Limitation or tradeoff

The number of PCs is a judgement call that the tutorial itself says to revisit. PCA is linear and unsupervised, so a rare cell type whose signal carries little of the total variance can sit in components that an elbow cut removes. This is a typical risk, not something the tutorial reports. The UMAP drawing preserves local neighbourhoods but not global relationships, so it should be used to look at the clusters, not to define or interpret them.

What the sources document

  • The tutorial analyses 2,700 single cells (PBMCs from 10X Genomics sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500), keeps cells with more than 200 and fewer than 2,500 detected features and under 5% mitochondrial content, and by default selects 2,000 variable features for downstream analysis such as PCA. [12]
  • PCA is run on the scaled data using the variable features, and the tutorial says that to overcome the extensive technical noise in any single feature, Seurat clusters cells based on their PCA scores. [12]
  • An elbow plot ranks the principal components by percentage of variance explained. The tutorial finds an elbow around PC 9 to 10, uses 10 PCs, and encourages users to repeat downstream analyses with a different number of PCs (10, 15 or even 50). [12]
  • FindNeighbors and RunUMAP both use dims 1:10, FindClusters is run at resolution 0.5, and the output reports nine communities among 2,638 cells. [12]
  • UMAP and t-SNE are presented as ways to visualise and explore the data that preserve local distances but often not global relationships, and the tutorial advises using them for visualisation while avoiding biological conclusions drawn solely from them. [12]

Population structure and GWAS stratification: PCA on genotype matrices

Documented in a paper

Patterson, Price and Reich (PLoS Genetics, 2006) apply PCA to genotype data to detect population structure and to test how many axes are significant. Price et al. (Nature Genetics, 2006), which Patterson et al. cite as the parallel paper on medical genetics, use principal components to correct genome-wide association studies for ancestry. The method claims rest on the full text of the first paper. For the second, only the abstract record could be read, because the Nature page cannot be fetched automatically. [13][14]

The problem

A genetic dataset records a genotype for each of m individuals at n markers, often hundreds of thousands. Two questions follow. Does the sample contain subpopulations, and how many axes of ancestry are real rather than noise? And in a disease study, how can ancestry differences between cases and controls be prevented from producing spurious associations at markers whose frequency differs between ancestral populations?

Where this topic fits

PCA is the structure-finding step. It runs on the individuals-by-markers matrix after each marker is centred and rescaled, its leading eigenvectors become ancestry coordinates for each individual, and a significance test decides whether structure is present. In an association study those coordinates are then used to adjust the analysis.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Arrange the genotypes as an individuals-by-markers matrix and subtract each marker's mean.
  2. Rescale each marker by a factor based on its estimated allele frequency, which the paper motivates by how fast genetic drift changes allele frequency. This plays the role of standardising columns.
  3. Compute the individuals-by-individuals covariance matrix and its eigenvectors. With K distinct populations the paper's theory predicts K-1 large eigenvalues, and the rest stay near the noise level.
  4. Test the largest eigenvalue against the Tracy-Widom distribution to get a p-value for the presence of structure, instead of judging a scree plot by eye.
  5. Use the leading eigenvectors as continuous ancestry coordinates for each individual.
  6. In an association study, Price et al. use principal components to model ancestry differences between cases and controls, so that the correction is specific to each candidate marker's frequency variation across ancestral populations.

Why this approach fits

Allele counts at different markers are correlated through shared ancestry, so the matrix has a few strong directions that reflect population history. PCA needs no population labels and returns continuous coordinates, which can describe gradients as well as clear groups. Because individuals are far fewer than markers, the m by m covariance matrix is small enough to decompose directly, and the Tracy-Widom test turns the elbow decision into a p-value.

Limitation or tradeoff

The method finds whatever dominates the variance of the rescaled matrix. Ancestry is the usual cause, but an axis should be inspected before it is used as a stand-in for ancestry. Below the detection threshold that the paper describes, real differences between populations stay invisible, and the work grows with the square of the number of individuals. In a typical implementation, ancestry differences that lie outside the leading axes are not adjusted for.

What the sources document

  • The method treats genotypes as a matrix with individuals as rows and biallelic markers as columns, subtracts each column's mean, normalises each marker using its estimated allele frequency, and takes the eigenvectors of the resulting individuals-by-individuals covariance matrix. [13]
  • Eigenvectors with large eigenvalues expose non-random population structure, and the theory predicts K-1 large eigenvalues when the sample contains K populations. [13]
  • The significance of the largest eigenvalue is tested with the Tracy-Widom distribution, and for a fixed amount of data a divergence between two populations below a threshold is essentially undetectable while a little above it detection is easy. [13]
  • The dominant cost is the matrix product, proportional to m squared times n: the paper reports four seconds for 100 individuals and 100,000 markers on a fast workstation and estimates about 2.5 hours for 2,000 individuals and 500,000 markers on the same single processor. [13]
  • Price et al. describe a method that uses principal components analysis to explicitly model ancestry differences between cases and controls, to detect and correct population stratification on a genome-wide scale, with a correction specific to each candidate marker's frequency variation across ancestral populations. [14]

Example 3 of 5

NCAR's PC-based North Atlantic Oscillation index from EOF analysis of sea-level pressure

NCAR Climate Data GuideDocumented in official docs

NCAR's Climate Data Guide documents EOF analysis, the climate-science name for PCA, and publishes the Hurrell PC-based North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) index, which is the time series of the leading EOF of Atlantic sea-level pressure anomalies. [15][16]

The problem

The NAO is a pattern in atmospheric pressure tied to the Icelandic low and the Azores high. Climatologists need one number per time step that tracks it. The raw material is a gridded pressure field with thousands of cells that move together, and the documentation says PC-based indices can be less noisy than indices built from fixed station records.

Where this topic fits

EOF analysis is the construction of the index. In the generic EOF recipe that the guide describes, pressure anomalies are weighted by area, the covariance of the field is decomposed, the leading eigenvector is the spatial NAO pattern, and the projection of the anomalies on it, a principal component, is the index. The NAO page itself states only that the index is the time series of the leading EOF and does not give these details.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Take gridded sea-level pressure anomalies, meaning departures from the mean state, over the Atlantic sector from 20 to 80 degrees north and 90 degrees west to 40 degrees east. The NAO page says the index uses the NCAR Sea Level Pressure dataset.
  2. In a typical EOF workflow, weight each grid cell by the square root of the cosine of its latitude, the standard area weighting named in the guide, so that crowded high-latitude cells do not dominate the variance. The NAO page does not say which weighting the index uses.
  3. Decompose the covariance of the weighted anomalies. The eigenvectors are the EOFs, which are spatial patterns, and the eigenvalues give the variance of each mode.
  4. Project the weighted anomalies on the leading EOF. The resulting time series, the principal component, is the amplitude of the pattern at each time and is published as the index.
  5. Recompute when more data arrive, remembering that the individual PC values are likely to change.

Why this approach fits

A pressure field has thousands of grid cells, but the NAO is one coherent large-scale pattern, which is what the leading eigenvector extracts. Projecting every time step onto the pattern uses all cells at once, which is why the documentation says PC-based indices can be less noisy than indices tied to fixed station locations. PCA also returns the spatial map, so the index can be shown together with its pattern.

Limitation or tradeoff

The documentation lists the limits. The orthogonality constraint forces each later mode to be perpendicular to the first, which can make later modes hard to give a physical meaning. The patterns depend on the domain chosen, and eigenvalues can be nearly tied because of sampling. The PC values change whenever the EOF is recomputed with more data, and the PC-based indices do not reach as far back as station-based ones. The NAO page also lists dependence on the weaknesses of the source dataset and its gridding scheme.

What the sources document

  • EOF analysis is known in statistics as principal component analysis. It is computed from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the anomaly covariance matrix of a spatially weighted field, with square root of cosine of latitude as the standard weighting. [15]
  • The principal component time series of each mode is found by projecting the eigenvectors onto the spatially weighted anomalies, which gives the amplitude of each mode over the period of record. [15]
  • The guide names two limits on physical interpretation, the orthogonality constraint and the dependence of the patterns on the domain, and notes that eigenvalues are not necessarily distinct because of sampling. [15]
  • The PC-based NAO indices are the time series of the leading EOF of sea-level pressure anomalies over the Atlantic sector, 20 to 80 degrees north and 90 degrees west to 40 degrees east. [16]
  • The page states that every time additional data are used to compute the EOF, the individual PC values will likely change, and that the PC-based indices are not available as far back as station-based ones. [16]

New York Fed staff report: five principal components of Treasury yields as pricing factors

Federal Reserve Bank of New YorkDocumented in a paper

Adrian, Crump and Moench's Staff Report 340, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, prices the term structure of US Treasury yields with linear regressions and builds its pricing factors from principal components of yields. It is a research paper, and the report states that its views are the authors' own. [17]

The problem

Treasury yields at different maturities are highly redundant, yet a pricing model needs a small set of factors. The question is how many principal components are needed. The paper notes that early work found three factors enough to explain the cross-section of yields, while more recent work argued that additional factors are needed to explain Treasury returns.

Where this topic fits

PCA is the first step of the model. Zero-coupon yields are demeaned, principal components are extracted, and the leading K of them become the observable pricing factors used by the paper's three-step regression approach to estimate how risk is priced and to produce term premium estimates.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Assemble 300 monthly observations, from January 1987 to December 2011, of zero-coupon yields for maturities from 3 to 120 months, constructed from fitted Nelson-Siegel-Svensson curves.
  2. Subtract the mean of each maturity's yield and extract principal components of the cross-section. Neighbouring maturities move together, so a few components describe the shape of the curve.
  3. Test how many components the model needs. The authors report that specification tests reject models with fewer factors and favour five principal components.
  4. Feed the five component scores into the paper's three-step regression approach, which estimates how the factors are priced and generates term premium estimates.
  5. Notice that the fifth component, which has negligible contemporaneous effect on the yield curve, still enters the price of level risk with a strongly significant coefficient.

Why this approach fits

Regressing on yields at every maturity would use inputs that are nearly copies of one another. Principal components give a handful of uncorrelated factors that together span nearly the same information, so the regressions stay well-behaved and fast. The first few also have familiar readings: the paper refers to the level factor, the slope factor (the second component) and the curvature factor (the third).

Limitation or tradeoff

Variance order says little about which components matter for returns. The paper's evidence is that a component with a negligible effect on the curve can still predict future excess returns, so K was set by statistical tests rather than by a variance cut-off. The components are also estimated from one sample period, so another period gives different axes.

What the sources document

  • The paper prices the term structure with a three-step linear regression approach and presents specification tests favouring a model with five principal components of yields as factors, which it reports outperforms the Cochrane and Piazzesi four-factor specification out of sample. [17]
  • The baseline data are zero-coupon yields constructed by Gurkaynak, Sack and Wright from fitted Nelson-Siegel-Svensson curves, for maturities from 3 to 120 months, with principal components extracted from those yields and models estimated over 1987:01 to 2011:12, a total of 300 monthly observations. [17]
  • Earlier research by Scheinkman and Litterman and by Garbade pointed out that three factors are enough to explain the cross-sectional variation of yields, while more recent papers emphasise additional factors for explaining Treasury returns. [17]
  • The paper reports that models with fewer factors than the five-factor specification are rejected in specification tests, and that the first five principal components of Treasury yields are needed to explain Treasury returns. [17]
  • The fifth principal component enters the price of level risk with a strongly significant positive coefficient, which the authors say shows that factors with negligible contemporaneous effects on the yield curve can have strong predictive power for future excess returns. [17]

Example 5 of 5

Faiss: PCA as a pre-processing stage in front of a vector-search index

Faiss (facebookresearch)Documented in official docs

The open-source Faiss similarity-search library documents PCA as one of its vector transforms (PCAMatrix) and as a prefix in index factory strings such as PCA80,Flat, in its index factory and pre- and post-processing wiki pages. [18][19]

The problem

Embeddings can have hundreds or thousands of dimensions, and both the memory of an index and the cost of each distance computation grow with the dimension. When vectors must be compressed to a few bytes, the Faiss wiki suggests reducing the dimension with a PCA first. The library needs a way to reduce dimension before the index sees a vector, and to do it identically for stored vectors and for queries.

Where this topic fits

PCA is a transform stage placed in front of the index and wrapped with it in an IndexPreTransform. It is trained on a sample of vectors together with the index, applied to every vector as it is added, and applied to every query before the search.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start with database vectors of dimension d, such as 128-dimensional descriptors, and choose an output dimension, for example 80.
  2. Build the index from a factory string such as "PCA80,Flat", which puts a PCA transform in front of an exhaustive-search index.
  3. Train the index on a sample of vectors. Training learns the PCA matrix as part of the index training.
  4. Add vectors. Each one is multiplied by the PCA matrix and stored in its 80-dimensional form.
  5. Search. Each query passes through the same fitted matrix and is compared with the stored reduced vectors.
  6. Measure the cost of the reduction by comparing results with an exact search on the full vectors for held-out queries.

Why this approach fits

The coordinates of embeddings are usually correlated, so most of the squared distance between two vectors typically lies in the leading principal directions. Keeping those retains most of the geometry that a nearest-neighbour search needs while cutting memory and arithmetic, and PCA is cheap to train and to apply, as it is one matrix multiplication. Because the matrix is stored inside the index, stored vectors and queries are always transformed consistently.

Limitation or tradeoff

Nearest neighbours in the reduced space can differ from those in the full space. The Faiss pages cited here do not quantify that loss, so recall must be measured against an exact search on held-out queries. The matrix is learned from a training sample, so if later vectors come from a different distribution, such as a new embedding model, the axes go stale and the index must be retrained.

What the sources document

  • The index factory page lists the PCA transforms PCA64, PCAR64, PCAW64 and PCAWR64, all backed by the PCAMatrix class, where the number is the output dimension, R follows the reduction with a random rotation and W with whitening. [18]
  • The factory string "PCA80,Flat" is given as an example that, for 128-dimensional vectors, reduces them to 80 dimensions by PCA and then performs exhaustive search. [18]
  • The page says that PCAR is especially useful as pre-processing for a Flat or scalar quantizer index. [18]
  • The pre- and post-processing page says that if input vectors are 2048-dimensional and have to be reduced to 16 bytes, it makes sense to reduce them with a PCA first, and shows faiss.PCAMatrix(2048, 256, 0, True) for a reduction from 2048 to 256 dimensions with a random rotation after it. [19]
  • An index can be wrapped in an IndexPreTransform so that the mapping is applied transparently and the transform's training is integrated with the index training. [19]

4Common mistakes

  1. Running PCA on columns that have different units or scales

    Why it causes problems
    The column with the largest numbers dominates the first component whatever it means. In scikit-learn's wine example the proline column dominates the raw first component, and a classifier after PCA scores 35.19% on the test set against 96.30% once the columns are standardised.
    How to recognise it
    One column has loadings close to 1 on PC1 and by far the largest variance, PC1 takes an implausibly large share of the variance, and that share changes sharply when a column is converted to other units.
    How to fix it
    Put StandardScaler in front of PCA inside the pipeline unless all columns share one unit and their differences in spread are meaningful, as with pixels. Compare the explained variance ratios with and without scaling.

  2. Fitting the scaler and PCA on all rows before the train/test split or cross-validation

    Why it causes problems
    The test rows influence the mean, the scaling and the axes, so validation scores are optimistic. The model is evaluated with a transform that could not have been learned from the training data alone.
    How to recognise it
    The code calls fit or fit_transform on the full X before train_test_split, or PCA appears outside the Pipeline that is passed to cross_val_score or GridSearchCV.
    How to fix it
    Put the scaler, PCA and model in one Pipeline and fit it on training data only. Use its transform for validation, test and production rows, and persist the fitted object together with the model.

  3. Choosing the number of components from a fixed variance share and never checking the task

    Why it causes problems
    A cut-off such as 95% keeps components by variance, not by usefulness. In scikit-learn's PCR example the target follows a low-variance direction and a one-component regression scores R-squared -0.026 against 0.658 for a method that uses the target. The New York Fed paper in the examples finds a component with negligible effect on yields that still predicts returns.
    How to recognise it
    The model on the reduced scores does clearly worse than the same model on the original features, or its cross-validated score keeps improving as components are added well beyond the variance target.
    How to fix it
    Treat n_components as a hyperparameter and cross-validate the downstream metric over a grid of values. When the target matters more than the spread of the inputs, compare with a supervised method such as partial least squares.

  4. Reading a t-SNE or UMAP picture as a measurement

    Why it causes problems
    Cluster sizes, gaps and even the number of clusters change with perplexity, n_neighbors and the random seed. The Distill article shows that cluster sizes carry no meaning and that low perplexity can create clumps in random noise, so a conclusion drawn from one plot may not exist in the data.
    How to recognise it
    A report says which cluster is largest or which two are most similar from the plot alone, only one setting or seed was tried, or the two map coordinates were fed to k-means or to a model.
    How to fix it
    Try several perplexity or n_neighbors values and seeds, colour the map with labels produced in the original or PCA space, check any pattern in that space, and keep the map as a picture for communication, not as model input.

  5. Treating a principal component as one real quantity, or assuming the axes survive a refit

    Why it causes problems
    A component is a weighted blend of every feature, and its sign is arbitrary. A label such as customer value is an interpretation, and refitting on new data can rotate or flip the axes, so anything built on the old scores breaks. NCAR notes that PC values will likely change whenever more data are used to compute the EOF.
    How to recognise it
    Components are named in a report without any check against an outside variable, loadings change sign between runs, or a model's accuracy drops after the PCA step was refitted on a newer extract.
    How to fix it
    Test an interpretation by correlating the scores with a known variable and by bootstrapping to see whether the loadings are stable. Version the fitted PCA with its model, and never read meaning into a sign.

def fmt(values, digits=4):
    return " ".join(f"{v:.{digits}f}" for v in values)


def svd_report(name, M):
    Mc = M - M.mean(axis=0)
    s = np.linalg.svd(Mc, compute_uv=False)
    ratio = s**2 / np.sum(s**2)
    cumulative = np.cumsum(ratio)
    k = int(np.argmax(cumulative >= 0.95)) + 1
    print(f"{name:12s} singular values {fmt(s, 3)}")
    print(f"{'':12s} ratio {fmt(ratio)}  cumulative {fmt(cumulative)}  k for 95%: {k}")
    return Mc


svd_report("raw", X)
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
Zc = svd_report("standardised", Z)

# Keep one component of the standardised data and rebuild
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Zc, full_matrices=False)
Z1 = (Zc @ Vt[:1].T) @ Vt[:1]
print("k=1 squared error", round(float(np.sum((Zc - Z1) ** 2)), 3),
      "| dropped s^2", round(float(np.sum(s[1:] ** 2)), 3))

print("scikit-learn ratio", fmt(PCA().fit(Z).explained_variance_ratio_))

  • 2.5. Decomposing signals in components (matrix factorization problems) (opens in a new tab)

    scikit-learn

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 45 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    The PCA variants in scikit-learn (exact, randomized, incremental, kernel, sparse and truncated SVD), their costs and when each applies, including the centring and sparse-data details this lesson only touches.
    When to use it
    After this lesson, when your data are large, sparse or clearly non-linear and you need to pick a variant.

  • Principal component analysis: a review and recent developments (opens in a new tab)

    Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A (via PubMed Central)

    PaperIntermediateFreeAbout 1 hour (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A compact review of PCA by authors who wrote the standard textbook: the eigenvector and SVD formulations, covariance versus correlation matrices, choosing the number of components, sensitivity to outliers, and the main variants.
    When to use it
    When you want the mathematics and the caveats stated carefully, for example before defending a choice of components in a report.

  • How to Use t-SNE Effectively (opens in a new tab)

    Distill

    ArticleBeginnerFreeAbout 30 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    Interactive demonstrations of how perplexity changes a t-SNE picture, why cluster sizes and distances mean little, and how random noise can look like structure.
    When to use it
    Before you publish, present or act on any t-SNE or UMAP plot.

  • Frequently Asked Questions (opens in a new tab)

    UMAP (umap-learn documentation)

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 20 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    How the UMAP authors compare it with PCA and t-SNE, whether and how to cluster its output, and how it can go wrong with outliers or uneven density.
    When to use it
    When you plan to use UMAP for more than a quick picture, or to decide between UMAP and PCA for a pipeline.

  • Seurat - Guided Clustering Tutorial (opens in a new tab)

    Satija Lab (Seurat)

    DocumentationIntermediateFreeAbout 60 minutes (estimate)

    What it teaches
    A complete pipeline on real data, from filtering and scaling through PCA, an elbow-plot choice of components and clustering on the PCs to a UMAP picture, with the commands and outputs shown.
    When to use it
    After the exercises, to see the preparation, reduction and visualisation steps joined end to end. It uses R, but the workflow transfers to Python.
