Voice and Speech AI (Speech-to-Text, Text-to-Speech, Realtime)

Voice and speech AI covers the models that turn spoken audio into text (speech-to-text), label who spoke when (diarization), turn text into spoken audio (text-to-speech) and hold a spoken conversation in real time. A voice agent combines them, either as a chain of separate models or as one speech-to-speech model.

1Overview

Why it matters

Voice is the natural interface on a phone line, in a car, or when someone's hands and eyes are busy, and a recording of a call or meeting is useless to software until it becomes text. Each of those uses fails in its own way. A transcript with a wrong account number or the wrong speaker label corrupts every system that reads it. A voice agent that answers two seconds late, or keeps talking after the caller has started to speak, gets hung up on however good its answers are.

The engineering also differs from text chat. Latency is a budget of about a second, spent across several services that each take a few hundred milliseconds. The user can interrupt, so the conversation history has to record what was heard, which may be less than what was generated. Cost is counted in minutes of audio or in audio tokens. Accuracy changes with accent, noise and vocabulary in ways a single benchmark score hides. Anyone who builds, buys or evaluates a transcription pipeline or a voice agent needs these ideas to choose an architecture and to know what to measure.

Key terms

Speech-to-text (STT)
Turning recorded or live speech into written text. Also called automatic speech recognition (ASR) or transcription.
Speaker diarization
Splitting a recording into segments and labelling each with an anonymous speaker id, so the transcript shows who spoke when.
Text-to-speech (TTS)
Generating spoken audio from text. Also called speech synthesis.
Word error rate (WER)
The number of substituted, deleted and inserted words in a transcript divided by the number of words in a human reference transcript. Lower is better.
Voice activity detection (VAD)
A model or rule that marks which parts of an audio stream contain speech and which do not.
End-of-turn detection
Deciding that a speaker has finished and it is the other party's turn. Also called endpointing or turn detection.
Barge-in
The user starting to speak while the agent is still talking. Handling it means stopping the agent and listening.
Cascaded pipeline
A voice agent built as a chain of separate models, with speech-to-text feeding a text LLM that feeds text-to-speech. Also called a chained pipeline.
Speech-to-speech model
One model that takes audio in and produces audio out, with no text transcript as a required step in between.
Mouth-to-ear turn gap
The time from the moment the user stops speaking until the first sound of the reply reaches their ear. It is the latency a user experiences.

2Explanation

Introduction

In Multimodal AI you sent images to a model alongside text. Audio is the next modality, and it adds something an image does not have: time. A picture is uploaded whole. Speech arrives as a stream, a reply is expected within about a second of the speaker finishing (the benchmarks below start at 1.1 seconds), and the speaker may start talking again while the reply is still playing.

Voice AI is built from three jobs:

  • Speech-to-text (STT) listens and writes down the words. A second task, diarization, works out which speaker said each part.
  • Text-to-speech (TTS) reads text aloud in a synthetic voice.
  • Real-time conversation does both fast enough, and with good enough turn-taking, to feel like a call.

Think of talking to an expert through an interpreter. In one arrangement a note-taker writes down what you say, hands the note to the expert, the expert writes a reply, and a reader speaks it to you. Every note can be filed and checked, and any of the three people can be replaced. But each hand-off takes time, and the note does not record that you sounded upset. In the other arrangement the expert listens to you and answers directly. That is faster and nothing is lost in the writing, but there is no note to check before the answer is spoken.

Those are the two basic architectures for a voice agent. The first is a cascaded pipeline (speech-to-text, then a text LLM, then text-to-speech). The second is a speech-to-speech model. A third design, full duplex, appears later in the lesson.

This lesson covers each building block, how transcripts are scored, how the architectures compare, where the milliseconds go in one conversational turn, what to do when the user interrupts, and what limits cost and accuracy. It assumes you know how streaming responses work from LLM APIs and Streaming. Product names and prices are those documented on 10 October 2026 and will change; the mechanisms will not.

How it works

Speech-to-text: audio in, words out

Speech-to-text is done by neural sequence models. Whisper (opens in a new tab), the open-source model many tools build on, is a Transformer encoder-decoder. Audio is processed in 30-second windows, each window becomes a log-Mel spectrogram (a picture of which frequencies are present over time), and the decoder writes text tokens one at a time, as a language model does. That makes transcripts fluent. It is also why a transcript can contain fluent text that nobody said.

You will meet two modes:

  • File transcription. Upload a finished recording and get text back. OpenAI's file transcription guide (opens in a new tab) recommends gpt-transcribe, accepts files up to 25 MB, and takes prompt, keywords and languages hints so that product names and codes are recognised.
  • Streaming transcription. Audio is sent while the person is still talking. The service returns partial text, then a final transcript once it decides the utterance is over. Voice agents depend on it.

Accuracy is reported as word error rate (WER). Align the machine transcript with a human reference, count substituted (SSS), deleted (DDD) and inserted (III) words, and divide by the number of reference words NNN:

WER=S+D+IN\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}WER=NS+D+I​

Lower is better, and the value can exceed 1. WER also depends on normalisation: whether case, punctuation and number formats are unified before counting. In the worked example one transcript scores 0.45, 0.30 or 0.15 depending on those rules, so only compare figures that were computed the same way.

Diarization: who spoke when

Speaker diarization splits the timeline into segments and labels each one SPEAKER_00, SPEAKER_01 and so on. It recognises voices, not names. Two designs are common:

  • Separate models joined by time. A diarizer produces speaker turns, a speech-to-text model produces words with timestamps, and each word takes the speaker whose turn overlaps it most.
  • One model for both. OpenAI's gpt-4o-transcribe-diarize returns segments with speaker, start, end and text, and can attach names if you supply up to four reference clips of 2 to 10 seconds.

Errors tend to cluster at turn boundaries and in overlapping speech, which the WhisperX README lists as a weak spot, and a short interjection such as "yes" is easy to attach to the wrong speaker. If each party is recorded on its own channel, as many call recorders can do, transcribe the channels separately and you do not need diarization at all.

Quality is reported as diarization error rate (DER): the time given to the wrong speaker, plus missed speech, plus non-speech marked as speech, divided by the total time that people were actually speaking. The open-source pyannote (opens in a new tab) community-1 pipeline reports DER from 8.9% to 46.8% across twelve benchmarks, so test on recordings like yours.

Text-to-speech

Text-to-speech runs the other way, and three things decide whether it works in a product:

  • Streaming. Audio is generated in chunks, so playback can begin before synthesis ends. The number to watch is time to first audio. OpenAI's text-to-speech guide (opens in a new tab) recommends wav or pcm output for the fastest response.
  • Context. A TTS model chooses intonation from the words around the one it is saying, so services buffer some incoming text before they speak. More buffering sounds better and starts later.
  • Speakable text. Some synthesisers read "$20.50", "03/28/2025" and "Dr." badly, and Twilio's ConversationRelay guidance (opens in a new tab) singles out ElevenLabs voices. Have the LLM write numbers, dates and abbreviations as words.

OpenAI's usage policies also require you to tell users that a TTS voice is AI-generated.

Cascaded, speech-to-speech and full duplex

OpenAI's voice agents guide (opens in a new tab) describes three ways to connect speech to reasoning. A chained (cascaded) pipeline runs speech-to-text, then your text agent, then text-to-speech. A speech-to-speech model, reached through the Realtime API, takes audio in and produces audio out within one session. A full-duplex model (GPT-Live (opens in a new tab)) can listen while it speaks, and it delegates reasoning and tool calls to a separate backend agent.

The trade is control against naturalness. Text between stages can be logged, checked and swapped, but it carries no tone of voice and every hand-off costs time. A speech model hears hesitation and emphasis and can answer sooner, but you see what it understood only through events and a transcript made on the side.

Transport is part of the design. With the Realtime API, browsers connect over WebRTC and phone calls over SIP, and in both cases OpenAI's server manages the output audio buffer. A server-side WebSocket connection hands your code the audio chunks, so your code plays them and must track how much the user has heard.

The latency budget

Twilio's guide to core latency (opens in a new tab) calls the number that matters the mouth-to-ear turn gap: the time from the moment the user stops speaking until the reply reaches their ear. Its starting targets for a plain cascaded agent, as of November 2025, are:

Three points from that guide explain the shape of the budget:

  • Inside the speech-to-text stage, end-of-turn detection dominates. The system has to wait to be sure the speaker has finished, and fixed silence thresholds default to about 500 ms.
  • For the LLM, only time to first token counts, because synthesis can start on the first tokens. Streaming is mandatory.
  • Network and media handling add the rest. One cascaded reply needs at least ten network traversals, so services hosted far apart cost real time.

As a sum for one cascaded turn:

tgap=tin+tSTT+tLLM+tTTS+toutt_{\text{gap}} = t_{\text{in}} + t_{\text{STT}} + t_{\text{LLM}} + t_{\text{TTS}} + t_{\text{out}}tgap​=tin​+tSTT​+tLLM​+tTTS​+tout​

Here tSTTt_{\text{STT}}tSTT​ includes the end-of-turn wait, tLLMt_{\text{LLM}}tLLM​ is time to first token, tTTSt_{\text{TTS}}tTTS​ is time to first audio, and the outer terms are the voice network in each direction. A speech-to-speech model merges the three middle terms into one model's time to first audio. It still has to decide that the turn has ended. The sum leaves out the hand-offs between services (Twilio's worked example assumes about 10 ms each) and any waiting your own orchestration adds, such as the first-sentence wait in the first exercise.

Track the median and the 95th percentile separately. Tool calls and slow turn decisions land in the tail.

Turn detection and interruption handling

Voice activity detection (VAD) marks where speech starts and stops. The OpenAI Realtime API documents two turn detection modes (opens in a new tab): server_vad ends a turn after a set length of silence, and semantic_vad uses a classifier that scores whether the words sound finished, so it waits longer after "my order number is" than after a complete sentence. LiveKit Agents (opens in a new tab) adds a turn detector model on top of VAD for the same reason.

When the user speaks during the reply (barge-in), four things must happen quickly:

  1. Stop playback and clear any buffered audio.
  2. Cancel the LLM and TTS generation still in flight.
  3. Decide what happens to a tool call that is already running.
  4. Truncate the conversation history to what was actually heard.

Step 4 is the one people forget. If the history keeps the whole generated reply, the model will later refer to things the user never heard.

Not every sound is an interruption. A cough or an "uh-huh" should not stop the agent, so frameworks let you require a minimum duration or number of words, and resume speech after a false alarm.

Cost and accuracy limits

Billing units differ by architecture. On OpenAI's pricing page (opens in a new tab) on 10 October 2026, file transcription with gpt-transcribe costs $0.0045 per audio minute, live transcription with gpt-live-transcribe or gpt-realtime-whisper $0.017 per minute, and a GPT-Live voice session (gpt-live-1) $0.05 per minute, billed per second, with backend model and tool usage charged separately. The Realtime API bills tokens: user audio is 1 token per 100 ms and assistant audio 1 token per 50 ms, at $32 and $64 per million for gpt-realtime-2.1. A minute of assistant speech is 1,200 tokens, about $0.077. The whole conversation is re-sent on every response, so later turns cost more unless prompt caching applies.

Accuracy has limits that no setting removes:

  • It is uneven across speakers. A 2020 study (opens in a new tab) of five commercial recognisers measured an average WER of 0.35 for Black speakers against 0.19 for white speakers.
  • Models can invent text. A 2024 study (opens in a new tab) found that about 1.4% of 13,140 interview segments sent to the Whisper API in 2023 came back with phrases that were not in the audio, though the authors saw many of those cases resolved after updates in late 2023.
  • Names, numbers and codes fail first. Confirm them by reading them back to the user.

Measure on your own audio, split by speaker group, before you trust a published figure.

Worked example: Scoring a diarized support-call transcript by hand and in plain Python

A short support call was recorded on one channel. You have a human reference transcript, the words a speech-to-text model returned with timestamps, and the speaker turns a diarizer returned. The script scores the transcript three ways and then gives every word a speaker. Save it as score_transcript.py. It needs only the Python standard library.

python
import re

REFERENCE = [  # human transcript: (speaker, text)
    ("agent", "Thanks for calling Northwind, how can I help?"),
    ("caller", "Hi. My order number is four two seven, and it arrived damaged."),
]
ASR_WORDS = [  # speech-to-text output: (word, start_seconds, end_seconds)
    ("Thanks", 0.20, 0.52), ("for", 0.52, 0.66), ("calling", 0.66, 1.05),
    ("North", 1.05, 1.36), ("Wind.", 1.36, 1.70), ("How", 1.95, 2.10),
    ("can", 2.10, 2.25), ("I", 2.25, 2.32), ("help?", 2.32, 2.70),
    ("Hi,", 3.05, 3.30), ("my", 3.30, 3.44), ("order", 3.44, 3.80),
    ("number", 3.80, 4.15), ("is", 4.15, 4.28), ("427", 4.28, 5.30),
    ("and", 5.45, 5.60), ("it", 5.60, 5.70), ("arrived", 5.70, 6.15),
    ("damage.", 6.15, 6.70),
]
TURNS = [("SPEAKER_00", 0.10, 3.20), ("SPEAKER_01", 3.20, 6.80)]  # diarizer output
TRUE_SPEAKERS = ["SPEAKER_00"] * 9 + ["SPEAKER_01"] * 10          # who really said each ASR word
SPOKEN_NUMBERS = {"427": "four two seven"}


def normalise(text, numbers=None):
    words = re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9' ]", " ", text.lower()).split()
    return " ".join((numbers or {}).get(word, word) for word in words).split()


def edit_counts(ref, hyp):
    """Substitutions, deletions and insertions in the cheapest word alignment."""
    row = [(j, 0, 0, j) for j in range(len(hyp) + 1)]  # (errors, S, D, I)
    for i, ref_word in enumerate(ref, start=1):
        new_row = [(i, 0, i, 0)]
        for j, hyp_word in enumerate(hyp, start=1):
            errors, s, d, ins = row[j - 1]
            step = 0 if ref_word == hyp_word else 1
            keep_or_swap = (errors + step, s + step, d, ins)
            errors, s, d, ins = row[j]
            delete = (errors + 1, s, d + 1, ins)
            errors, s, d, ins = new_row[j - 1]
            insert = (errors + 1, s, d, ins + 1)
            new_row = [*new_row, min(keep_or_swap, delete, insert)]
        row = new_row
    return row[-1][1:]


def report(label, ref, hyp):
    s, d, ins = edit_counts(ref, hyp)
    print(f"{label:<22} N={len(ref)} S={s} D={d} I={ins} WER={(s + d + ins) / len(ref):.2f}")


def overlap(word, turn):
    return max(0.0, min(word[2], turn[2]) - max(word[1], turn[1]))


def speaker_of(word, turns):
    return max(turns, key=lambda turn: overlap(word, turn))[0]


reference_text = " ".join(text for _, text in REFERENCE)
asr_text = " ".join(word for word, _, _ in ASR_WORDS)
report("raw text", reference_text.split(), asr_text.split())
report("normalised", normalise(reference_text), normalise(asr_text))
report("numbers spelled out", normalise(reference_text), normalise(asr_text, SPOKEN_NUMBERS))

labels = [speaker_of(word, TURNS) for word in ASR_WORDS]
for speaker in dict.fromkeys(labels):
    said = " ".join(word[0] for word, label in zip(ASR_WORDS, labels) if label == speaker)
    print(f"{speaker}: {said}")
wrong = sum(label != truth for label, truth in zip(labels, TRUE_SPEAKERS))
print(f"words given to the wrong speaker: {wrong} of {len(labels)}")

Running python3 score_transcript.py prints:

numbers spelled out    N=20 S=2 D=0 I=1 WER=0.15
SPEAKER_00: Thanks for calling North Wind. How can I help? Hi,
SPEAKER_01: my order number is 427 and it arrived damage.

Check each line against the 20-word reference:

Scoring ruleErrors countedWER
Raw textThe six below, plus how/How, Hi./Hi, and My/my9 / 20 = 0.45
Lower-cased, punctuation removednorthwind to north wind (1 S, 1 I), four two seven to 427 (1 S, 2 D), damaged to damage (1 S)6 / 20 = 0.30
Digits also spelled outnorth wind (1 S, 1 I) and damage (1 S)3 / 20 = 0.15

The model heard the order number correctly and wrote it as digits. Half of the "errors" in the middle row are a formatting difference, which is why a WER figure means little until you know the normalisation behind it. WER also treats every word alike: damage for damaged is harmless, but a wrong digit in 427 would count as just one substitution too, so the score cannot tell a harmless error from a costly one.

The speaker labels show the usual diarization failure. "Hi," runs from 3.05 s to 3.30 s and the diarizer put the turn boundary at 3.20 s, so 0.15 s of the word lies in SPEAKER_00's turn and 0.10 s in SPEAKER_01's. The largest overlap wins and the caller's greeting is attached to the agent. One word in 19 looks small, but the errors gather at turn changes, where short replies such as "yes" and "no" live.

Edge cases and limitations

The transcript contains sentences nobody said
Encoder-decoder speech models generate text, so on silence, noise or long pauses they can produce fluent invented phrases. The Careless Whisper study found this in about 1.4% of 13,140 interview segments sent to the Whisper API in 2023, at 1.7% for speakers with aphasia against 1.2% for a control group, and found more non-vocal audio in segments that produced it (5.3 against 3.5 seconds on average). The authors saw many of those cases disappear after late-2023 updates, so the rate is a warning about the mechanism, not a current benchmark. Remove silence with voice activity detection before transcribing, as WhisperX does, and have a person review transcripts that feed medical, legal or hiring decisions.
A thinking pause is taken for the end of the turn
A caller who stops for a second to find an order number passes a fixed silence threshold, so the agent starts answering half a question. Twilio's guide notes that fixed thresholds default to about 500 ms and that shortening them is risky. Semantic or model-based turn detection waits longer after an unfinished phrase, but when it wrongly decides the caller is still talking, the turn closes only on the fallback silence timeout, which shows up as a jump in tail latency.
A cough or an "uh-huh" stops the agent mid-sentence
Voice activity detection reacts to any speech-like sound, including backchannel words, background voices and the agent's own audio echoing into the microphone. Frameworks expose controls for this: Twilio ConversationRelay has interruptSensitivity and ignoreBackchannel, and LiveKit Agents can require a minimum duration or word count and resume speech after a false interruption. Test on speakerphone and in a noisy room, not only with a headset.
The truncated audio and its transcript do not line up
With a speech-to-speech model the audio is the record and the text transcript is produced alongside it. OpenAI's Realtime documentation says the model cannot align transcript and audio precisely, so conversation.item.truncate cuts the audio at the given point and removes the transcript of the unplayed part without giving you a transcript cut at the same word. Do not treat a logged assistant transcript as proof of what the caller heard.
Numbers and codes change form in both directions
A recogniser may write a spoken "four two seven" as 427, and a synthesiser may read 03/28/2025 or a currency amount in an unexpected way. Twilio recommends writing numbers, dates and abbreviations as words before synthesis. WhisperX notes that tokens such as "2014." or a price cannot be aligned and get no timestamp. Normalise deliberately on both sides, and read critical values back to the user for confirmation.
Live transcripts usually arrive without speaker labels
Diarization needs enough audio from each voice to tell speakers apart, so it is mostly a batch feature. OpenAI documents that speaker labelling is not supported in Realtime transcription sessions, and that in streamed file transcription a speaker is assigned only when a segment is final. For live captions with names, capture each participant on a separate channel or track and label by channel.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The channel is a phone line or the user's hands and eyes are busy, so typing and reading are not available.
  • You hold recordings of calls, meetings or interviews that need to be searched, summarised or analysed, which needs transcription and usually speaker labels.
  • The exchange is short and conversational, such as booking, status checks and simple troubleshooting, where a reply within about a second matters more than a long, detailed answer.
  • You can test with recordings of your real users, including their accents, their vocabulary and the noise of the places they call from.

Think twice when

  • The answer is a table, a long identifier, code or anything the user must copy exactly. Speech is slow and error-prone for that content, so send text.
  • A transcript would be used as a clinical, legal or hiring record without human review. Recognition errors and invented text make unreviewed transcripts unsafe for that.
  • You cannot keep the mouth-to-ear turn gap near one second, for example because every turn needs a slow tool call and you have no way to fill the wait.
  • Callers have not been told they are speaking to an AI voice, or recording and transcription consent has not been obtained where it is required.

A simpler alternative. Text chat, or push-to-talk voice input: the user holds a button, you transcribe the finished clip with a file transcription call, and the answer comes back as text or as one synthesised clip. That removes turn detection and interruption handling entirely. It stops being enough when the user cannot press a button or look at a screen, or when the conversation has to feel like a call.

Key takeaways

  • Speech-to-text, diarization and text-to-speech are separate tasks with separate error measures, and a voice agent is those tasks plus turn-taking under a latency budget.
  • Word error rate is substitutions plus deletions plus insertions divided by reference words, and a figure is only comparable when the normalisation rules and the test audio are the same.
  • A cascaded pipeline gives you text to log, check and swap at every stage, while a speech-to-speech model hears tone and can answer sooner but exposes less to inspect.
  • The latency that matters is the mouth-to-ear turn gap, and its two largest terms are usually the wait to be sure the user has finished and the LLM's time to first token.
  • When the user interrupts, stop playback, cancel generation and truncate the conversation history to what was actually heard.
  • Cost is billed per audio minute or per audio token and grows with conversation length, and accuracy varies by speaker, noise and vocabulary, so measure both on your own calls.

Example 1 of 5

WhisperX: long recordings turned into transcripts with word timings and speaker labels

WhisperX open-source projectDocumented in an open-source project

WhisperX is an open-source Python tool for transcribing recordings. Its README describes a pipeline that combines the Whisper speech-to-text model with a phoneme alignment model and the pyannote speaker diarization toolkit, and the pyannote README documents the diarization pipeline it calls. [19][20]

The problem

Whisper transcribes accurately, but the WhisperX README points out two gaps that matter for long recordings such as interviews and meetings: its timestamps are at the level of an utterance and can be wrong by several seconds, and it does not natively support batching. It also has no notion of speakers. Subtitles need word timing, and a meeting record needs to show who said what.

Where this topic fits

This is batch transcription with diarization, the first two building blocks of this lesson. It runs after a recording is complete and before anything that consumes the transcript, such as subtitle files, search or summarisation. There is no latency budget, so several models can each make a full pass over the audio.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Voice activity detection finds the stretches of speech and cuts the recording into segments that can be transcribed in batches.
  2. Whisper, running on the faster-whisper backend, transcribes the batched segments into text.
  3. A wav2vec2 phoneme model performs forced alignment, which matches the known text to the audio and yields a start and end time for each word.
  4. The pyannote diarization pipeline processes the same audio and returns speaker turns with anonymous ids.
  5. The assign_word_speakers function gives each word a speaker id from the diarization turns. A typical implementation picks the turn that overlaps the word most, the same join by time as in this lesson's worked example.
  6. The result is a list of segments carrying text, word timings and speaker ids, which can be written out as subtitle files with the current word highlighted.

Why this approach fits

Each model does the one job it is good at, and time is the shared key that joins their outputs. Because the work is offline, accuracy can be bought with extra passes: one for text, one for alignment, one for speakers. Running locally also keeps recordings on your own machine, which matters for interviews and internal meetings.

Limitation or tradeoff

The README lists the limits plainly. Words containing characters the alignment model does not know, such as "2014." or "£13.60", get no timing. Overlapping speech is handled poorly, diarization is described as far from perfect, and each language needs its own alignment model. pyannote's benchmark table shows how much diarization quality depends on the recording: error rates for its community-1 pipeline range from 8.9% to 46.8% across datasets. The join by time also inherits both models' timing errors, as the misattributed "Hi," in the worked example shows.

What the sources document

  • The WhisperX README describes the project as fast automatic speech recognition, 70x realtime with the large-v2 model, with word-level timestamps and speaker diarization. [19]
  • The README states that Whisper's own timestamps are at the utterance level, not per word, can be inaccurate by several seconds, and that OpenAI's Whisper does not natively support batching. [19]
  • It lists word-level timestamps from wav2vec2 alignment, multispeaker transcription using speaker diarization from pyannote-audio, and VAD preprocessing that reduces hallucination and allows batching with no WER degradation. [19]
  • Its limitations section says that words with characters outside the alignment model's dictionary, such as "2014." or "£13.60", cannot be aligned and are given no timing, that overlapping speech is not handled particularly well, and that diarization is far from perfect. [19]
  • The pyannote.audio README shows the community-1 pipeline returning turns with a start time, a stop time and a speaker label, and its benchmark table reports diarization error rates for that pipeline between 8.9% and 46.8% depending on the dataset. [20]

Example 2 of 5

Twilio ConversationRelay: a cascaded phone agent behind a text-only WebSocket

TwilioDocumented in official docs

ConversationRelay is Twilio's managed service for putting an AI agent on a phone call. Twilio's product documentation, its WebSocket message reference and its TwiML reference describe how the service runs speech-to-text and text-to-speech and exchanges text with the developer's application. [10][11][12][13][14]

The problem

A phone agent has to handle telephony audio, streaming recognition, synthesis, turn boundaries and callers who talk over the reply. Twilio's documentation presents the service as taking over speech recognition and speech synthesis, so that the developer's application only processes the conversation logic and sends text back.

Where this topic fits

It is the cascaded architecture with both audio stages hosted by the platform. Twilio runs speech-to-text, end-of-turn detection, text-to-speech and playback. Your server receives the caller's words as text over a WebSocket and returns the LLM's reply as text tokens. Interruption handling is split: Twilio stops the audio, and your application repairs the conversation history.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. TwiML containing a ConversationRelay noun inside Connect routes the call to the service, which opens a WebSocket to your server and sends a setup message describing the call.
  2. The caller speaks. The configured speech-to-text provider transcribes the audio, and the service sends a prompt message whose voicePrompt field holds the text. Its last field says whether the caller's speech has completed.
  3. Your application sends the text to an LLM with streaming switched on and forwards each token in a text message as soon as it arrives, marking the final one with last set to true.
  4. The service synthesises the tokens with the configured text-to-speech provider and plays the audio to the caller.
  5. If the caller starts talking during playback and the session allows interruption, the service stops the speech and sends an interrupt message with the text spoken so far and the playback time in milliseconds.
  6. A typical application then replaces the assistant message in its history with the utteranceUntilInterrupt text, cancels the LLM stream that is still running, and waits for the next prompt.

Why this approach fits

A text interface lets the application use any LLM or agent framework and keep a complete, checkable record of what was said. The audio stages sit with the provider that already terminates the phone call, and Twilio's latency guide argues that audio services are the ones that most need to be close to the media edge. The interrupt message carries exactly what the application needs to truncate its history.

Limitation or tradeoff

The LLM sees text only, so a caller's tone never reaches it. Provider choice is limited to those Twilio lists (Google and Deepgram for speech-to-text; Google, Amazon and ElevenLabs for text-to-speech). The WebSocket is a single point of failure: the reference says that if it disconnects unexpectedly Twilio does not reconnect and the call ends with a failed status, unless your action callback returns new TwiML. Twilio's sub-half-second median for the service comes from its own internal benchmarks with the note that results may vary. The guide does not say which stages that figure spans, so do not set it against the 1,115 ms mouth-to-ear target; measure your own turn gap with your own LLM and server location. The default interruptSensitivity is high, the easiest setting to trigger, which needs tuning on noisy lines.

What the sources document

  • The product page describes three steps: Conversation Relay converts the caller's speech into text and sends it to your application, your application uses AI to analyse the text and generate a response, and the caller hears your chosen voice reading that response. [10]
  • The WebSocket reference defines a prompt message with voicePrompt, lang and last fields, sent when the caller says something, and an interrupt message with utteranceUntilInterrupt and durationUntilInterruptMs fields, sent when the caller interrupts text-to-speech playback by speaking. [11]
  • Twilio recommends streaming text tokens to the service as soon as they are available instead of waiting for the entire message, because this reduces latency, and using the last attribute to mark the final token of a talk cycle. [11][13]
  • The TwiML reference lists an interruptible attribute (none, dtmf, speech or any, default any), an interruptSensitivity attribute (high, medium or low, default high), an ignoreBackchannel attribute that filters phrases such as "yeah" and "uh-huh", and a speechTimeout between 600 and 5000 milliseconds. [12]
  • Twilio's latency guide calls ConversationRelay a managed voice orchestration service and reports, from internal benchmarks, a median latency under 0.5 seconds and under 0.725 seconds at the 95th percentile (p50 491 ms, p95 713 ms), noting that results may vary. [14]

Example 3 of 5

OpenAI Realtime API: speech-to-speech sessions that track what the user actually heard

OpenAIDocumented in official docs

The OpenAI Realtime API runs speech-to-speech voice agents. Its getting-started guide, its guide to realtime conversations and its voice activity detection guide document how a session takes audio in, produces audio out and handles turns and interruptions. [5][6][7][8]

The problem

A model that speaks directly has no transcript hand-off at which an application can decide when to answer or record what was said. The session itself has to decide when the user's turn has ended, stop when the user talks over the reply, and keep its record of the conversation consistent with what was played. The documentation gives the reason: the model should know where it was interrupted so that it can continue naturally, for example when the user asks what the last thing was.

Where this topic fits

This is the speech-to-speech architecture. Turn detection and interruption handling, which a cascaded pipeline implements in an orchestration layer, are features of the session here. The developer configures them with session events and, on a WebSocket connection, supplies the one fact the server cannot know: how much audio was played.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The client opens a session, over WebRTC from a browser or over a WebSocket from a server, and streams microphone audio into the session's input audio buffer.
  2. Turn detection runs on the server. With server_vad a turn ends after a configured length of silence. With semantic_vad a classifier estimates from the words whether the user has finished.
  3. When a turn ends the server creates a response, and the model's audio is streamed back to the client.
  4. If the user speaks during the response, the server emits input_audio_buffer.speech_started and cancels the response in progress.
  5. On WebRTC and SIP connections the server manages the output audio buffer and removes the unplayed audio itself. On a WebSocket the client stops playback and sends conversation.item.truncate with the number of milliseconds that were played.
  6. The conversation now holds only the heard part of the reply, and the next response is generated from that state.

Why this approach fits

The model that produces the speech also holds the conversation state, so cancelling and truncating are one event each. A cascaded pipeline has to coordinate the same abort across a recogniser, an LLM stream and a synthesiser. The model also receives the audio itself, which the documentation says gives it more to work with around tone and inflection than a transcript would.

Limitation or tradeoff

Less is visible between input and output. The text of the user's speech comes from a separate transcription model, so the transcript you log may differ from what the speech model understood, and truncation cannot return a transcript cut at the exact word. Checking the user's input before the model answers means switching off automatic responses and accepting extra latency. Billing is by token with the whole conversation sent for every response, so long sessions cost more per turn, and prompt caching is described as best-effort. A session lasts at most 60 minutes.

What the sources document

  • The conversations guide describes voice-to-voice interaction with the model without an intermediate text-to-speech or speech-to-text step, and says this enables lower latency and gives the model more data to work with around the tone and inflection of voice input. [6]
  • Voice activity detection is enabled by default in speech-to-speech sessions. The server_vad mode chunks audio on periods of silence and is tuned with threshold, prefix_padding_ms and silence_duration_ms. The semantic_vad mode uses a semantic classifier and an eagerness setting. [7]
  • With VAD enabled the API detects user speech, cancels the ongoing response and starts a new one. Truncating is defined as removing the unplayed portion of the model's last response from the conversation. [6]
  • On WebRTC and SIP connections the server manages a buffer of output audio and truncates unplayed audio automatically. On a WebSocket the client must stop playback and send conversation.item.truncate with audio_end_ms, and the guide notes that this does not provide a truncated transcript. [6]
  • The cost guide states that the entire conversation is sent to the model for each response, that audio in user messages is 1 token per 100 ms and in assistant messages 1 token per 50 ms, and that input transcription uses a different model from the speech-to-speech model. The conversations guide gives a maximum session duration of 60 minutes. [8][6]

Example 4 of 5

ElevenLabs text-to-speech WebSocket: speaking an LLM's reply while it is still being written

ElevenLabsDocumented in official docs

ElevenLabs offers a text-to-speech WebSocket endpoint that accepts text in pieces and returns audio in pieces. Its real-time audio guide, its latency concept page and its latency optimization guide describe how the endpoint buffers text and what determines the delay before the first audio. [16][17][18]

The problem

In a voice agent the text to be spoken does not exist yet when speech should start, because the LLM is still producing it token by token. Waiting for the full reply adds the whole generation time to the turn gap. Synthesising every token the moment it arrives is not the answer either: the guide explains that the model needs a certain amount of input text to deduce context and produce high-quality audio.

Where this topic fits

This is the text-to-speech stage of a cascaded pipeline, at the hand-off from the LLM. It applies the two ideas from this lesson's text-to-speech section, streaming the output and buffering the input for context, and exposes the trade between them as a setting.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The application opens a WebSocket to the stream-input endpoint for a chosen voice and names a low-latency model such as eleven_flash_v2_5.
  2. Its first message carries the voice settings and, optionally, a chunk_length_schedule inside generation_config.
  3. As LLM tokens arrive, the application sends them as text messages on the same connection.
  4. The service buffers the text until its length reaches the next threshold in the schedule, then generates audio and returns it as base64-encoded chunks, which the application decodes and plays.
  5. With the last text of the turn the application sends flush set to true, so the remaining buffered text is spoken without waiting for more input.
  6. Sending an empty string closes the connection.

Why this approach fits

A two-way socket matches the shape of the problem: text trickles in while audio trickles out. The schedule turns the latency and quality trade into numbers you can test, and the flush flag removes the stall at the end of a turn, when no more text is coming to fill the buffer.

Limitation or tradeoff

The guide warns that lowering the thresholds to cut latency may reduce quality. The headline figure of about 75 ms for Flash models is model inference only. The latency page lists what is added before a user hears anything: a network round trip, typically 20 to 200 ms over the public internet, and audio player buffering, where 500 ms is common. The WebSocket closes after 20 seconds without input, and this endpoint does not support the eleven_v3 or eleven_v4 models. Text that was sent but not yet played when a user interrupts still has to be removed from the conversation history by your own code.

What the sources document

  • The real-time guide says the model requires a certain threshold of input text to produce high-quality audio and deduce context, and that text below the threshold is added to a buffer until the buffer is full. [16]
  • chunk_length_schedule is an array of character counts. With the value [120, 160, 250, 290] audio is generated after 120, 160, 250 and 290 characters have been sent. Setting flush to true forces generation of any buffered text, which the guide advises at the end of a conversation turn. [16]
  • The latency page states that Flash models achieve about 75 ms of model inference for typical short inputs, excluding network round trips, and that time to first audio is almost always the number that matters for user experience and is always larger. It gives 20 to 200 ms as a typical network round trip and says a 500 ms audio player buffer is common. [17]
  • The latency optimization guide describes the WebSocket endpoint as suited to real-time text input such as LLM output, and reports time to first byte for Flash models over WebSockets of 100 to 150 ms in North America, Europe and South East Asia and 150 to 200 ms in South Asia and North East Asia. [18]
  • The real-time guide notes that the WebSocket connection closes automatically after 20 seconds of inactivity and that this endpoint does not support the eleven_v3 or eleven_v4 models. [16]

Example 5 of 5

An audit of five commercial speech recognisers that found error rates nearly twice as high for Black speakers

Documented in a paper

Koenecke and colleagues (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2020) tested the speech-to-text services of Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM and Microsoft on recorded interviews with Black and white speakers in five US locations. The paper describes the data, the scoring procedure and the results. [23]

The problem

A vendor reports one accuracy figure for a recogniser, measured on its own test sets. The researchers asked whether accuracy is the same for different groups of speakers. Speech-to-text sits inside products such as virtual assistants, closed captioning and medical dictation, so a higher error rate for one group means a worse product for that group.

Where this topic fits

This is evaluation of the speech-to-text stage with the word error rate from this lesson. It is the step that belongs before choosing a vendor or relying on transcripts: score the candidate systems on audio from the people who will use them, apply one normalisation procedure to every system, and compare the results by group.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The team took sociolinguistic interviews from two collections that already had human transcripts, the Corpus of Regional African American Language and Voices of California.
  2. They cut the interviewees' speech into snippets of 5 to 50 seconds and matched snippets from Black and white speakers on age, gender and duration, which left 2,141 snippets per group and 19.8 hours of audio.
  3. Every snippet was transcribed by each of the five commercial services.
  4. Human and machine transcripts were standardised with the same rules, including lower-casing, removing punctuation, unifying numerals and spellings, and removing filler words.
  5. WER was computed for each snippet and averaged for each service and each group.
  6. To look for the cause they compared 206 identical short phrases spoken by Black and white speakers, and checked how many of the spoken words were in each system's vocabulary.

Why this approach fits

WER against a human reference is cheap to compute and comparable across vendors as long as the normalisation is identical, which the team enforced. Matching snippets on age, gender and duration removes the obvious alternative explanations. Comparing identical phrases separates what was said from how it sounded, and that comparison pointed to the acoustic side of the systems.

Limitation or tradeoff

The paper was received for review in October 2019, so it describes the services as they were then, in US English, for 115 speakers in five places. The absolute numbers do not describe today's products. WER also treats every word alike: a wrong filler word and a wrong medication name each count once. An average hides the tail, which is why the authors also report the share of snippets above a WER of 0.5. What lasts is the method and the warning, not the figures.

What the sources document

  • The study examined five automated speech recognition systems, from Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM and Microsoft, on structured interviews with 42 white speakers and 73 Black speakers, using 19.8 hours of audio matched on the age and gender of the speaker. [23]
  • All five systems showed substantial disparities, with an average WER of 0.35 for Black speakers and 0.19 for white speakers. For Microsoft's system, the best overall, the figures were 0.27 and 0.15; for Apple's, the worst overall, 0.45 and 0.23. [23]
  • Assuming that a WER above 0.5 makes a transcript unusable, 23% of snippets from Black speakers were unusable compared with 1.6% of snippets from white speakers. [23]
  • On 206 matched utterances with identical ground-truth text, error rates were about twice as large when the phrases were spoken by Black speakers, while 98 to 99% of the words spoken by both groups were in the systems' reconstructed vocabularies. The authors attribute the gap primarily to the acoustic models. [23]
  • Before computing WER the authors standardised human and machine transcripts: single spacing, consistent handling of numerals, removal of punctuation and special characters, lower-casing, standardised spellings and removal of filler words. [23]

4Common mistakes

  1. Quoting one component's latency as the agent's response time.

    Why it causes problems
    A text-to-speech model's inference time or an LLM's benchmark score leaves out the end-of-turn wait, the network hops and the audio player's buffer. A plan built on those figures promises a response time the product cannot deliver, and callers read the extra silence as the agent not having heard them.
    How to recognise it
    The dashboard has a timing for each service but nothing measured from the end of the caller's speech to the first reply audio played on the caller's device.
    How to fix it
    Timestamp the end of user speech and the first played audio at the client, report the median and the 95th percentile of that gap for every release, and only then break it down by stage.

  2. Waiting for the complete LLM reply before starting speech synthesis.

    Why it causes problems
    The whole generation time is added to every turn, so the silence before the agent speaks grows with the length of the answer and long answers feel like dropped calls.
    How to recognise it
    The turn gap grows with the length of the reply, and time to first audio follows the LLM's total generation time instead of its time to first token.
    How to fix it
    Stream LLM tokens into a streaming text-to-speech connection, let it buffer to a sensible chunk, flush at the end of the turn, and listen to the result at each buffering setting before choosing one.

  3. Keeping the full generated reply in the conversation history after the user interrupted it.

    Why it causes problems
    The model believes it has already said things the caller never heard. It refers back to them, skips information the caller still needs, or contradicts what was actually spoken.
    How to recognise it
    After a barge-in, the stored assistant message is longer than the audio that was played, and later replies contain phrases such as "as I mentioned" about content that was cut off.
    How to fix it
    On every interruption cut the stored reply at the playback position, using the platform's signal for it, such as the utteranceUntilInterrupt field in ConversationRelay or a conversation.item.truncate event in the Realtime API, and cancel the generation that is still running.

  4. Comparing word error rates that were computed with different normalisation or on different audio.

    Why it causes problems
    A figure measured on clean read speech says little about phone calls from your customers, and formatting differences such as digits against spelled-out numbers are counted as recognition errors. The team picks a vendor on numbers that do not describe its own traffic.
    How to recognise it
    WER figures are quoted without the test set or the normalisation rules, or your own WER moves a lot when you lower-case the text or spell out digits.
    How to fix it
    Build a reference set from your own recordings that covers your speaker groups and noise conditions, run every candidate through one normaliser, and report WER by group and separately for names, numbers and codes.

  5. Shortening the silence threshold to cut latency without a way to abort a reply that started too early.

    Why it causes problems
    The agent starts talking whenever a caller pauses to think. Twilio's guide points out that being interrupted by the agent feels far worse to a user than a little extra latency.
    How to recognise it
    Recordings show the agent starting during a caller's mid-sentence pause, and many turns have a caller interruption within the first second of the reply.
    How to fix it
    Use semantic or model-based turn detection with a fallback silence timeout, and implement a graceful abort (clear the audio buffer, abort the LLM stream, cancel open tool requests, clean up the history) before tightening any threshold.

  6. Treating diarization labels as identities.

    Why it causes problems
    SPEAKER_00 only means the first voice the diarizer separated in this file. Mapping it to a role or a name by position puts statements in the wrong person's mouth, and words near turn changes are the ones most often misattributed.
    How to recognise it
    Code maps SPEAKER_00 to the agent in every file, or the two roles swap between transcripts of calls handled by the same agent.
    How to fix it
    Record each party on a separate channel when you can. Otherwise supply reference clips for known speakers where the service supports them, or assign names by review, and mark words at turn boundaries as uncertain.

  • The history keeps only the words whose audio had finished playing when playback stopped. A word that was cut off part-way is not kept.
  • When the reply is interrupted, return an event shaped like the Realtime API's conversation.item.truncate, with audio_end_ms set to the time playback stopped.
  • If the confirmation time is at or after the end of the reply, the reply was heard in full, so return no event and the complete text.
  • Do not modify the input lists. Return a new dictionary on every call.

Expected behaviour

For "cough then interruption" the 180 ms burst is ignored, playback stops at 1,950 ms, the event carries audio_end_ms 1950, the history is Your order ships on Tuesday and and the unplayed text is arrives by Friday.. For "cough only" nothing is interrupted and the history is the full sentence. For "speech after the reply" the confirmation at 3,000 ms falls after the reply ended at 2,900 ms, so there is no event and the history is the full sentence.

Hint 1

Work out one number first, the time at which playback stops, or None. Everything else follows from it.

Hint 2

A word was heard in full when its end time is less than or equal to the stop time.

Hint 3

The cases "no interruption" and "interruption confirmed after the reply ended" can share one early return.

Show solution

Add this below the data from the prompt and save the file as barge_in.py.

python
def interruption_time(speech_segments, min_ms=MIN_INTERRUPTION_MS):
    """Time at which the first long-enough burst of caller speech is confirmed, or None."""
    for start, stop in speech_segments:
        if stop - start >= min_ms:
            return start + min_ms
    return None


def handle_barge_in(reply_words, speech_segments, item_id=ITEM_ID):
    reply_end = reply_words[-1][2]
    ignored = [(start, stop) for start, stop in speech_segments
               if stop - start < MIN_INTERRUPTION_MS]
    cut = interruption_time(speech_segments)
    full_text = " ".join(word for word, _, _ in reply_words)
    if cut is None or cut >= reply_end:  # nothing was cut off
        return {"interrupted": False, "ignored": ignored, "event": None,
                "history": full_text, "unplayed": ""}
    heard = [word for word, _, end in reply_words if end <= cut]
    unplayed = [word for word, _, end in reply_words if end > cut]
    event = {"type": "conversation.item.truncate", "item_id": item_id,
             "content_index": 0, "audio_end_ms": cut}
    return {"interrupted": True, "ignored": ignored, "event": event,
            "history": " ".join(heard), "unplayed": " ".join(unplayed)}


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for name, segments in SCENARIOS.items():
        result = handle_barge_in(REPLY_WORDS, segments)
        print(f"--- {name}")
        for key in ("interrupted", "ignored", "event", "history", "unplayed"):
            print(f"{key}: {result[key]}")

Why it is built this way:

  • The stop time is 1,950 ms, not 1,650 ms. The handler cannot know that a burst is long enough until 300 ms of it has passed, so the caller hears 300 ms more of the reply. That is the price of ignoring coughs. Real systems soften it by lowering the volume or pausing as soon as speech starts and resuming if the burst turns out to be short.
  • A half-played word is dropped. "arrives" was playing from 1,700 ms and was cut at 1,950 ms. Leaving it out means the agent may repeat it, which is safer than assuming the caller heard it.
  • Speech after the reply is a new turn. In the third scenario there is nothing left to truncate, so sending a truncate event would be wrong. The caller's speech is simply the next input.
  • In a real client you would also stop the player, discard buffered audio and send the event to the server. On WebRTC and SIP connections to the Realtime API the server does the truncation for you.

  • Does speech that starts at 2,700 ms leave the full reply in the history, because its confirmation at 3,000 ms comes after the reply ended at 2,900 ms?
