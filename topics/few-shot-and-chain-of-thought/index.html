Few-Shot Prompting and Chain of Thought

Few-shot prompting puts a handful of solved input and output examples in the prompt so the model can follow the pattern they show. Chain-of-thought prompting has the model write its intermediate reasoning before the final answer, which improves accuracy on problems that take several steps.

1Overview

Why it matters

Most prompts that fail in production fail in one of two ways. The output has the wrong shape (wrong format, wrong length, wrong label for a borderline case), or the answer to a multi-step problem is wrong because the model jumped straight to a conclusion. Examples fix the first kind of failure and written reasoning fixes the second. Both are cheap to try and need no training run, and their effect can be measured on a small labelled test set.

They are also easy to get wrong. A badly chosen set of examples biases a classifier towards whichever label appears most often or last. A step-by-step instruction adds cost and latency to tasks that never needed it, and on a reasoning model it duplicates work the model already does internally. Anyone who writes, reviews or debugs prompts needs to know what each technique buys, what it costs in tokens and calls, and how the advice changes from one class of model to another.

Key terms

Zero-shot prompt
A prompt that contains instructions and the input but no solved examples of the task.
Few-shot prompt
A prompt that includes a small number of solved examples before the new input. Each example is called a shot, so a prompt with one example is one-shot.
In-context learning
A model picking up a task from examples in its prompt. Nothing is trained and no weights change; the effect lasts only for that request.
Exemplar
One solved example placed in a prompt, also called a demonstration. In chain-of-thought prompting it has three parts, namely the question, the reasoning and the answer.
Majority label bias
The tendency of a model to predict the answer that appears most often among the examples in its prompt.
Recency bias
The tendency of a model to repeat answers from the examples nearest the end of its prompt.
Chain of thought (CoT)
A series of intermediate reasoning steps that the model writes out before it gives its final answer.
Zero-shot CoT
Chain-of-thought prompting without examples, triggered by an instruction such as a request to think step by step.
Self-consistency
Running the same chain-of-thought prompt several times with sampling switched on and returning the answer that the most runs agree on.
Tree of thoughts (ToT)
A search procedure in which a program has the model propose several next steps, score the partial solutions, keep the best few and expand them, with the option to backtrack.

2Explanation

Introduction

In Prompt Engineering you wrote instructions: who the model is, what the task is, and what the output should look like. This lesson covers two things to add when instructions alone do not get the result you need. The first is examples of the task done well. The second is room for the model to work a problem out before it commits to an answer.

You already use both when you train a new colleague. Telling someone to write ticket summaries in the house style gets you something generic. Handing them three good summaries gets you the style, the length and the level of detail without your having to describe any of them. And when the task is a calculation or a decision with several conditions, you ask them to show their working, because an answer that arrives with its steps is right more often and is much easier to check.

Prompting has names for these ideas:

  • A zero-shot prompt contains instructions and the input, and no examples.
  • A few-shot prompt adds a small number of solved examples. Each one is a "shot". The model's weights do not change. It picks the pattern up from the text in front of it, which is called in-context learning.
  • A chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt has the model write intermediate steps before its answer.
  • Self-consistency and tree of thoughts build on chain of thought. They generate more than one line of reasoning and then choose between them.

Each row of the table costs more than the row above it: more input tokens for examples, more output tokens for reasoning, more model calls for voting or search. The skill is to climb only as far as the task needs. The sections below explain how each technique works, which examples to pick and in what order, how to get a parseable answer out of a reasoning chain, and what changes now that many models reason internally before they reply.

How it works

Zero-shot or few-shot

Instructions describe a task. Examples demonstrate it, and they carry things that are hard to describe: an output format, a tone, the boundary between two categories, how much detail is enough. A few-shot prompt is the zero-shot prompt with a block of solved cases placed before the new input:

text
Classify the review as Positive, Negative or Neutral. Reply with one word.

<examples>
<example>
Review: The battery lasts two full days.
Label: Positive
</example>
<example>
Review: It works, but the strap feels cheap.
Label: Neutral
</example>
<example>
Review: Stopped charging after a week.
Label: Negative
</example>
</examples>

Review: Setup took five minutes and everything just worked.
Label:

The tags follow a convention from Anthropic's guide, which recommends wrapping examples so that the model can tell them apart from instructions. Any delimiter works if you use it consistently.

Vendor guidance differs in emphasis. Google's Gemini prompting guide (opens in a new tab) recommends always including few-shot examples and says clear examples can even replace instructions. Anthropic's prompting guide (opens in a new tab) calls examples one of the most reliable ways to steer format, tone and structure, and suggests three to five. OpenAI's guide for reasoning models (opens in a new tab) says to try zero-shot first, because those models often do not need examples. Product teams report the same spread. Honeycomb's account of building its Query Assistant (opens in a new tab) says zero-shot prompting did not work for it, one example worked but poorly, and several examples seemed to work well.

A workable rule: start zero-shot with clear instructions, then add examples when outputs come back in the wrong shape or the model keeps misjudging the same kind of case.

Choosing examples

Taken together, the vendor guides ask for three properties:

  • Relevant. Examples look like real inputs, including their length and messiness.
  • Diverse. They cover edge cases and vary incidental features. If every example answer is two sentences long, expect two sentences back.
  • Consistently formatted. Every example uses the same labels, separators and whitespace, because the format is part of what is being taught.

For classification, balance the labels as well. Zhao and colleagues (opens in a new tab) showed with GPT-3 that a model favours answers that are frequent in the prompt (majority label bias) and answers that appear near its end (recency bias).

Examples can be fixed for every request or chosen per request. In dynamic few-shot selection you keep a pool of solved cases, embed the incoming input (see Embeddings), and retrieve its nearest neighbours to use as the examples. Liu and colleagues (opens in a new tab) found that examples retrieved this way consistently beat randomly chosen ones on GPT-3.

More examples are not always better. Each one costs input tokens on every request, and the Gemini guide warns that too many can make the model overfit to them. Start with three to five and measure.

Ordering examples

Order can matter as much as selection. In the Zhao study, changing only the order of four sentiment examples moved accuracy from 54.3% to 93.4%, and a prompt with three positive examples followed by one negative example produced a negative prediction almost 90% of the time. Those figures come from a small 2021 model. Liu and colleagues, using retrieved examples, saw little variation between orders: placing the most similar example last was best on one dataset and placing it first was slightly better on two others. No order is best everywhere, so use these defaults and test them:

  • Interleave labels, and do not end with a run of the same label.
  • With retrieved examples, put the most similar one last, then try the reverse on your own data.
  • Once an order tests well, freeze it so that results stay comparable from one run to the next.

Chain of thought

A language model writes its answer one token at a time, and each token is predicted from everything before it. Asked for a bare answer to a multi-step problem, it must get the result right in a single step. Asked to write the steps first, it can compute an intermediate value, put it on the page and use it in the next step.

Wei and colleagues (opens in a new tab) introduced the technique in few-shot form. Each exemplar has three parts: question, reasoning, answer. An exemplar in that style:

text
Q: A bakery had 40 rolls. It sold 26 in the morning and baked 18 more. How many rolls does it have now?
A: It started with 40. After selling 26 it had 40 - 26 = 14. Baking 18 more gives 14 + 18 = 32. The answer is 32.

With eight exemplars, the paper raised the share of GSM8K maths word problems solved from 17.9% to 56.9% on PaLM 540B and from 15.6% to 46.9% on GPT-3 (text-davinci-002). Two of its ablations show what matters. Reasoning written after the answer gave no gain. A run of dots in place of the reasoning (the same extra tokens with no content) gave none either. The benefit comes from meaningful steps that precede the answer.

Kojima and colleagues (opens in a new tab) showed that the examples are optional. Adding "Let's think step by step" to a zero-shot prompt raised the accuracy of GPT-3 (text-davinci-002) on the MultiArith benchmark from 17.7% to 78.7%. This zero-shot CoT still scored below few-shot CoT in their comparison and did not help on commonsense questions.

Two rules follow:

  1. Reasoning first, answer last, in a fixed place. End with a marker such as Answer: or an <answer> tag so that code can extract the result without reading the reasoning.
  2. Treat the chain as a draft. Wei and colleagues caution that nothing guarantees a correct reasoning path. A fluent chain can reach a wrong answer, or a right answer for a wrong reason.

Models with built-in thinking run this process internally before they reply. OpenAI's guide says that asking such models to think step by step is unnecessary. Anthropic's guide describes manual chain of thought as a fallback for when thinking is off, and adds that worked examples still help because they shape how the model approaches similar problems in its own thinking. Reasoning Models and Thinking Budgets continues from here.

Self-consistency

One chain of thought is one sample, and it can go wrong at any step. Self-consistency, from Wang and colleagues (opens in a new tab), runs the same CoT prompt several times with sampling on (a temperature above zero; see Sampling Parameters), extracts the final answer from each completion and returns the most common one. Different chains make different mistakes, while correct chains tend to agree. On GSM8K with PaLM-540B the paper reports 74.4% with 40 samples against 56.5% for a single greedy chain, and notes that five or ten samples capture most of the gain.

A simple model shows why voting helps and when it backfires. Suppose each sample is right with probability ppp, independently of the others, and every wrong sample gives the same wrong answer, which is the worst case for a vote. With nnn samples (nnn odd), the majority is right with probability

Pn=∑k=(n+1)/2n(nk) pk(1−p)n−kP_n = \sum_{k=(n+1)/2}^{n} \binom{n}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{n-k}Pn​=k=(n+1)/2∑n​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k

For p=0.6p = 0.6p=0.6 this gives 0.648 at n=3n = 3n=3 and 0.683 at n=5n = 5n=5. For p=0.4p = 0.4p=0.4 it gives 0.352 and 0.317. Voting amplifies whatever the model does most often, including a systematic mistake.

Real samples share a prompt and a model, so they are not independent, which makes real gains smaller than this model predicts. Against that, real wrong answers are often scattered over several values, so the right answer can win a plurality more easily than in the worst case assumed here. Use the formula to see the shape and measure on your own data. The cost is exact: nnn samples cost nnn times the tokens. The method also needs answers that can be compared for equality, such as a number, a label or a multiple-choice letter. The share of samples that agree with the winner is a useful confidence signal, and the paper found that it correlates strongly with accuracy. Voting over parallel calls is also one form of the parallelization pattern.

Tree of thoughts, in overview

Chain of thought follows one path. Self-consistency follows several and compares them only at the end. Tree of thoughts (ToT), from Yao and colleagues (opens in a new tab), compares them along the way. A controller program asks the model to propose several possible next steps, asks it again to rate each partial solution, keeps the most promising few and expands those, dropping branches that cannot succeed. It is a classical search (breadth-first or depth-first) in which the model acts as both move generator and evaluator.

On the Game of 24 puzzle, GPT-4 solved 4% of the test cases with a CoT prompt and 74% with ToT. The price was about 5,500 generated tokens per puzzle, and the authors say such search may be unnecessary for many tasks a model already does well. Treat ToT as a design idea (generate candidates, score them, prune) for problems where an early wrong step ruins everything and partial progress can be judged.

Worked example: A pricing question answered with retrieved examples, chain of thought and a five-sample vote

The task is to price an order: 3 notebooks at 4.50 each, a 20% discount coupon, and flat shipping of 5.00. The correct total is 15.80, because the coupon applies to the items (13.50 becomes 10.80) and shipping is added afterwards. A model can easily discount the shipping too, or forget the coupon.

The script builds a few-shot chain-of-thought prompt from a pool of four solved examples and then votes over five completions. So that everyone gets identical output, the completions are written into the script as stand-ins for five samples from a model. Everything else is real code that needs only the Python standard library. Save it as fewshot_cot.py.

python
import re
from collections import Counter

STOPWORDS = {"a", "an", "and", "at", "each", "for", "how", "in", "is", "it", "much", "of", "the", "to", "what"}
POOL = [  # solved examples: question, reasoning, answer
    {"id": "discount", "a": "14.40",
     "q": "A customer buys 2 mugs at 8.00 each with a 10% discount coupon. What is the total?",
     "r": "Items: 2 x 8.00 = 16.00. Discount: 10% of 16.00 = 1.60. Total: 16.00 - 1.60 = 14.40."},
    {"id": "shipping", "a": "8.00",
     "q": "A customer buys 4 pens at 1.25 each. Shipping is a flat 3.00. What is the total?",
     "r": "Items: 4 x 1.25 = 5.00. Shipping is added once: 5.00 + 3.00 = 8.00."},
    {"id": "coupon-shipping", "a": "9.00",
     "q": "A customer buys 5 folders at 2.00 each with a 50% discount coupon. "
          "Shipping is a flat 4.00. What is the total?",
     "r": "Items: 5 x 2.00 = 10.00. The coupon applies to items only: 50% of 10.00 = 5.00, leaving 5.00. "
          "Shipping is added after the discount: 5.00 + 4.00 = 9.00."},
    {"id": "refund", "a": "51.00",
     "q": "An order of 60.00 is refunded minus a 15% restocking fee. How much is refunded?",
     "r": "Fee: 15% of 60.00 = 9.00. Refund: 60.00 - 9.00 = 51.00."},
]
NEW_QUESTION = ("A customer buys 3 notebooks at 4.50 each with a 20% discount coupon. "
                "Shipping is a flat 5.00. What is the total?")
INSTRUCTIONS = ("Answer the pricing question. Write your reasoning on a line that starts with "
                "'Reasoning:', then a line that starts with 'Answer:' followed by the number only.")


def words(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())) - STOPWORDS


def similarity(a, b):
    """Jaccard similarity: shared words divided by all distinct words."""
    return len(words(a) & words(b)) / len(words(a) | words(b))


def select_examples(question, pool, k=3):
    ranked = sorted(pool, key=lambda ex: similarity(question, ex["q"]), reverse=True)
    return list(reversed(ranked[:k]))  # most similar example last, next to the new question


def build_prompt(question, examples):
    shots = "\n\n".join(f"Q: {ex['q']}\nReasoning: {ex['r']}\nAnswer: {ex['a']}" for ex in examples)
    return f"{INSTRUCTIONS}\n\n{shots}\n\nQ: {question}\nReasoning:"


def extract_answer(completion):
    match = re.search(r"Answer:\s*\$?(-?\d+(?:\.\d+)?)", completion)
    return f"{float(match.group(1)):.2f}" if match else None  # "15.8" and "$15.80" both become "15.80"


def majority_vote(completions):
    answers = [answer for answer in map(extract_answer, completions) if answer is not None]
    if not answers:
        return {"answer": None, "agreement": 0.0, "counts": {}}
    counts = Counter(answers)
    answer, votes = counts.most_common(1)[0]
    return {"answer": answer, "agreement": votes / len(completions), "counts": dict(counts)}


SAMPLED = [  # stand-ins for five samples of the same prompt at temperature 0.7
    " Items: 3 x 4.50 = 13.50. Coupon: 20% of 13.50 = 2.70, leaving 10.80. "
    "Shipping after the discount: 10.80 + 5.00 = 15.80.\nAnswer: 15.80",
    " Items cost 13.50. Add shipping: 18.50. Take 20% off: 18.50 x 0.80 = 14.80.\nAnswer: 14.80",
    " 3 notebooks are 13.50. 80% of 13.50 is 10.80. Plus 5.00 shipping is 15.80.\nAnswer: $15.80",
    " Items: 3 x 4.50 = 13.50. Shipping: 5.00. Total: 13.50 + 5.00 = 18.50.\nAnswer: 18.50",
    " Unit price after coupon: 4.50 x 0.80 = 3.60. Three cost 10.80. Add 5.00.\nAnswer: 15.8",
]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for ex in sorted(POOL, key=lambda ex: similarity(NEW_QUESTION, ex["q"]), reverse=True):
        print(f"{similarity(NEW_QUESTION, ex['q']):.3f}  {ex['id']}")
    chosen = select_examples(NEW_QUESTION, POOL)
    print("order in prompt:", [ex["id"] for ex in chosen])
    prompt = build_prompt(NEW_QUESTION, chosen)
    print("\n\n".join(prompt.split("\n\n")[-2:]))  # the last example and the new question
    print("extracted:", [extract_answer(completion) for completion in SAMPLED])
    print(majority_vote(SAMPLED))

What to notice:

  • Selection. After stopwords are removed the new question has 9 distinct words. It shares 8 of them with coupon-shipping, and the two questions contain 10 distinct words between them, so the score is 8 / 10 = 0.800. discount shares 6 of 10 and shipping shares 5 of 10. The refund example shares none and is left out.
  • Ordering. The three chosen examples are reversed so that the closest one sits next to the new question. It is the only example that shows the coupon being applied before shipping is added.
  • Chain of thought. Every example shows its reasoning and then an Answer: line. The printed tail of the prompt ends with Reasoning:, so the model continues in the same format.
  • Vote. Three chains reach 15.80. One discounts the shipping as well: (13.50 + 5.00) x 0.80 = 14.80. One forgets the coupon: 13.50 + 5.00 = 18.50. The vote returns 15.80 with an agreement of 3 / 5 = 0.6.
  • Normalisation. extract_answer turns 15.8 and $15.80 into 15.80. Without that step, 15.8 would count as a separate answer and agreement would fall to 0.4.

To use a live model, replace SAMPLED with five calls that send prompt at a temperature of about 0.7 and collect the text of each reply. Treat an agreement of 0.6 as a weak majority. A sensible policy returns the answer when agreement is 0.8 or higher and sends weaker cases to a person or a stronger model.

Edge cases and limitations

The model repeats an example's answer instead of following the pattern
Examples are strong evidence about what a good answer looks like, and a model can lean on them too literally. Zhao and colleagues measured this on a fact-completion task with four examples: about half of the model's predictions repeated one of the four example answers, roughly double the rate at which a repeat would have been correct. The sign is outputs that echo an example's wording, entity or length. Vary the examples, and when the same answers keep coming back, check whether they came from the prompt.
A step-by-step instruction makes vague inputs worse
Reasoning prompts assume there is a well-posed problem to reason about. Honeycomb reports that when it tested a zero-shot chain-of-thought prompt for its natural-language query feature, the prompt reliably failed to produce a query at all when the user's input was vague, and vague inputs were common in its real traffic. Evaluate a reasoning prompt on the inputs you really receive, including short and underspecified ones, before shipping it.
A confident vote for the wrong answer
Self-consistency measures agreement, not correctness. If the prompt or the model has a systematic mistake, most samples share it and the vote returns it with high agreement. In the simple model from this lesson, a per-sample accuracy of 0.4 falls to 0.317 with five samples. Check accuracy on a labelled test set before trusting the agreement rate as a confidence score.
Equal answers that do not match as text
A vote compares strings unless you tell it otherwise, so 15.8, 15.80 and \$15.80 count as three answers. Normalise numbers, case, whitespace and units before counting. Free-text answers cannot be voted on this way at all. The self-consistency paper says the method applies when the final answer comes from a fixed set, and that extending it to open text would need a separate measure of whether two outputs agree.
Asking a reasoning model to show its reasoning
On a model that thinks internally, a step-by-step instruction adds little. OpenAI's reasoning guide calls such prompts unnecessary. Anthropic's guide goes further for several of its newest models: it says to rely on built-in thinking, because a prompt that asks the model to write out its reasoning may be declined. If you need something for the user to read, ask for a short explanation of the answer, and control the depth of thinking with the provider's effort setting.
Small models reason fluently and wrongly
The original chain-of-thought paper found that the technique did not help small models. They produced chains that read well but were illogical, and scored below plain prompting; gains appeared only at around 100 billion parameters in the models of that time. If you run a small local model, measure chain of thought against a direct answer before adopting it.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The output must follow a format, tone or labelling convention that is easier to show than to describe. Add three to five examples.
  • The model keeps misjudging the same kind of borderline case. Add examples of exactly those cases, with the labels balanced.
  • Inputs vary widely and you have a pool of solved cases. Retrieve the most similar examples for each request.
  • The task needs several dependent steps, such as arithmetic or a policy with several conditions, and the model has no built-in thinking or it is switched off. Ask for reasoning before the answer.
  • A wrong answer is costly, answers can be compared for equality, and you can afford several calls. Add self-consistency and route low-agreement cases to a person.

Think twice when

  • The task is a lookup, an extraction or a one-step classification. Written reasoning adds output tokens and latency with no accuracy to gain.
  • You are using a reasoning model. Do not add a think-step-by-step instruction, and try the prompt without examples first.
  • The output is open-ended writing. There is no single answer to vote on, so self-consistency does not apply.
  • One reasoning chain already solves the task reliably. Tree of thoughts would multiply the cost for no gain.

A simpler alternative. A clear zero-shot prompt with an explicit output format is enough for most routine tasks. It stops being enough when outputs drift in shape or the same class of error keeps appearing in your test set, which is the point to add examples. For multi-step problems on a model that offers built-in thinking, switching thinking on or raising its effort setting is simpler than writing a chain-of-thought prompt by hand.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a clear zero-shot prompt, and add three to five relevant, varied, identically formatted examples when the output has the wrong shape or the same kind of case keeps going wrong.
  • Which examples you choose and the order you put them in can change results, so balance the labels, avoid ending on a run of one label, and test the order on your own data.
  • Chain of thought works because the model writes intermediate results before the answer and can then use them, so the reasoning must come first and the answer must sit in a fixed place that code can parse.
  • Self-consistency samples several reasoning chains and returns the majority answer, which costs one call per sample and amplifies a systematic error as readily as a correct answer.
  • Tree of thoughts is a search in which the model proposes and scores partial solutions, and it is worth its cost only where a single chain keeps failing.
  • Reasoning models do the step-by-step work internally, so drop think-step-by-step instructions for them and add examples only if a zero-shot prompt falls short.

Uber QueryGPT retrieving solved SQL examples for each plain-language question

UberDocumented in an engineering article

Uber's engineering article QueryGPT - Natural Language to SQL Using Generative AI (September 2024) describes an internal tool that turns a plain-language question into a SQL query for Uber's data platform. The article covers the first version and the redesign that followed. [14]

The problem

Writing a query means searching the data dictionary for the right datasets and then authoring SQL against them. The article says Uber's data platform handles about 1.2 million interactive queries a month and, as a conservative estimate, puts about 10 minutes on authoring one. A general model cannot know Uber's tables, so the article's first version gave it table schemas for the columns that exist and SQL samples for how to use them.

Where this topic fits

Few-shot examples are the core of the generation prompt. Before the model is called, a retrieval step decides which solved SQL samples and which table schemas go into it. In the redesigned version an intent step narrows the pool first, and further steps let the user confirm the tables and prune the schemas before the query is generated.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Uber keeps a pool of sample SQL queries alongside table schemas. The first version used 20 queries and 7 tier-1 tables.
  2. When a question arrives, the system converts it to a vector and runs a k-nearest-neighbour similarity search over the samples and schemas.
  3. The retrieved items, 7 SQL samples and 3 tables in the first version, are placed in the prompt as few-shot examples together with the question.
  4. In the current design an intent agent first maps the question to one or more workspaces, which are curated collections of SQL samples and tables for a business domain, so retrieval searches a smaller and more relevant pool.
  5. A table agent proposes the tables to use and the user confirms or edits the list. A column-prune agent then removes irrelevant columns from the schemas to save tokens.
  6. The generation call combines Uber's custom instructions, the schemas, the SQL samples and the question in a system prompt, and the model returns a SQL query with an explanation of how it built it. The article describes this for the first version and says the output structure is unchanged in the current design.

Why this approach fits

The knowledge the model lacks is local: which tables exist and how this company's analysts query them. Solved queries carry that knowledge in its most direct form. Retrieving them per question keeps the prompt on the relevant domain, which a fixed handful of examples could not do for every team. Because the pool is data and not code, people who know a domain can improve results by curating its samples without touching the prompt.

Limitation or tradeoff

Examples do not stop the model inventing things. The article says the model still sometimes writes queries that use tables or columns that do not exist, and that the team has not fully solved this. Retrieval quality also caps output quality. Anthropic's Define tools documentation specifies the field, and its engineering post Introducing advanced tool use on the Claude Developer Platform (November 2025) explains why the feature exists and reports its effect in internal testing. The problem

A model calls a tool by producing arguments that fit the tool's JSON Schema. The post points out that a schema says what is valid but not what is usual: which date format an API expects, what an identifier looks like, when an optional nested object should be filled in, or how one parameter relates to another. The result is tool calls that pass schema validation and are still malformed or inconsistent.

Where this topic fits

This is few-shot prompting applied to a model's structured output instead of its prose. The examples live inside the tool definition, and the API places them in the prompt alongside the schema. They are static: the same examples accompany every request that offers the tool.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The developer writes the tool's name, description and input schema as usual.
  2. The developer adds an input_examples array holding a few complete, realistic argument objects for that tool.
  3. Following the post's advice, the examples vary: one call with only the required fields, one partial call, and one that fills in the optional and nested fields, so the model sees which fields go together.
  4. The API checks every example against the schema and rejects the request if one does not conform.
  5. The examples reach the model as part of the prompt. When the model later calls the tool, it produces arguments that follow the conventions the examples demonstrate.

Why this approach fits

Format conventions and combinations of optional fields are what examples communicate well and prose communicates badly. Keeping the examples in the tool definition puts them next to the schema they illustrate, so the two are versioned and reviewed together. Validation at request time means an example cannot drift out of step with the schema without someone noticing.

Limitation or tradeoff

Examples add input tokens to every request that includes the tool. They can also over-steer: if every example uses the express service, the model may pick express too often, which is the majority label bias that affects any few-shot prompt. The documentation still ranks a detailed description above examples, and the field is not available for server tools such as web search. The accuracy figure comes from Anthropic's own internal testing, so treat it as a vendor's report and measure on your own tools.

Where this topic fits

That is chain of thought moved inside the agent loop (see The Agent Loop). The few-shot part is in the system prompt, which shows worked examples of the reasoning to do in that space.

  2. The system prompt tells the model to use the tool before acting on tool results: list the rules that apply to the request, check that all required information has been collected, and verify that the planned action complies with policy.
  3. The prompt then gives worked examples of such reasoning for typical requests, such as what to verify and which rules to check when a customer wants to cancel a flight.
  4. During a conversation the model calls think after receiving tool results, writes its analysis in the demonstrated shape, and then makes the next real tool call or replies to the customer.
  5. Anthropic compared four configurations on the benchmark: a baseline, extended thinking alone, the think tool alone, and the think tool with the optimised prompt.

Why this approach fits

The policy is too long and conditional to apply reliably in one step, and the facts it depends on arrive piecemeal from tool calls. Writing down the applicable rules and the missing facts before acting is the mechanism of chain of thought: intermediate conclusions become text that the next step can use. The worked examples matter because they show what a useful thought contains in this domain. Microsoft's Medprompt: retrieved examples, generated reasoning and a shuffled vote on medical exams

The paper Can Generalist Foundation Models Outcompete Special-Purpose Tuning? Case Study in Medicine (Nori and colleagues, Microsoft, November 2023) and the Microsoft Research post The Power of Prompting describe Medprompt, a prompting strategy for GPT-4 on multiple-choice medical exam questions. [12][13]

The problem

Medical question answering had been treated as a job for models trained specially on medical text. The authors asked whether a general model with no extra training could match those specialist systems through prompting alone, without clinicians hand-writing the prompt examples. The test bed was a suite of medical question-answering benchmarks that includes MedQA, a dataset of United States medical licensing exam (USMLE) questions.

Where this topic fits

Medprompt composes three of this lesson's techniques into one pipeline. Dynamic few-shot selection chooses the examples, chain of thought is the format of every example and of the model's answer, and a self-consistency vote picks the final answer. A preprocessing pass builds the example pool before any test question is asked.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. In preprocessing, every training question is embedded and GPT-4 is asked to write a chain of thought and an answer for it.
  2. If the generated answer matches the known correct answer, the question, its embedding, its reasoning and its answer are stored. Otherwise the question is discarded, which removes chains that led to a wrong answer.
  3. At inference time the test question is embedded with the same model and its 5 nearest stored examples are retrieved by cosine similarity.
  4. The prompt is built from those 5 examples, each with its generated reasoning, followed by the test question. The model replies with its own chain of thought and a choice.
  5. The call is made 5 times. Before each one the order of the answer options is shuffled, which varies the reasoning and cancels any preference for a particular option position.
  6. The most frequent answer across the 5 runs is returned.

Why this approach fits

Each technique covers a different weakness. Retrieved examples put the most relevant worked reasoning in front of the model for each question. Generated chains of thought remove the need for clinicians to write explanations, and the correct-answer filter keeps the worst chains out of the pool. The vote suits the task because the answer is one of a few letters, which is the setting where self-consistency applies cleanly, and shuffling the options also cancels position bias.

Limitation or tradeoff

Every question costs five model calls with long prompts, on top of a preprocessing pass over the training set. The method needs a labelled training set to build its pool and answers that are multiple-choice letters; the authors say they did not test adaptations to other answer formats. A chain that reaches the right answer can still contain wrong reasoning, which the label filter cannot detect. The authors also state that strong benchmark scores do not show the method works in real clinical practice, and that contamination of the model's training data by benchmark questions cannot be ruled out.

What the sources document

  • The paper presents Medprompt as a composition of three techniques: dynamic few-shot selection, self-generated chain of thought and choice shuffle ensembling. Microsoft's research post describes the same three components. [12][13]
  • For dynamic few-shot selection, training and test questions are embedded with text-embedding-ada-002 and the nearest training examples are retrieved for each test question. The standard configuration uses 5 retrieved examples and an ensemble of 5 API calls. [12]
  • GPT-4 generates a chain of thought and an answer for each training question, and the sample is discarded if that answer does not match the ground-truth label. [12]
  • For the ensemble, the answer choices are shuffled before each reasoning path is generated and the most frequent answer is selected. The paper says shuffling reduces position bias and increases the diversity of the reasoning paths. [12]
  • The standard configuration scored 90.2% on MedQA. In the paper's ablation, chain-of-thought steps contributed the most (3.4 points), followed by few-shot prompting and choice shuffle ensembling (2.2 points each). The authors caution that benchmark performance does not demonstrate real-world efficacy in healthcare. [12]

Tree of Thoughts solving the Game of 24 with GPT-4

Princeton University and Google DeepMindDocumented in a paper

The paper Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models (Yao and colleagues, Princeton University and Google DeepMind, 2023) evaluates the method on three tasks, and its official code repository holds the prompts and a runnable implementation. This example covers the Game of 24 experiment. [8][9]

The problem

In the Game of 24 the player combines four numbers with addition, subtraction, multiplication and division to make exactly 24, using each number once. The first operation often decides whether a solution is still possible, and a model that writes left to right cannot take a step back. The paper found that about 60% of chain-of-thought samples had already failed after their first step.

Where this topic fits

ToT replaces the single prompt with a search program around the model. The program holds the tree of partial solutions. It calls the model with a propose prompt to generate next steps and with a value prompt to judge each resulting state. Few-shot examples still appear, inside those two prompts.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. The task is split into three thought steps. Each step is one equation that combines two of the remaining numbers.
  2. For each state the program holds, a propose prompt asks the model for possible next equations.
  3. A value prompt asks the model whether each resulting set of numbers can still reach 24, answering sure, maybe or impossible. The paper samples this judgement three times per candidate.
  4. The program keeps the five best states (breadth b = 5) and discards the rest, which makes the procedure a breadth-first search.
  5. After the third step, a surviving state whose one remaining number is 24 gives the final equation.

Why this approach fits

The puzzle has the two properties that make search pay off. A wrong first move is fatal, so committing to one path is risky. And a partial state can be judged cheaply: with three numbers left it is often easy to see that 24 is out of reach. Pruning those branches early spends the token budget on states that can still succeed, which is how ToT beat even the best of 100 independent chain-of-thought attempts in the paper.

Limitation or tradeoff

The cost is high. The paper reports about 5,500 generated tokens and 0.74 US dollars per puzzle, and each task needs its own propose and value prompts plus controller code. Results depended on model strength: with GPT-3.5 the success rate fell to 19%, and they vary between runs, since the repository reports a rerun at 69% against the paper's 74%. The authors say such deliberate search may be unnecessary for many tasks that a strong model already handles, and suggest it for hard tasks where chain of thought struggles.

What the sources document

  • The test set was 100 relatively hard games, indexed 901 to 1,000 in data taken from 4nums.com. An output counted as a success if it was a valid equation equal to 24 that used each input number exactly once. [8]
  • In the ToT setup the task is decomposed into 3 thought steps, each an intermediate equation. Breadth-first search keeps the best b = 5 candidates per step, and the model evaluates each candidate as sure, maybe or impossible, with values sampled 3 times per thought. [8]
  • With GPT-4, standard prompting solved 7.3% of games, chain-of-thought prompting 4.0% and self-consistent chain of thought 9.0%, while ToT solved 45% with b = 1 and 74% with b = 5. The best of 100 chain-of-thought samples reached 49%. [8]
  • The paper's cost analysis reports about 5.5k generated tokens and 0.74 US dollars per case for ToT, against 6.7k generated tokens and 0.47 dollars for best-of-100 chain of thought. Its limitations section says such search might not be necessary for many tasks GPT-4 already excels at. [8]

4Common mistakes

  1. Writing examples that all share one label, one length or one phrasing.

    Why it causes problems
    The model treats the shared feature as part of the task. A classifier drifts towards the over-represented label, and a generator copies the length and opening words of the examples whatever the input calls for.
    How to recognise it
    Count the labels in the prompt and in a batch of outputs. If the output distribution mirrors the prompt and not your test set, the examples are steering it. For generated text, look for outputs that all begin the same way or have the same length.
    How to fix it
    Balance the labels, vary length and wording, and include at least one edge case. When examples are retrieved per request, enforce the balance in code, as the first exercise does.

  2. Asking for the answer first and the reasoning after it.

    Why it causes problems
    Once the answer has been written, later text cannot change it, so the model is justifying an answer and not working one out. The chain-of-thought paper tested this arrangement and found it performed about the same as no reasoning at all. A JSON schema whose answer field comes before its reasoning field has the same problem.
    How to recognise it
    Read the output format in the prompt, the examples and any schema, and check which comes first. Accuracy that does not move when you add a request to explain the reasoning is another sign.
    How to fix it
    Put the reasoning before the answer in the instructions, in every example and in the schema, and keep the final answer behind a fixed marker or in a fixed field.

  3. Extracting the final answer with a loose pattern, such as the last number in the reply.

    Why it causes problems
    A reasoning chain is full of intermediate values, so a loose pattern silently returns the wrong one. In a vote, inconsistent formats also split the count between answers that are really equal, which lowers the agreement rate and can change the winner.
    How to recognise it
    Parsed answers that appear in the reasoning but not on the answer line, replies without a marker that were still counted, and agreement rates that jump when you normalise number formats.
    How to fix it
    Require a fixed marker or tag, parse only what follows it, normalise the value, and treat a reply with no marker as an abstention instead of guessing.

  4. Running self-consistency at temperature 0, or through a cache that returns one stored reply.

    Why it causes problems
    The samples come back identical or nearly so. You pay for several calls, get one answer, and see a perfect agreement score that carries no information.
    How to recognise it
    The completions are word-for-word identical, and agreement is 1.0 even on questions the model gets wrong on your test set.
    How to fix it
    Sample at a temperature of roughly 0.5 to 0.7, the range used in the self-consistency paper, confirm that the reasoning text differs between samples, and bypass any response cache for these calls.

  5. Carrying a think-step-by-step instruction and a large example block over to a reasoning model unchanged.

    Why it causes problems
    The model already reasons internally, so the instruction adds tokens and latency without adding accuracy. OpenAI's guide also warns that examples which do not align closely with the instructions can produce poor results, and Anthropic's guide notes that some models may decline a request to write out their reasoning.
    How to recognise it
    After a model upgrade, responses get longer and slower with no change in accuracy, the visible answer restates reasoning nobody asked to read, or requests are declined.
    How to fix it
    Re-test with a plain zero-shot prompt first, add examples back only if a measured gap remains, and control reasoning depth with the provider's effort setting.

  6. Tuning the examples on the same cases that are used to report accuracy.

    Why it causes problems
    You end up choosing examples that happen to suit those cases, so the reported accuracy overstates what new inputs will get. With retrieved examples it is worse: a test case that also sits in the pool is answered by lookup.
    How to recognise it
    Accuracy falls when fresh cases are tried, or the example pool and the test set share items.
    How to fix it
    Keep three separate sets: the example pool, a development set for tuning, and a held-out test set that nobody looks at while tuning. The Medprompt authors describe setting aside such an unseen set to guard against overfitting.

import re
from itertools import permutations

STOPWORDS = {"a", "an", "and", "for", "i", "in", "is", "it", "my", "of", "on", "the", "to", "was"}

POOL = [
    {"id": "b1", "text": "I was charged twice for my subscription this month", "label": "billing"},
    {"id": "b2", "text": "My card was charged after I cancelled the subscription", "label": "billing"},
    {"id": "b3", "text": "The invoice for this month shows the wrong amount", "label": "billing"},
    {"id": "t1", "text": "The app crashes when I open the dashboard", "label": "technical"},
    {"id": "t2", "text": "Export to CSV fails with an error this month", "label": "technical"},
    {"id": "t3", "text": "The page loads slowly on my phone", "label": "technical"},
    {"id": "a1", "text": "I cannot log in after changing my password", "label": "account"},
    {"id": "a2", "text": "Please change the email address on my account", "label": "account"},
    {"id": "a3", "text": "I want to cancel my subscription and delete my account", "label": "account"},
]

QUERY = "I was charged for my subscription twice this month and I want to cancel"


def words(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())) - STOPWORDS


def similarity(a, b):
    wa, wb = words(a), words(b)
    return len(wa & wb) / len(wa | wb)


def select_top_k(query, pool, k=3):
    """Naive selector: the k most similar examples, whatever their labels."""
    return sorted(pool, key=lambda ex: similarity(query, ex["text"]), reverse=True)[:k]


def select_balanced(query, pool):
    """One example per label: the most similar example inside each label."""
    labels = sorted({ex["label"] for ex in pool})
    return [max((ex for ex in pool if ex["label"] == label),
                key=lambda ex: similarity(query, ex["text"]))
            for label in labels]


def render_prompt(query, examples):
    labels = ", ".join(sorted({ex["label"] for ex in POOL}))
    shots = "\n\n".join(f"Ticket: {ex['text']}\nLabel: {ex['label']}" for ex in examples)
    return f"Classify the support ticket as one of: {labels}.\n\n{shots}\n\nTicket: {query}\nLabel:"


def order_variants(examples):
    """Every ordering of the chosen examples, for measuring order sensitivity."""
    return [list(order) for order in permutations(examples)]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    naive = select_top_k(QUERY, POOL)
    print("naive   :", [(ex["id"], ex["label"], round(similarity(QUERY, ex["text"]), 3)) for ex in naive])
    balanced = select_balanced(QUERY, POOL)
    print("balanced:", [(ex["id"], ex["label"], round(similarity(QUERY, ex["text"]), 3)) for ex in balanced])
    variants = order_variants(balanced)
    print("variants:", len(variants))
    for order in variants:
        print("  ", " > ".join(ex["label"] for ex in order))
    print()
    print(render_prompt(QUERY, variants[0]))

Decide how many samples a self-consistency vote is worth

A team answers 1,000 invoice-coding questions a day with a few-shot chain-of-thought prompt. One call uses 900 input tokens and 180 output tokens. The model costs $0.50 per million input tokens and $2.00 per million output tokens. On a labelled test set, a single sampled answer is right 70% of the time.

Use the voting model from this lesson: samples are independent, and all wrong samples give the same wrong answer.

  1. What does one call cost? What do 1,000 questions cost with n=1,3,5n = 1, 3, 5n=1,3,5 and 777 samples each?
  2. What is the probability that the majority answer is right for n=3,5n = 3, 5n=3,5 and 777?
  3. How many wrong answers per 1,000 questions do you expect at each nnn? For each step up (1 to 3, 3 to 5, 5 to 7), what is the extra daily cost per additional error avoided?
  4. Correcting a wrong answer costs the team about $0.05 of a person's time. Which nnn would you choose, and what would you check before committing to it?
  5. A second task has a single-sample accuracy of 45%. Compute the majority accuracy for n=3n = 3n=3 and n=5n = 5n=5 and say what the team should do in place of voting.

Requirements

  • Show the arithmetic for the cost of one call and for each majority probability.
  • Report wrong answers per 1,000 questions and the cost per avoided error for each step up in n.
  • For part 4, name the assumption in the voting model that real samples violate and say how that changes your choice.
  • For part 5, state a rule for when voting should not be used at all.

Expected behaviour

One call costs $0.00081, so 1,000 questions cost $0.81, $2.43, $4.05 and $5.67 for n=1,3,5,7n = 1, 3, 5, 7n=1,3,5,7. The majority is right with probability 0.784, 0.837 and 0.874. Expected wrong answers per 1,000 are 300, 216, about 163 and about 126. The cost per avoided error rises from about $0.019 to $0.031 to $0.044. For the second task the majority accuracy falls to 0.425 and 0.407, so voting makes it worse.

Hint 1

For n = 3 the majority is right when 2 or 3 samples are right. There are 3 ways for exactly 2 of 3 to be right.

Hint 2

The extra cost of each step up is the same, because every step adds two calls per question.

Hint 3

Compare the cost per avoided error with the cost of correcting one error by hand.

The model assumes independent samples. Real samples share a prompt and a model, so their mistakes are correlated and the true gains are probably smaller than these figures, although wrong answers scattered over several values push the other way. The step from 5 to 7 is already close to break-even, and a modest shortfall would make it a loss. A defensible decision is to start at n=5n = 5n=5, measure real majority accuracy at n=5n = 5n=5 and n=7n = 7n=7 on the labelled test set, and move to 7 only if the measured gain justifies it. Voting lowers accuracy from 0.45 to 0.425 and then 0.407 while multiplying the cost. The rule: under this model, do not vote unless a single sample is right more than half the time. If wrong answers are scattered over many values a plurality can still win below 50%, so confirm the cut-off on a test set. The team should first improve the single call, with better examples, clearer instructions, a stronger model or built-in thinking, and come back to voting once single-sample accuracy is comfortably above 50%.
