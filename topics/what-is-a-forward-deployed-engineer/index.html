What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer employed by a product company who works inside a customer's team and systems to get that product solving the customer's real problem in production. The FDE also carries what the customer's situation reveals back to the people who build the product.

1Overview

Why it matters

Buying an AI product and getting value from it are two separate events. A model or an agent platform does nothing for an insurer until it is connected to the insurer's claims data, fitted into how claims handlers work, tested on the insurer's own cases and trusted by the people who use it. Projects stall in that gap. OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering has said that after ChatGPT appeared companies found it hard to get value from the models, and that the only approach that worked consistently was to embed with clients, learn their workflows and work alongside their staff (Business Insider, republished by AOL (opens in a new tab)). The forward deployed engineer is the role built to close that gap.

You need to understand the role for two reasons. If you want the job, you should know that it combines production engineering, customer discovery and judgement about scope, often in the same week, and that the title is used loosely enough that some advertised FDE roles are really pre-sales or support work. If you work next to FDEs, as a product engineer, a solutions engineer or a customer, you should know what they are accountable for, so that you do not treat them as demo builders or as rented staff.

Before you start

No prerequisites. You can start here.

Key terms

Forward deployed engineer (FDE)
A software engineer employed by a product company who works inside a customer's team and systems, builds the solution there, and feeds what they learn back into the product.
Delta and Dev
Palantir's names for its two engineering roles. A Delta is a forward deployed engineer serving one customer. A Dev builds one part of the platform for every customer.
Embedding
Working as part of a customer's team for weeks or months, often on their premises, with access to their systems, data and users.
Solutions engineer
A technical specialist, also called a sales engineer or solutions architect, who helps win and keep customers by explaining, demonstrating and prototyping the product.
Consultant or systems integrator
Someone from a services firm who advises a client or builds a system to the client's specification, without a product of their own to improve.
Product engineer
An engineer on the vendor's core product team who builds capabilities intended for every customer.
Eval
A repeatable quality check for an AI application, usually a set of real cases with expected results that the system is scored against after each change.
Generalising
Turning something built for one customer into a product capability that many customers can use with little or no custom work.
Pilot
A limited first deployment with real users, run to find out whether the solution works before the customer commits to a full rollout.
Product-led growth (PLG)
A way of selling software in which customers adopt the product by themselves, without a sales call or an implementation project.

2Explanation

Introduction

Most software engineers build for people they never meet. A request reaches them as a ticket written by a product manager, who heard it from an account manager, who heard it from a customer's manager, who heard it from the person doing the work. Every hop drops detail, and the engineer builds from what is left.

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) removes the hops. The engineer goes to the customer, which may be an insurer's claims floor, a factory or a bank's operations team, watches the work, and builds the solution there, using their own company's product as the main building material. The name is literal. Ramp's engineering blog describes the engineers of Palantir, the company where the role began, as deployed at military sites and customer offices (Ramp (opens in a new tab)).

An everyday comparison is a fitter employed by an oven manufacturer. A showroom adviser explains what the oven can do. An independent builder will fit whatever kitchen you draw. The manufacturer's own fitter comes to your house, finds that the gas line is in the wrong place, gets the oven working anyway, and then tells the factory which fitting keeps causing trouble in houses like yours. An FDE is that fitter: one customer gets a working result, and every later customer gets a better product.

So the job runs on two loops at once.

The customer loop ends with software running in the customer's production systems and handed over to their team. The product loop ends with a product that needs less custom work the next time. A role that runs only the first loop is consulting. A role that runs only the second is product engineering. The FDE is defined by running both.

This lesson covers where the role came from, the four responsibilities that published descriptions of it share, how an FDE differs from a solutions engineer, a consultant and a product engineer, and why AI companies in particular are hiring FDEs. The next lesson, The FDE Engagement Lifecycle, follows one engagement phase by phase.

How it works

Where the role came from

The role began at Palantir, a data-software company founded in 2003 whose early customers were mostly government agencies. Its engineers worked at the customer's site, so they could understand problems and iterate quickly (Ramp (opens in a new tab)). Accounts differ on the date. A biography that Palantir's chief technology officer, Shyam Sankar, submitted to the US House Armed Services Committee says he joined in 2006 as employee number 13 and envisaged the role (House record (opens in a new tab)). The Pragmatic Engineer and Salesforce both date the named role to the early 2010s.

Palantir called these engineers Deltas and its product engineers Devs. In a 2019 post on the Palantir Blog (opens in a new tab), also quoted by The Pragmatic Engineer (opens in a new tab), Palantir's own summary is that a Dev's focus is "one capability, many customers" and a Delta's is "one customer, many capabilities". Deltas belonged to Business Development and measured success by the effect on the customer's goal. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that until about 2016 Palantir had more Deltas than ordinary software engineers, and that after the Foundry platform launched that year more of them moved back into product work.

For years the role stayed close to Palantir. Ramp's account is that Scale AI and C3.ai adopted the title in the late 2010s and that Databricks and Snowflake built similar functions. AI products made it mainstream. OpenAI started its FDE team at the beginning of 2025 with two engineers (The Pragmatic Engineer), and Salesforce launched its team in April 2025. An Indeed and Financial Times analysis, cited on Salesforce's blog, found job postings for the role rose by more than 800% between January and September 2025 (Salesforce (opens in a new tab)).

The four responsibilities

Descriptions of the job cut it up differently. FDE Academy (opens in a new tab), a selective eight-month training programme, uses a six-step cycle, and the roadmap.sh Forward Deployed Engineer roadmap (opens in a new tab) uses three phases: audit, evals and deployment. Underneath, the same four responsibilities recur. OpenAI details below are from The Pragmatic Engineer unless another source is linked.

1. Find the real problem. Palantir's job posting (opens in a new tab) says projects often start with an open-ended question, such as why so many flights are being delayed. A Salesforce FDE director describes peeling back a request to find out why the customer is making it. The skills are covered in Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering and Technical Scoping and Sequencing.

2. Build working software in the customer's environment. This separates the role from advisory work. OpenAI's FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure with the customer's tooling. An Anthropic posting for the role lists building production applications inside customer systems and delivering MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills (posting (opens in a new tab)). That means working under the customer's access rules and security reviews: see Deploying in Customer Environments.

3. Own the outcome. An FDE is judged by whether the customer's number moved, not by whether a deliverable was accepted. Palantir's example is a manufacturer that wants fewer defective products coming off the line. OpenAI's FDEs agree validation criteria with the customer and report measured results against them before delivery. Ramp says its FDEs stay accountable after the deal closes and after implementation ends.

4. Feed the product. The FDE is the product team's closest view of real use. At OpenAI this is scheduled: regular sessions with the research team, fortnightly readouts with product leaders and a shared channel of field notes. At Palantir, when a capability the customer needs is missing, the FDE can do the Dev work of adding it. This loop is the subject of From Field Work to Product.

Two published numbers calibrate the mix. An FDE at Baseten estimates their time as about 75% software engineering and model optimisation, 15% technical consulting and solution design, and 10% customer relationship management (Baseten (opens in a new tab)). OpenAI tells new FDEs to expect about half their time on the customer's site (Engineering Leadership (opens in a new tab)).

How an FDE differs from neighbouring roles

Solutions engineer (also sales engineer or solutions architect). The US Bureau of Labor Statistics describes sales engineers (opens in a new tab) as selling products or services that need technical expertise: presenting, working out requirements, and helping sales teams secure and renew orders. OpenAI keeps both roles. Its solutions architects are advisory, rarely write code on customer infrastructure, and usually build proofs of concept on anonymised or offline data. Its FDEs write code on the customer's infrastructure and work with more ambiguity.

Consultant or systems integrator. A services firm has no product of its own to improve. Marty Cagan describes the classic custom-solutions model as one where the client supplies the specification, a price is agreed and the firm builds what was requested, so the firm cannot commit to an outcome because the solution was dictated to it (SVPG (opens in a new tab)). An FDE is expected to question the specification, and builds on one vendor's product. Palantir's own blog draws the same line: consultants generally deliver a one-time recommendation or solution, Deltas build a long-term one with the customer (Palantir Blog (opens in a new tab)).

Product engineer. Palantir's Dev works on one capability for many customers. A product engineer serves the general case and can answer "not on the roadmap". An FDE serves one customer's result and must find another route to it. Each needs the other: without product engineers the FDE's work stays bespoke, and without FDEs product engineers guess what customers need.

Five yes-or-no tests make the comparison usable on a job description:

  1. Is the employer the company whose product is being deployed?
  2. Does the engineer work inside one customer's team for weeks or longer?
  3. Does their code run in the customer's production systems?
  4. Are they measured on the customer's result after go-live?
  5. Are they expected to change the core product?

Five yes answers describe an FDE whatever the title says. Titles vary: Sierra calls the role agent engineer (Latent Space (opens in a new tab)), and Microsoft's Industry Solutions Engineering team describes working side by side with customers while helping Microsoft improve its products (ISE (opens in a new tab)), though its page does not say how engineers are measured.

Why AI companies hire FDEs

An AI product is unfinished until it is connected. a16z argues that enterprise AI products need deep integration with a company's internal databases, APIs and workflows before the model has the context to be useful (a16z (opens in a new tab)).

Quality has to be measured on the customer's own cases. No public benchmark says whether a model is good enough for one company's call centre. OpenAI's FDEs build evals with users, label examples and improve the system against them. See Evaluating LLM Applications.

Trust takes longer than the build. At Morgan Stanley, OpenAI's head of FDE told a podcast, the technical work took six to eight weeks and a further four months went on pilots, evaluations and iteration with financial advisers before, in his words, about 98% of them adopted it (reported by Business Insider).

The vendor is still learning what to build. In one OpenAI project a voice model was not good enough for a call-centre customer to commit. The FDEs took their eval data to the research team, the model improved, and the Realtime API improved for every customer.

The price is margin, because engineers on site cost far more than documentation. a16z's case is that early in a platform shift the trade is worth making, and it notes that ServiceNow and Workday went public with gross margins of 63.2% and 54.1%, well under the roughly 80% expected of software. Ted Mabrey, writing from inside Palantir, gives the opposing warning: companies that copy the title often absorb integration costs without gaining long-term leverage (Mabrey (opens in a new tab)).

The tension inside the role

Every week an FDE decides whether to patch something for this customer or build it properly for all of them. Ramp prefers the general feature, because one-off customisations make a codebase harder to maintain. Cagan's warning is that a company with only FDEs ends up maintaining thousands of bespoke solutions. The worked example puts numbers on that decision.

Worked example: Four weeks at an insurer: a time log, five tests and a patch-or-generalise decision

This is a teaching scenario with invented data, built so that you can reproduce every number.

Setup. A vendor sells an AI agent platform. A regional insurer has bought it and asked for "AI that reads claims". One FDE spends four weeks with the claims team and logs every block of work with the responsibility it served and where it was done. The script holds the log and two calculations.

rows = [dict(row, hours=int(row["hours"])) for row in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(LOG))]
total = sum(row["hours"] for row in rows)
print(f"entries={len(rows)} total_hours={total}")

for name in ["discover", "build", "outcome", "product", "internal"]:
    hours = sum(row["hours"] for row in rows if row["responsibility"] == name)
    print(f"{name:<9}{hours:>4} h {hours / total:>6.1%}")

onsite = sum(row["hours"] for row in rows if row["place"] == "onsite")
print(f"on site  {onsite:>4} h {onsite / total:>6.1%}")

# Patch or generalise? Costs in engineer-days.
PATCH_PER_CUSTOMER = 3.0      # one-off adapter, built and maintained for a year
GENERAL_ONCE = 16.0           # connector built into the product
GENERAL_PER_CUSTOMER = 0.5    # configuring that connector for one customer

break_even = GENERAL_ONCE / (PATCH_PER_CUSTOMER - GENERAL_PER_CUSTOMER)
print(f"break-even = {break_even:.1f} customers; generalising is cheaper from customer {math.floor(break_even) + 1}")
for customers in [4, 7, 10]:
    patch = PATCH_PER_CUSTOMER * customers
    general = GENERAL_ONCE + GENERAL_PER_CUSTOMER * customers
    print(f"{customers:>2} customers: patch={patch:>4.1f} days  general={general:>4.1f} days  saving={patch - general:>5.1f}")

The answer depends on nnn, which is a forecast, so write the assumption down and revisit it when the pipeline changes.

Edge cases and limitations

The title without the job
Some roles advertised as forward deployed engineering are configuration, support or pre-sales work, with no production code and no route into the product. The Pragmatic Engineer notes that every company has its own flavour of the role and that some treat it as technical consulting. Apply the five tests, and ask in the interview where your code would run, which organisation the team reports to, and how the last field finding became a product change.
The job under another title
Sierra calls its customer-facing agent builders agent engineers, a16z describes Decagon's technical Agent Product Managers doing implementation work with customers, and Microsoft's Industry Solutions Engineering team works side by side with customers and with product teams. If you search only for the three-letter title you will miss these. Search job descriptions for embedding, production deployment and customer outcomes instead.
The customer starts treating the FDE as extra headcount
An engineer who sits with the team and solves problems attracts unrelated requests, and the engagement never ends. Engineering Leadership reports OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering describing the aim as solving the problem and ideally becoming unnecessary; being needed again for the same problem usually means it was not solved. Agree exit criteria at the start and hand over with a runbook, as covered in the lesson on production handoff and support.
Environments you cannot take work out of
A former Palantir FDE told The Pragmatic Engineer he worked on the final assembly line at Airbus and that peers worked in air-gapped environments, and the Palantir posting read for this lesson, which is for Warsaw, requires the ability to obtain a security clearance. In such places you may have no cloud tools, no way to copy data out and a slow path for installing anything. Plan for more on-site time, synthetic data for off-site work and longer feedback loops.
Embedded, but not neutral
An FDE sits with the customer and is paid by the vendor. Published descriptions are open about the commercial side. Palantir's S-1 lists its forward-deployed engineers' ability to help customers identify new use cases among the factors in a customer's decision to expand, and a Ramp advert quoted by The Pragmatic Engineer asks FDEs to work with sales to close deals. When the right answer for the customer is a tool the vendor does not sell, or no build at all, say so plainly. Trust lost by overselling costs more than one expansion is worth.
Several customers at once
The Baseten account describes juggling multiple in-depth projects while managing customer relationships, with a constant risk of disappearing into a technical rabbit hole. The more accounts one engineer carries, the less embedding is possible and the closer the job drifts to solutions engineering. If a role assigns many accounts per engineer, expect shallower discovery and ask which accounts get real on-site time.

When to use it, and when something simpler is enough

Use it when

  • The product delivers value only after it is integrated with a customer's internal systems, data and workflows, and the customer cannot do that integration alone.
  • Contracts are large enough to pay for weeks of an engineer's time per customer, as in enterprise sales.
  • The product is young and the vendor still needs real deployments to learn what to build, so field findings can change the roadmap.
  • Success has to be proven on the customer's own data before they will commit, for example with evals for an AI feature.

Think twice when

  • The product is a consumer product or is adopted self-serve. Ramp lists consumer and PLG-only companies as cases where the model does not apply.
  • No product team is ready to generalise what the field finds, so every engagement would leave bespoke code to maintain for ever.
  • The customer wants a system built to its own fixed specification with no product involved, which is work for a consultancy or an internal team.
  • You personally want long uninterrupted stretches on one technical problem. Published accounts describe heavy context switching and 25 to 50 percent travel.

A simpler alternative. Solutions engineers, good documentation, reference implementations and implementation partners are enough for a product that customers can mostly set up themselves, especially if product engineers also visit customers and meet users from time to time, which Marty Cagan recommends as the minimum. This stops being enough when deals stall after purchase because customers cannot get the product into production, or when the same integration gap shows up in several accounts and nobody owns closing it.

Key takeaways

  • A forward deployed engineer works for a product company but inside one customer's team and systems, and is judged by the customer's result in production.
  • The role began at Palantir, which called these engineers Deltas and summed up the split as one customer, many capabilities for a Delta and one capability, many customers for a product engineer.
  • Four responsibilities recur in descriptions of the job. They are finding the real problem, building in the customer's environment, owning the outcome and feeding the product.
  • A solutions engineer mostly stops at the demo or proof of concept, a consultant has no product to improve, and a product engineer serves the general case. The FDE runs the customer loop and the product loop together.
  • AI companies hire FDEs because AI products need integration, quality has to be proven on the customer's own cases, adoption takes longer than the build, and the vendor is still learning what to build.
  • The hardest recurring judgement is whether to patch for one customer or generalise for many, and a job title alone never tells you whether a role is really forward deployed engineering.

Example 1 of 5

Palantir's Deltas and Devs: where the forward deployed role started

PalantirDocumented in a case study

Palantir sells data platforms to governments and large companies. Its own job posting for Forward Deployed Software Engineers, its 2019 Palantir Blog post on Deltas and Devs (also quoted by The Pragmatic Engineer), its 2020 S-1 filing and a biography of its chief technology officer in the US House record together describe how the company organised engineering around the role. [6][4][7][8][5]

The problem

Palantir's customers arrive with open-ended questions, not specifications. The posting gives two: why are we delaying so many flights, and how can we better identify instances of money laundering. Answering them means working with the customer's own data, which The Pragmatic Engineer describes as often sensitive and proprietary, inside organisations that its author suspects would struggle to integrate such a platform by themselves. A product team that never leaves its office cannot design for questions like these.

Where this topic fits

Deltas sit between the customer and the platform. They belong to Business Development, work in a team that supports one customer, and use the platform plus whatever other tools the problem needs. Devs sit behind them and build platform capabilities for all customers. When the platform lacks something a customer needs, the gap is found by a Delta first.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Palantir split engineering into two roles. Deltas, the forward deployed software engineers, deploy the platforms to a customer. Devs build the platforms.
  2. Deltas work in small teams with minimal supervision and own a project from end to end, often at the customer's site. The posting lists a preferred ability to travel 25 to 50 percent.
  3. Each project starts from the customer's question. The engineer works out what data and analysis could answer it before deciding what to build.
  4. The team deploys and customises the platform, using other languages and open-source tools where needed, and builds what is missing, such as a custom web application.
  5. Success is measured against the customer's goal. Palantir's example is a manufacturer that wants fewer defective products coming off the assembly line.
  6. When the platform gets in the way, FDEs configure new data models, contribute stability improvements or commit fixes to it, according to The Pragmatic Engineer. Palantir's own post adds that larger feature requests are validated against the product team's roadmap.
  7. The balance shifted over time. After the Foundry platform launched in 2016, more FDEs returned to product engineering and brought their field experience with them.

Why this approach fits

The customer's question, data and constraints are only visible on site, so the people who can build have to be there. Splitting the roles lets one group commit fully to a single customer's result while another protects the platform from becoming a pile of one-off features. The Delta's freedom to do Dev work on small gaps keeps the two groups from turning into a queue of tickets.

Limitation or tradeoff

The model is expensive and slow to pay back. The S-1 warns that the sales model often requires months of work and significant resources on pilot deployments at little or no cost to the customer, with no guarantee of later revenue. It also concentrates risk in individuals: Ted Mabrey (opens in a new tab) writes from inside Palantir that building product this way produces as many misses as hits and that the company is constantly under- and over-generalising. Most of what is public about Palantir's practice comes from Palantir or its former staff, so treat the success stories as first-party accounts.

What the sources document

  • Palantir's posting says Forward Deployed Software Engineers work directly with customers, that projects often start with an open-ended question such as why so many flights are being delayed, and that the responsibilities resemble those of a startup CTO, with small teams, minimal supervision and end-to-end execution. [6]
  • Palantir's 2019 blog post, also quoted by The Pragmatic Engineer, says Deltas deploy its platforms to customers, are part of Business Development and measure success by impact on the customer's goal, that a Dev's focus is one capability, many customers while a Delta's is one customer, many capabilities, and that Deltas often contribute code back to the core product, with larger feature requests validated against the product team's roadmap. [5][4]
  • The Pragmatic Engineer reports that until about 2016 Palantir had more FDEs than ordinary software engineers, and that after Foundry launched that year more FDEs went back to working as software engineers on the product. [4]
  • Palantir's S-1 lists, among the factors behind customers' decisions to expand, the ability of its forward-deployed engineers to help customers identify new use cases, and says the company often provides its platforms at no or low cost through short-term pilot deployments. [7]
  • A biography of Shyam Sankar submitted to the House Armed Services Committee says he joined Palantir in 2006 as employee number 13 and envisaged the role of the Forward Deployed Engineer. [8]

Example 2 of 5

OpenAI's FDE team: validate with evals, then take model gaps back to research

OpenAIDocumented in a case study

OpenAI set up a forward deployed engineering team in 2025. Three articles based on interviews with its head, Colin Jarvis, describe how it works: The Pragmatic Engineer's 2025 deep dive, a Business Insider report of a podcast interview (read in AOL's republication), and Engineering Leadership's 2026 interview. [4][16][17]

The problem

Jarvis says that after ChatGPT appeared companies were excited but found it hard to get value from the models. What a customer describes during scoping often does not match the data and systems on the ground, in his words to The Pragmatic Engineer. And a customer that uses a hosted model cannot change the model itself, so when it is not good enough for a use case the fix has to come from the lab that trains it.

Where this topic fits

Engineering Leadership reports that the FDE organisation sits between go-to-market and the product and research teams and acts as the link between them. OpenAI also keeps solutions architects, who advise and build proofs of concept. FDEs take the engagements where they write code on the customer's infrastructure and where the work can inform OpenAI's research direction.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Early scoping: spend a couple of days on site, sit with users, map their processes, identify the biggest areas of value, prototype with synthetic data and prioritise ideas on a whiteboard.
  2. Validation: on site, check that what was scoped is the most valuable thing to do, then agree validation criteria with the customer.
  3. Build evals with user input and labelling, scale up the labelling, build features, and improve the system against the evals. Finish validation with a report that compares eval performance with the objectives.
  4. Delivery: work on the customer's site a few days a week, get the data, build solutions (typically at OpenAI's offices, according to the article) and demo them to the customer, aiming for the smallest unit that gives an end-to-end result.
  5. When the model is the limit, take the evidence to the research team. Engineering Leadership gives an example of the specific kind of feedback the team sends, namely the types of COBOL-to-Java tasks a model gets wrong.
  6. Share what was learned inside OpenAI through regular sessions with research, fortnightly readouts with product leaders and a field-notes channel.

Why this approach fits

The quality of a language-model application can only be established by measuring it on real cases, so someone has to build the measurement with the customer's users. An engineer who is on site can do that. An engineer who also has a route to the research team can do something no consultant could: get the model itself changed. Validating before delivery means the brick walls are found in days, when changing the scope is still cheap.

Limitation or tradeoff

The team is selective about which customers it works with, so an FDE is not available to every customer: The Pragmatic Engineer says FDEs work with customers whose solutions can advance OpenAI's research direction. Jarvis told the podcast that his team avoids services revenue and concentrates on creating product playbooks, and Engineering Leadership reports that OpenAI keeps the FDE organisation deliberately small and has set up a separate deployment company for customers who need help integrating its products. The job also asks a lot of the engineer, with about half of working time spent on customer sites. Every account here originates from OpenAI's own head of FDE, so the outcomes are self-reported.

What the sources document

  • The Pragmatic Engineer reports that OpenAI's solutions architects rarely write code on customer infrastructure and usually build MVPs or proofs of concept with anonymised or offline data, while its FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure using customer tooling. [4]

Example 3 of 5

Ramp: FDEs who follow enterprise customers from sales to support

RampDocumented in an engineering article

Ramp sells finance software. A post on its engineering blog, Ramp Builders, written by the head of its forward deployed engineering team, describes how the team started in 2023, how it operates and what it learned while growing. The Pragmatic Engineer quotes Ramp's job advert for the role. [9][4]

The problem

Ramp wanted to win large enterprise customers, which have legacy systems and long-standing processes that the product has to fit. The post describes a pattern it says Ramp saw across teams and at other companies before it had FDEs. A customer asked for something, an account executive or customer success manager worked out the scope and passed it to product engineering, and engineering scoped a large project that took months, slipped, and saw its requirements change. The post calls the result a lose-lose situation for the customer, the account team, the product managers and the engineers.

Where this topic fits

The FDE team works with a customer through the whole lifecycle: as a prospect in the sales funnel, during implementation and rollout, and in long-tail support. It is an engineering team, and the post says most of its time goes on building features, many of which ship in the core product.

How it is applied, step by step

  1. Start where the pain is. The function took shape in fall 2023, when an engineer stepped in to help support Ramp's very largest customers. The author joined a team of two engineers, and the early days were spent fighting fires.
  2. Set priorities in order: serve existing customers well, then make onboarding and activating new customers more efficient, then expand product capabilities.
  3. Stay with each customer from sales through implementation to support, so that context is kept and the team remains accountable for long-term success and not only for closing or implementing.
  4. Scope with the customer directly. When a customer asks for something, FDEs talk to them and question every requirement before any project is planned.
  5. Look for the workaround first. In one case a customer in onboarding was blocked by a gap estimated at about three days of engineering, and a call with the customer produced a workaround on the spot.
  6. Generalise deliberately. The team asks whether to hack something together for one customer or take longer to build something many customers will use, and it prefers the general feature.
  7. Tie the team's metrics to customer and business outcomes.

Why this approach fits

Each handoff between the customer and the engineer adds delay and distortion, and in enterprise deals the cost of building the wrong thing is measured in months. Putting an engineer in the conversation from the sales stage means the person who knows what is cheap to build hears the need first-hand. Keeping the same team through support means the people who made the promises live with the result.

Limitation or tradeoff

The post itself says the model does not apply to consumer products or to companies that sell only through product-led growth. It also admits that generalising work pulls against shipping the smallest fix, and that deciding between them takes taste and skill, which makes results depend on who is hired. The early period is described as chaotic and very demanding. The account is first-party and gives no before-and-after measurements beyond the three-day example.

4Common mistakes

  1. Treating a forward deployed engineer as a solutions engineer who builds better demos.

    Why it causes problems
    The engagement ends at a proof of concept on sample data. Nothing reaches production, the customer's number does not move, and the contract does not grow. The customer concludes that the product does not work.
    How to recognise it
    Several weeks in, no code is running on the customer's infrastructure, nobody on your side has access to real data, and the last milestone in the plan is a demo.
    How to fix it
    Agree at the start what will be running in production, for which users, and measured by what. Ask for access to the real systems in the first week, and treat a refusal as the first risk to raise.

  2. Building exactly what the customer's request says without questioning it.

    Why it causes problems
    Requests arrive as solutions and are often larger than the problem behind them. Ramp describes the usual result, which is a months-long project that runs late while the requirements change underneath it.
    How to recognise it
    The requirement reached you through an account manager's summary, and nobody who writes code has spoken to the person who has the problem.
    How to fix it
    Talk to that person directly. Ask what they were trying to do when they hit the gap, and look for a workaround before planning a build. Ramp reports removing about three days of engineering this way in one call.

  3. Leaving a different custom build behind at every customer and never generalising.

    Why it causes problems
    Maintenance grows with every customer, margins never improve, and the product team learns nothing. Marty Cagan's warning is that a company doing only this ends up maintaining thousands of bespoke solutions indefinitely.
    How to recognise it
    You are writing the same adapter for the third time, and your team has opened no product issues or pull requests in the last quarter.
    How to fix it
    Count how many customers share each gap, estimate the patch and the general cost, and compute the break-even as in the worked example. Take the cases that clear it to the product team with the evidence.

  4. Generalising too early from one customer's peculiarity.

    Why it causes problems
    Product engineers spend weeks on a capability that one customer uses, and the platform gains an abstraction that does not fit the next customer. The first customer also waits longer than a patch would have taken.
    How to recognise it
    The proposal for a general feature names one customer, has no count of others with the same need, and nobody can say what the per-customer patch would cost.
    How to fix it
    Patch first, write the gap down, and generalise when a second and third customer confirm the pattern and the pipeline count passes the break-even.

  5. Keeping what you learn in the field in your own head.

    Why it causes problems
    The product loop never runs. The next engineer hits the same gap at the next customer, and the product team hears about field problems only through escalations.
    How to recognise it
    You cannot point to a written note, issue or readout from the last month that tells the product or research team something specific.
    How to fix it
    Write field notes on a schedule and make them specific. OpenAI's FDEs report to research in terms such as which kinds of task a model gets wrong, with examples, and not as a general complaint.

  6. Judging the engagement by delivery and not by use.

    Why it causes problems
    A system can pass every test and still be ignored by the people it was built for. At Morgan Stanley the adoption work took about four months after six to eight weeks of technical work, according to OpenAI's head of FDE.
    How to recognise it
    Your status reports list features shipped and accuracy numbers, with no figure for how many intended users used the system last week.
    How to fix it
    Agree an adoption measure with the customer alongside the quality measure, run a pilot with real users, and plan the time to change the system in response to what they say.

Apply the five tests to six role descriptions

The five tests, in order: vendor_product (works for the company whose product is deployed), embedded (works inside one customer's team for weeks or longer), customer_production (their code runs in the customer's production systems), owns_outcome (measured on the customer's result after go-live), changes_product (expected to change the core product).

  • A. Deployed Engineer, AI agent platform. Spends three months with one bank. Writes integration code in the bank's cloud account. Bonus depends on the agent's resolution rate after launch. Expected to send connectors upstream to the platform.
  • B. Solutions Engineer, database vendor. Supports account executives on about twelve open deals. Builds demos and proofs of concept on sample data. Hands the account to another team when the contract is signed.
  • C. Senior Consultant, systems integrator. On site four days a week at a retailer for six months. Builds and deploys a stock system to the retailer's signed specification. The firm is paid per milestone and sells no product.

Decide which of three field gaps to take to the product team

Requirements

  • Compute the break-even number of customers for each gap using the formula from the worked example.
  • Compute the total cost of patching and of generalising at the expected number of customers, and state a decision for each gap.
  • Recompute G1 with half the expected customers and say whether the decision changes.
  • Write a field note for one gap with five parts, namely the customer problem, the evidence, the current workaround, the proposal and the assumption that would change your mind.

Expected behaviour

Your numbers should show break-even points of 6.67, 7.5 and 6 customers. G1 should come out as generalise, saving 8 engineer-days. Hint 1

The break-even is F divided by (c minus g). Patching costs c times n. Generalising costs F plus g times n.

Hint 2

A tie on cost is not a tie on everything. Think about who maintains six patches next year, and how sure you are of the count.

Hint 3

A good field note lets someone who has never met the customer decide. Put numbers in the evidence.

G1 has 12 expected customers against a break-even of 6.67, so the general feature wins clearly. G2 has 3 against 7.5, so patch each of the three and record the gap in case more mainframe customers appear. G3 sits exactly on its break-even. Cost does not decide it, so other things do. Six separate patches all need maintaining, which argues for the feature. An estimate of six customers could easily be four, which argues for patching. A reasonable answer is to patch the customers in hand, and propose the feature when a seventh is confirmed.

Sensitivity for G1. With 6 customers, patching costs 2 × 6 = 12 days and generalising costs 10 + 0.5 × 6 = 13 days, so patching wins by 1 day. The decision depends on the pipeline estimate, so the note has to say where the 12 comes from.

Totals. Following the decisions costs 16 + 18 + 24 = 58 engineer-days. Patching everything costs 24 + 18 + 24 = 66, and generalising everything costs 16 + 36 + 24 = 76. Treating each gap on its own numbers beats either blanket policy.

Field note for G1.

  • Customer problem. Enterprise customers manage access through groups in their identity provider. Our product maps single sign-on users one at a time, so an administrator has to re-enter group membership by hand and repeat it whenever someone moves team.
  • Evidence. Hit at three customers this quarter. At each, an FDE wrote a sync script taking about 2 days. Sales lists 9 more open deals whose security questionnaires ask for group mapping, which gives 12 in the next 12 months.
  • Current workaround. A per-customer script that reads groups from the identity provider nightly and calls our admin API. Each copy is slightly different and each is ours to maintain.
  • Proposal. Build group-to-role mapping into the product, estimated at 10 days, with about half a day of configuration per customer. At 12 customers that is 16 days against 24 for patching.
  • Assumption that would change my mind. If fewer than 7 of the 12 materialise, patching is cheaper. I will recheck the count with sales at the end of next quarter.

The note does the job of the product loop. It turns three separate fixes into one decision that the product team can take with the numbers in front of them.
